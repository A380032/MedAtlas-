3 · Formulas

Let's understand the formulas

Under each formula it is explained what the letters mean, with a simple example. In the example: a 70 kg man drank 200 ml of vodka (40%).

Step 1

Mass of pure ethanol in a drink

A = V × %100 × 0,789
A
pure ethanol, grams
V
drink volume, ml
%
alcohol fraction (by volume)
0,789
ethanol density, g/ml (1 ml of alcohol is not 1 g!)

200 × 0,40 × 0,789 = 63 g of ethanol. This is ≈ 6 standard drinks (WHO: 1 standard drink = 10 g).

Step 2

Widmark formula — maximum concentration

Cmax = Am × r
Cmax
blood concentration, ‰ (g/L)
m
body weight, kg
r
Widmark coefficient — the fraction of body water: man 0.68, woman 0.55

63 / (70 × 0,68) = 63 / 47,6 = 1,32 ‰. For a woman with the same weight and dose: 63 / (70 × 0.55) = 1,64 ‰ — the concentration is higher because there is less water.

Step 3

Concentration over time

C(t) = Cmax − β × t
β
elimination rate ≈ 0.15 ‰/h (0.10–0.20)
t
time elapsed after absorption has finished, hours

After 4 hours: 1.32 − 0.15 × 4 = 0,72 ‰. The graph falls as a straight line — this is zero-order kinetics: a constant amount (≈ 7–10 g) is broken down per hour, not a fraction.

Step 4

Time for the body to clear alcohol

t0 = Cmaxβ
t0
time for the concentration to fall to zero, hours

1,32 / 0,15 ≈ 8.8 hours. A person who drank at 11 PM last night is still not fully sober at 8 AM.

Units

Converting between ‰, g/L, mg% and mmol/L

1 ‰ = 1 g/L = 100 mg% = 21.7 mmol/L
mg%
mg per 100 ml of blood (mg/dL) — this unit is used in the department textbook
21,7
1000 mg/L ÷ 46 (molar mass of ethanol)

In the textbook, "intoxicated — 100–200 mg%" = 1–2 ‰; "acute poisoning — 300–400 mg%" = 3–4 ‰; "death — 500–800 mg%" = 5–8 ‰.

Toxicology

Osmolar gap — detecting methanol and ethylene glycol

Osmcalc = 2Na + glucose + urea + ethanol
Gap = Osmmeasured − Osmcalc
units
all in mmol/L; ethanol (mmol/L) = ‰ × 21.7
normal
gap < 10 mOsm/kg

Na 140, glucose 5, urea 6, ethanol 0: calculated = 291. Measured 325 → gap 34 — there is an "unknown" osmotically active substance in the blood: methanol or ethylene glycol is suspected. To calculate, use the calculator in section 5.

Energy

"Empty" calories

E = A × 7.1 kcal
7,1
energy released by the oxidation of 1 g of ethanol, kcal (department textbook)

63 g × 7.1 = 447 kcal — almost a full meal, but with no vitamins, protein or minerals. This is the reason for thiamine, folic acid and protein deficiency in alcoholics.

Clinical scale

Maddrey discriminant function

DF = 4.6 × (PTpatient − PTcontrol) + bilirubin
PT
prothrombin time, seconds
bilirubin
mg/dL (µmol/L ÷ 17.1)

PT 20 s (control 12 s), bilirubin 8 mg/dL: 4.6 × 8 + 8 = 44,8. DF ≥ 32 — severe alcoholic hepatitis, an indication for prednisolone 40 mg/day for 28 days.

4 · Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics

Ethanol in the body: from absorption to elimination

EthanolCH₃CH₂OH~20% is absorbed in the stomach, the rest in the small intestine; 90–98% is oxidized in the liver, 2–10% is excreted unchanged through the lungs, urine and sweat
ADH
NAD⁺ → NADHinhibited by fomepizole
AcetaldehydeCH₃CHOToxic: facial flushing, nausea, tachycardia; forms adducts with proteins; IARC group 1 carcinogen
ALDH2
NAD⁺ → NADHinhibited by disulfiram and metronidazole
Acetic acidCH₃COOH → acetyl-CoACO₂ + H₂O (7.1 kcal/g) or fatty acid synthesis
Absorption

Fast on an empty stomach (peak within 30–60 minutes); food — especially fats and carbohydrates — slows absorption and lowers the peak. Carbonated drinks speed up gastric emptying.

Distribution

Soluble in water and distributed evenly through all body water (hence the Widmark r is the water fraction). Crosses the blood-brain barrier and the placenta freely and passes into breast milk.

Zero-order kinetics

ADH becomes saturated at low concentrations: ≈ 7–10 g of ethanol or ≈ 0.15 ‰ is broken down per hour. "Sobering" remedies do not speed it up.

MEOS (CYP2E1)

Induced by chronic drinking: tolerance develops and more NAPQI is formed from paracetamol. Acute drinking, on the contrary, can inhibit the metabolism of other drugs.

Increase in NADH/NAD⁺

Gluconeogenesis is inhibited (hypoglycemia), lactate accumulates, fatty acid oxidation decreases — fatty liver (hepatic steatosis), hyperuricemia.

Mechanism of action (CNS)

GABAA activity is enhanced, NMDA-glutamate receptors are blocked and mesolimbic dopamine release increases. As the dose rises, the effect "descends" from the cortex to the medulla oblongata.

By the textbook

Department textbook and Kharkevich: ethyl alcohol

Below are the main points from the Department of Pharmacology's teaching material and D.A. Kharkevich's textbook. Green blocks show differences from modern recommendations.

General description

  • A colorless, highly flammable, light liquid with a characteristic smell.
  • In medicine it is used mainly as an antiseptic and a solvent; because it is widely consumed in beverages, its resorptive action must be known.
  • It has a general depressant (narcotic-type) effect on the CNS.

Local action — depends on concentration

  • 40% — dilates skin vessels and causes hyperemia (irritant, warming compress).
  • 70% — the most effective for skin antisepsis: it penetrates the deep layers of the epidermis.
  • 95–96% — rapidly coagulates proteins, giving an astringent effect; disinfection of cutting instruments.

Modern note. For hand antisepsis the WHO recommends 60–80% alcohol-based products; 96% alcohol penetrates poorly and is less effective.

Stages of CNS action

  • A small dose weakens inhibition in the cortex — "false excitation": critical thinking decreases, emotion increases.
  • First analgesia, then a long period of excitation, then the "surgical" stage — consciousness is lost.
  • The narcotic margin is very narrow: the surgical stage quickly passes into the agonal stage (paralysis of the respiratory and circulatory centers). For this reason ethanol is not used for anesthesia.

Other resorptive effects

  • Skin vessels dilate — heat loss increases and body temperature falls (risk of rapid freezing in the cold).
  • Suppresses the secretion of antidiuretic hormone — diuresis increases.
  • Stomach: concentrations up to 20% increase secretion; higher concentrations reduce HCl and pepsin production and cause pylorospasm.
  • 1 g of ethanol provides 7.1 kcal, but the energy is not stored; alcohol cannot be a nutrient or plastic material.

Blood levels (according to the textbook)

  • Intoxication: 100–200 mg% (1–2 ‰).
  • Acute poisoning: 300–400 mg% (3–4 ‰).
  • Death: 500–800 mg% (5–8 ‰).
  • Treatment: gastric lavage, oxygen, in severe cases hemodialysis and symptomatic therapy.

Modern note. The analeptics mentioned in the textbook are no longer recommended. The main measures are to secure the airway, place the patient on the side, thiamine, then glucose, infusion and warming. Gastric lavage only within 1 hour of drinking and if the airway is protected.

Alcoholism — chronic poisoning

  • Mental work, memory and thinking decline; Korsakoff psychosis, alcoholic delirium (delirium tremens).
  • Polyneuritis, chronic gastritis, hepatitis, cirrhosis, fatty degeneration of the heart and kidneys.
  • Functional cumulation: even when ethanol is not detected in the blood, changes in the CNS keep accumulating.

Treatment of alcoholism (according to the textbook)

  • Teturam (disulfiram, Antabuse) — inhibits ALDH; when given with a small amount of alcohol, acetaldehyde accumulates and causes vomiting and an unpleasant state — a conditioned-reflex aversion to alcohol develops.
  • Esperal — a long-acting form of disulfiram implanted under the skin.
  • Apomorphine (a central emetic) — together with alcohol.
  • Naltrexone — an opioid receptor antagonist.

Modern note. Disulfiram only with the patient's consent and under supervision; caution above age 50 and in heart, liver and kidney disease. "Emetic therapy" with apomorphine is no longer used. First line: naltrexone and acamprosate + psychotherapy (section 7).

Use in medicine

  • Antiseptic: hands, injection site, surgical field (70%).
  • Solvent and preservative: tinctures (in 40% or 70% alcohol), extracts.
  • Warming compress (40%).
  • Its vapor to break down foam in pulmonary edema (rarely used now; antifomsilane is preferred).
  • Antidote in methanol and ethylene glycol poisoning (when fomepizole is not available).

5 · Toxicology

Methanol, ethylene glycol and other "surrogates"

They are also broken down by ADH, but their metabolites are extremely toxic. Ethanol and fomepizole occupy ADH and stop the formation of the toxic metabolite — hence they are antidotes.

6 · Withdrawal syndrome

The hours after drinking stops

With chronic ethanol exposure, GABAA receptor sensitivity decreases and NMDA receptors increase. If ethanol is stopped abruptly, the "brake" is lost and the "accelerator" remains — excitation of the CNS and the sympathetic system.

Benzodiazepines — first choice

Diazepam or chlordiazepoxide titrated to symptoms (using the CIWA-Ar scale). In liver failure — lorazepam or oxazepam (no active metabolites).

Thiamine for everyone

Prophylactic 100–300 mg; if Wernicke is suspected, 500 mg intravenously three times a day — before glucose.

What is not done

Giving neuroleptics alone (they lower the seizure threshold), leaving the patient tied up, giving alcohol "as treatment".
