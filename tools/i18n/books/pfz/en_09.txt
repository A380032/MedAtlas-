s1614 In the frog, fat injected into the abdominal vein also enters the portal system and the liver; in humans, fat entering through a vein is first trapped in the pulmonary capillaries. s1615 1. Ischemia

s1652 Factors determining the outcome of ischemia
s1653 The outcome of ischemia can range from complete recovery to **infarction** (ischemic necrosis of the tissue). The outcome depends on the following:
s1654 **The development of collateral circulation** — the most important factor.
s1655 **The rate of development of ischemia** — with slow narrowing the collaterals have time to develop; with sudden occlusion they do not.
s1656 **The duration of ischemia.**
s1657 **The sensitivity of the tissue to hypoxia** and the intensity of its metabolism.
s1658 **The state of the general circulation** (heart failure and hypotension aggravate the outcome).

s1675 Cell injury in ischemia and reperfusion injury
s1676 The main mechanisms of cell injury in ischemia: cessation of oxidative phosphorylation → ATP deficiency → failure of the Na+/K+ pump → entry of sodium and water into the cell (cellular swelling) → anaerobic glycolysis and lactic acidosis → accumulation of Ca2+ inside the cell → activation of phospholipases and proteases → membrane injury → necrosis.
s1677 **Reperfusion injury** is a paradoxical increase in injury when blood flow is restored after ischemia. Its mechanisms: formation of large amounts of reactive oxygen species (free radicals), massive entry of Ca2+ into cells, activation of leukocytes and plugging of microvessels (the no-reflow phenomenon), reperfusion arrhythmias.
s1678 In myocardial infarction, “time is myocardium”: the faster blood flow in the coronary artery is restored (thrombolysis or coronary angioplasty), the smaller the volume of necrosis. The same applies to ischemic stroke — thrombolysis is effective only in the first hours after the onset of symptoms (the “therapeutic window”). Along with reperfusion, measures are also taken to reduce its injurious effect.
s1679 2. Stasis
s1680 **Stasis** is the slowing and cessation of blood flow in the vessels of the microcirculation (mainly in the capillaries).
s1681 Table 5.4. Types of stasis
s1682 Ischemic
s1683 Sharp decrease in the inflow of blood from the arteries
s1684 Arterial occlusion, spasm
s1685 Venous (congestive)
s1686 Impeded outflow of blood through the veins
s1687 The severe stage of venous hyperemia
s1688 True (capillary)
s1689 Changes in the blood itself and in the capillary wall: aggregation of red blood cells, hemoconcentration
s1690 Inflammation, burns, frostbite, poisoning, shock
s1691 The main mechanism of true stasis is **aggregation of red blood cells (“sludge”)**: red blood cells stick together like “stacks of coins” and plug the capillaries. This is caused by a decrease in the negative surface charge of red blood cells, an increase in high-molecular-weight plasma proteins (fibrinogen, globulins), hemoconcentration (water loss) and increased permeability of the capillary wall.
s1692 If stasis is brief, it is a reversible process; if prolonged, it leads to tissue necrosis and thrombus formation.
s1693 3. Thrombosis
s1694 **Thrombosis** is the clotting of blood in a vessel or heart cavity in the living organism with the formation of a clot called a thrombus. Unlike a blood clot, a **thrombus** (Greek thrombos — clot) is attached to the vessel wall and forms under conditions of blood flow.

s1709 Stages of thrombus formation
s1710 **Platelet adhesion** — at the site of injury, platelets adhere to exposed collagen via von Willebrand factor.
s1711 **Platelet activation and aggregation** — platelets change shape and release ADP, thromboxane A2 and serotonin; they stick to one another via fibrinogen bridges (the white head of the thrombus).
s1712 **Activation of the coagulation cascade** — thrombin is generated via tissue factor (the extrinsic pathway) and factor XII (the intrinsic pathway).
s1713 **Conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin** — a fibrin network forms, in which red blood cells and leukocytes become trapped.
s1714 **Clot retraction** — the thrombus becomes denser owing to the contractile proteins of platelets.

s1732 Outcomes of a thrombus
s1733 **Aseptic autolysis** (dissolution) — the thrombus dissolves under the action of the fibrinolytic system (plasmin), and blood flow is restored.
s1734 **Organization** — connective tissue grows into the thrombus.
s1735 **Canalization and vascularization** — new channels form within the organized thrombus, and blood flow is partly restored.
s1736 **Petrification** — calcium salts are deposited in the thrombus (“phleboliths” in veins).
s1737 **Septic (purulent) dissolution** — the thrombus becomes infected; risk of septic embolism and sepsis.
s1738 **Detachment of the thrombus** — thromboembolism.
s1739 Thrombus formation is a pathological manifestation of a protective mechanism (hemostasis). Arterial thrombosis leads to ischemia and infarction (myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke), whereas venous thrombosis leads to venous congestion and thromboembolism. Prevention: antiplatelet agents (acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) — in arterial thrombosis), anticoagulants (heparin, warfarin — in venous thrombosis and atrial fibrillation).

s1785 Pulmonary embolism (PE) is one of the most dangerous types of embolism. The main source of emboli (about 90%) is deep vein thrombosis of the legs and the small pelvis.
s1786 Pathogenesis: the embolus occludes the pulmonary artery → pulmonary vascular resistance increases and the right ventricle is overloaded (acute cor pulmonale) → the return of blood to the left heart decreases, and cardiac output and arterial pressure fall → the ventilation-perfusion relationship is disturbed and hypoxemia develops. In addition, serotonin and thromboxane released from platelets cause reflex spasm of the bronchi and pulmonary vessels and spasm of the coronary vessels (the pulmonocoronary reflex).
s1787 **Fat embolism** usually develops 24–72 hours after a fracture of a long bone (the femur): shortness of breath and hypoxemia (occlusion of the pulmonary capillaries), neurological disorders (brain), and a petechial rash on the skin of the chest and axillae.
s1788 In **air embolism**, entry of 10–20 mL of air into the blood is usually harmless, but a large volume entering rapidly (more than 100 mL) may form an “air lock” in the right ventricle and lead to cardiac arrest.
s1789 5. Experimental part
s1790 Experiment 1: ischemia in the frog tongue and the rabbit ear
s1791 Fix the anesthetized frog on a cork board with its belly down, pull out the tongue, spread it over the opening and observe the vessels under the microscope.
s1792 Apply 1–2 drops of epinephrine solution (1:1000) to the tongue.
s1793 Observe the constriction of the arterioles, the decrease in the number of working capillaries, the slowing of blood flow and the blanching of the tongue.
s1794 In the rabbit ear: press the ear artery with a finger or inject a small amount of epinephrine under the skin — the ear blanches, the vascular network becomes invisible and the temperature falls.
s1795 After the pressure is removed or the action of epinephrine ends, observe the development of postischemic hyperemia.
s1796 **Analysis:** epinephrine constricts the arterioles via alpha-adrenergic receptors — **angiospastic ischemia** develops. The reddening after ischemia has been eliminated is postischemic arterial hyperemia caused by metabolites.
s1797 Experiment 2: observing thrombus formation
s1798 Place the frog mesentery under the microscope and find a larger vessel (a venule or arteriole).
s1799 Place a crystal of table salt (sodium chloride) on the vessel wall.
s1800 Observe the injury of the vessel wall at the site of the crystal, the adhesion and accumulation of platelets (in the frog — cells that perform the function of platelets) on the wall, and then the growth of the thrombus and the narrowing of the blood flow.
s1801 Record the time of thrombus formation and the degree to which it has occluded the vascular lumen.
s1802 **Analysis:** the hypertonic solution injures the endothelium (the first factor of Virchow's triad), as a result of which platelet adhesion and aggregation begin.
