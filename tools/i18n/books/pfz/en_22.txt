s4413 Explain the mechanism of hepatocyte injury. What does the rise in ALT and AST indicate? Why is acetylcysteine an antidote?
s4414 A large dose of paracetamol forms a toxic metabolite in the liver via cytochrome P450 — N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine. Normally it is neutralized by glutathione; at a large dose the glutathione stores are exhausted and the metabolite binds covalently to proteins, enhances oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation and damages the mitochondria — hepatocyte necrosis develops. The sharp rise in ALT and AST indicates damage to the hepatocyte membrane and release of enzymes from these cells into the blood — extensive necrosis; prolonged prothrombin time shows that the synthetic function of the liver is impaired. Acetylcysteine supplies cysteine for glutathione synthesis and restores glutathione stores, i.e. it restores the capacity to neutralize the toxic metabolite; it is most effective in the first 8–10 hours.
s4415 A patient who had been in a closed room during a fire was brought in unconscious. The skin and mucous membranes are pinkish-red and breathing is rapid. Blood carboxyhemoglobin is 40 %.
s4416 What is the main mechanism of cell injury? Which tissues are damaged first, and why?
s4417 Carbon monoxide binds to hemoglobin about 200–250 times more strongly than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin; the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood decreases and the remaining oxyhemoglobin gives up oxygen less readily (the dissociation curve shifts to the left). In addition, CO partly blocks cytochrome oxidase. The result is tissue hypoxia → ATP deficiency → ion pumps stop, cell swelling, calcium accumulation and necrosis. The tissues with the highest oxygen demand and the greatest sensitivity to hypoxia — the brain and myocardium — are damaged first. The pinkish-red color is due to the color of carboxyhemoglobin and masks the severity of the hypoxia.
s4418 A patient with cancer received a course of radiotherapy. After treatment the tumor decreased in size, but the patient developed leukopenia and diarrhea.
s4419 How does ionizing radiation kill tumor cells? Why are the complications related specifically to the bone marrow and intestine?
s4420 Ionizing radiation damages DNA directly and through free radicals formed by radiolysis of water (breaks in DNA strands). Damaged DNA triggers apoptosis via the p53 protein, or the cell dies during division (mitotic death). Tumor cells divide rapidly and are therefore sensitive to radiation. According to the Bergonié–Tribondeau law, the rapidly renewing tissues of the body — the bone marrow and intestinal epithelium — are also sensitive to radiation: death of bone marrow cells leads to leukopenia, and damage to the epithelium of the intestinal crypts leads to impaired absorption and diarrhea.

s4459 1. The concept of a tumor
s4460 **Tumor (neoplasia, blastoma)** — a typical pathological process characterized by unlimited proliferation of cells that escapes the body's control and by impaired differentiation, resulting from changes in their genetic apparatus. These properties of tumor cells are passed on to subsequent cell generations.

s4535 Theories of carcinogenesis
s4536 **Mutation theory** — a tumor is the result of the accumulation of several successive mutations (in colon cancer: APC → KRAS → TP53 mutations, the adenoma–carcinoma sequence).
s4537 **Viral-genetic theory** (L.A. Zilber) — oncogenic viruses insert their genome into the cell DNA and transform it.

s4539 **Cancer stem cell theory** — a tumor develops from a small group of self-renewing cells, which are resistant to treatment.
