s0813 In July, at an air temperature of +42 °C, a 35-year-old construction worker did heavy physical work outdoors all day and drank little water. In the evening he complained of severe headache and nausea, and then lost consciousness. On examination: the skin is dry and hot, there is no sweating, body temperature is 41.5 °C, pulse 140 beats/min, arterial pressure 85/50 mm Hg, convulsions.
s0814 What condition has developed in the patient? Explain its pathogenesis. Why are antipyretics ineffective in this condition?
s0815 The patient has developed the decompensation stage of hyperthermia — heatstroke. High external temperature and heavy physical work increased heat production, while heat loss was insufficient. Profuse sweating led to dehydration, loss of sodium and chloride, hemoconcentration and a decrease in circulating blood volume; together with dilatation of the skin vessels, this caused arterial hypotension (collapse). Hypoxia and edema of the brain injured the thermoregulatory center, sweating stopped and the temperature rose even further (a vicious circle). Antipyretics lower the set point of the thermoregulatory center that has been changed by pyrogens; in hyperthermia, however, the set point is unchanged, and the problem is insufficient heat loss. Therefore physical cooling of the body (a cool place, wet wrapping, cold compresses) and restoration of the water-electrolyte balance are necessary.
s0816 A 28-year-old mountaineer ascended from sea level to an altitude of 4500 m in one day. The next day he complained of headache, weakness, shortness of breath and insomnia. Later a cough with pink frothy sputum appeared.
s0817 What condition has developed? What is the main link of pathogenesis? How can this condition be prevented?
s0818 Mountain sickness has developed, complicated by pulmonary edema. The main link is hypoxic hypoxia and hypoxemia resulting from the decreased partial pressure of oxygen in the air. Hypoxemia causes hyperventilation and respiratory alkalosis, hypoxic spasm of the pulmonary arterioles and increased pressure in the pulmonary artery; as a result, capillary permeability increases and pulmonary edema develops. Prevention: stepwise ascent and time for acclimatization; when severe signs appear, immediate descent and oxygen therapy.
s0819 In an experiment, students “raised” three mice in a pressure chamber. Convulsions appeared at 9000 m in the control mouse, at 7000 m in the mouse heated at +40 °C, and at 11,000 m in the cooled mouse whose body temperature had fallen to 30 °C.

s0858 **Reactivity** is “how does it respond?”, **resistance** is “how much can it withstand?”. A decrease in reactivity does not always mean a decrease in resistance: in anesthesia and hypothermia, reactivity decreases while resistance to hypoxia increases. This is proved in the experimental part of this session.
s0859 The blood-brain barrier protects the brain, but many drugs (for example, some antibiotics) cross it poorly. In the treatment of meningitis, drugs that cross the barrier well are chosen. In inflamed meninges the permeability of the barrier increases.
s0860 **“Thin — stomach, stout — vessels”**: people of the asthenic type are more prone to peptic ulcer and tuberculosis, whereas people of the hypersthenic type are prone to hypertension, atherosclerosis and diabetes.
s0861 **“Congenital”** and **“hereditary”** are not the same concept. A congenital disease is present at birth, but it may not be hereditary (for example, defects caused by the rubella virus or alcohol during pregnancy). A hereditary disease, in contrast, may appear long after birth (Huntington chorea — at the age of 30–50). Conditions that develop under the influence of external factors and resemble a hereditary disease but are not passed from generation to generation are called **phenocopies**.
s0862 Although anesthesia increases resistance to hypoxia, narcotic substances in large doses depress the respiratory center and can themselves lead to hypoxia (respiratory hypoxia).
