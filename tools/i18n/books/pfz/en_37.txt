s7376 **Normally** total blood bilirubin is 8.5–20.5 µmol/l, mainly the indirect fraction (about 75 percent).
s7377 **Jaundice (icterus)** — yellow coloring of the skin, mucous membranes and sclerae due to increased blood bilirubin (hyperbilirubinemia). Yellowing of the sclerae becomes visible when bilirubin exceeds about 34 µmol/l.

s7379 Types of jaundice and their laboratory signs
s7380 Prehepatic (hemolytic) jaundice
s7381 **Cause:** increased breakdown of erythrocytes (hemolytic anemias, incompatible blood transfusion, hemolytic poisons, malaria, hemolytic disease of the newborn) — formation of indirect bilirubin exceeds the conjugating capacity of the liver.
s7382 **Signs:** indirect bilirubin raised in the blood; no bilirubin in the urine (indirect bilirubin is not filtered); a lot of bilirubin enters the intestine — **urobilin** in the urine and **stercobilin** in the stool are increased (dark stool); the skin is lemon-yellow, no itching; anemia, reticulocytosis.
s7383 Hepatic (parenchymal) jaundice
s7384 **Cause:** damage to hepatocytes — viral and toxic hepatitis, cirrhosis, leptospirosis.
s7385 **Mechanism:** uptake, conjugation and excretion of bilirubin into the bile capillaries are impaired in the liver cells; the cells die and conjugated bilirubin returns to the blood (the barrier between the bile capillaries and blood vessels is broken); the liver cannot retain urobilinogen coming from the intestine.
s7386 **Signs:** **both fractions** raised in the blood; direct bilirubin present in the urine (urine “the color of beer”) and urobilin increased; stool decolorized (less stercobilin); ALT and AST sharply raised, synthetic function of the liver impaired; the skin is saffron-yellow.
s7387 **By stage of hepatitis:** urobilinuria in the initial (prodromal) period; at the peak, if cholestasis develops, urobilin may temporarily disappear; it reappears during recovery.
s7388 Posthepatic (obstructive, mechanical) jaundice
s7389 **Cause:** obstruction of bile outflow — bile duct stones, cancer of the head of the pancreas, tumor of the papilla of Vater, cicatricial narrowing of the bile ducts, helminths, external compression.
s7390 **Mechanism:** pressure in the bile ducts rises, the bile capillaries rupture and conjugated bilirubin, bile acids and cholesterol pass into the blood (**cholemia**); bile does not enter the intestine (**acholia**).
s7391 **Signs:** **direct bilirubin** sharply raised in the blood; much bilirubin in the urine (dark urine, “the color of beer”), **no urobilin** (since bile does not enter the intestine, no urobilinogen is formed); stool **white-gray (acholic)**, fatty (steatorrhea); alkaline phosphatase, GGT and cholesterol raised; the skin is greenish-yellow, with severe itching.

s7409 6. Cholestasis, cholemia and acholia syndromes
s7410 **Cholemia** — entry of bile components (mainly bile acids) into the blood:
s7411 skin itching (action of bile acids and other pruritogens on nerve endings);
s7412 bradycardia and arterial hypotension (action of bile acids on the sinus node and the vagal nuclei, reduced vascular tone);
s7413 depression of the CNS — weakness, depression, drowsiness;
s7414 hemolysis of erythrocytes, reduced leukocyte activity;
s7415 hypercholesterolemia, xanthomas on the skin.
s7416 **Acholia** — bile does not enter the intestine:
s7417 emulsification and digestion of fats are impaired — **steatorrhea**;
s7418 fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are not absorbed — **vitamin K deficiency** (reduced synthesis of factors II, VII, IX, X, bleeding), osteomalacia, night blindness;
s7419 intestinal peristalsis and the bacteriostatic action of bile decrease — putrefaction and fermentation in the intestine, flatulence, dysbiosis;
s7420 the stool is decolorized.
s7421 7. Features of hepatobiliary disorders in children
s7422 **Physiological jaundice of the newborn** — appears on day 2–3 and disappears by day 7–10 (by 2–3 weeks in premature infants). Causes:
s7423 the short life of erythrocytes containing fetal hemoglobin (HbF) and their massive breakdown after birth;
s7424 low activity of hepatic **UDP-glucuronyltransferase** (immaturity of the enzyme);
s7425 absence of bacteria in the intestine and intense enterohepatic circulation.
s7426 Pathological jaundice:
s7427 **Hemolytic disease of the newborn** — maternal antibodies against the fetus's Rh-positive (or AB0) antigens destroy fetal erythrocytes; it appears on the first day and increases rapidly.
s7428 **Bilirubin encephalopathy (kernicterus)** — in newborns the permeability of the blood–brain barrier is high and albumin is low; indirect bilirubin (which is fat-soluble) accumulates in the subcortical nuclei of the brain and damages them. The risk is high when bilirubin exceeds about 340 µmol/l (even lower in premature infants). Treatment — phototherapy, exchange transfusion.
s7429 **Hereditary pigmentary hepatoses: Gilbert syndrome** (mild impairment of bilirubin uptake and conjugation — indirect bilirubin rises with fasting and stress, harmless), **Crigler–Najjar syndrome** (glucuronyltransferase absent or markedly reduced — severe indirect hyperbilirubinemia, risk of kernicterus), **Dubin–Johnson syndrome** (impaired excretion of direct bilirubin).
s7430 **Biliary atresia** — congenital obstructive jaundice; early surgery is required.
s7431 8. Experimental part: detecting bile pigments in urine
s7432 Detecting bilirubin in urine (Rosin test)
s7433 Pour 4–5 ml of urine into a test tube.
s7434 Carefully layer 1 percent alcoholic iodine solution along the wall of the test tube.
s7435 If a **green ring** appears at the boundary of the two liquids, bilirubin is present in the urine (positive test).
s7436 Detecting urobilin in urine
s7437 A reagent for urobilinogen (e.g. Ehrlich's reagent) is added to the urine: the appearance of a red color indicates increased urobilinogen (urobilin); normally the color is very faint or appears only on heating.

s7543 A 62-year-old man has had gradually increasing jaundice for 2 months, severe skin itching, loss of appetite and weight loss. The skin is greenish-yellow with scratch marks; pulse 56 beats/min. Blood: total bilirubin 280 µmol/l (direct 230), alkaline phosphatase and GGT sharply raised, ALT slightly raised, prothrombin index decreased. Urine dark, bilirubin positive, urobilin negative. Stool white-gray and fatty. CT: a tumor in the head of the pancreas.
s7544 Determine the type of jaundice and explain the laboratory signs. What is the mechanism of the itching, bradycardia and decreased prothrombin index?
s7545 Posthepatic (obstructive) jaundice — a tumor of the head of the pancreas has compressed the common bile duct. Pressure in the bile ducts rises and conjugated bilirubin and bile acids pass into the blood (cholemia): direct bilirubin predominates and the cholestatic enzymes (alkaline phosphatase, GGT) are sharply raised; direct bilirubin is water-soluble and is excreted by the kidneys — dark urine, bilirubin positive. Bile does not enter the intestine (acholia) — no urobilinogen is formed (no urobilin in the urine), and the stool is acholic and fatty (steatorrhea). Itching and bradycardia — the action of bile acids on the nerve endings of the skin, the sinus node and the vagal centers. Vitamin K is not absorbed without bile acids — synthesis of factors II, VII, IX and X decreases and the prothrombin index falls (vitamin K is given parenterally before surgery).
s7546 A 25-year-old student developed jaundice a week after fever and weakness. Urine dark, stool pale. The liver is enlarged and tender. Blood: bilirubin 140 µmol/l (direct 80, indirect 60), ALT 1800 U/l (normal up to 40), AST 1200 U/l. Urine: bilirubin and urobilin positive. HBsAg positive.
s7547 Determine the type of jaundice. Why are both fractions raised in the blood? Explain the mechanism of urobilinuria.
s7548 Hepatic (parenchymal) jaundice — acute viral hepatitis B. Hepatocytes are damaged (cytolysis — ALT and AST sharply raised): uptake and conjugation of indirect bilirubin are impaired — the indirect fraction rises; excretion of conjugated bilirubin into the bile capillaries is impaired and direct bilirubin returns to the blood through the dead cells — the direct fraction also rises and is found in the urine. Part of the bile still enters the intestine, urobilinogen is formed and absorbed, but the damaged liver cannot capture and reprocess it — urobilinogen passes into the systemic circulation and is excreted in the urine (urobilinuria). Because less bile enters the intestine, the stool is pale.
s7549 A 55-year-old man has drunk alcohol for 20 years and has been diagnosed with liver cirrhosis. In recent days he has had black (tarry) stools, followed by drowsiness, confusion, a “flapping” tremor when he stretches out his hands and a characteristic sweetish-musty odor from the mouth. The abdomen is enlarged (ascites), with dilated veins around the navel.
s7550 What complication has developed? Explain the precipitating factor and the pathogenesis. How did the ascites and the periumbilical veins arise?
s7551 Hepatic encephalopathy (precoma) — mixed (hepatocellular failure and portosystemic shunting). The precipitating factor is bleeding from esophageal varices (black stools): bacteria form large amounts of ammonia from blood proteins in the intestine; the cirrhotic liver cannot convert it into urea, and in addition blood bypasses the liver through portocaval anastomoses. In the brain, ammonia binds alpha-ketoglutarate and reduces energy production, and glutamine accumulates in astrocytes and causes swelling; false neurotransmitters (octopamine) and mercaptans (“fetor hepaticus”) also accumulate — impaired consciousness, asterixis. Ascites is the result of portal hypertension (increased hydrostatic pressure), hypoalbuminemia (decreased oncotic pressure), secondary hyperaldosteronism and sodium and water retention. The periumbilical veins (“caput medusae”) are dilated portocaval anastomoses.
