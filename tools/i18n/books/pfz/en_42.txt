s8278 General pathogenesis of coma:
s8279 **disturbed energy supply of neurons** — hypoxia, hypoglycemia (the brain depends mainly on glucose for energy), mitochondrial damage;
s8280 **direct action of toxins** — ammonia, uremic toxins, ketone bodies, alcohol, drugs;
s8281 **water–electrolyte and acid–base disturbances** — osmotic imbalance between the intracellular and extracellular compartments (cerebral edema or dehydration of brain cells), acidosis;
s8282 **cerebral edema and raised intracranial pressure** — reduced cerebral blood flow, compression of the brainstem (herniation);
s8283 **disturbed neurotransmitter metabolism** (false neurotransmitters, increased GABA-ergic inhibition);
s8284 as a result, the activity of cortical and subcortical structures is inhibited and the activating influence of the reticular formation is lost — consciousness is lost; if the process deepens, the respiratory and vasomotor centers in the medulla oblongata are damaged.
s8285 **Degrees of impaired consciousness:** clear consciousness → obtundation (somnolence, pre-sopor) → **sopor** (protective movements only in response to strong painful stimuli) → **coma** (I — moderate, II — deep, III — terminal, transcendental coma).

s8296 5. Crush syndrome (prolonged compression syndrome)
s8297 **Crush syndrome** — a severe condition that develops after soft tissues (mainly limb muscles) have been compressed for a long time (more than 4–6 hours) and then released (earthquakes, car accidents, building collapses).
s8298 Main links of the pathogenesis:
s8299 **Pain factor** — prolonged pain impulses during compression, elements of traumatic shock.
s8300 **Plasma loss** — after release, blood flows into the damaged muscles and, because capillary permeability is increased, plasma leaks into the tissue — the limb swells sharply, with **hypovolemia** and hemoconcentration.
s8301 **Toxemia** — **myoglobin**, **potassium**, phosphates, creatine, lactate and other toxins are released from the ischemic muscles into the blood (**rhabdomyolysis**); **hyperkalemia** can cause cardiac arrest.
s8302 **Acute kidney failure** — myoglobin precipitates in the tubules in an acidic environment, obstructs them and damages the epithelium; hypovolemia aggravates renal ischemia (topic 25).
s8303 **Periods:** early (days 1–3, shock and hemodynamic disturbances predominate), intermediate (days 3–12, acute kidney failure), late (recovery, local complications — infection, muscle atrophy, contractures).
s8304 Care: start intravenous fluids **before** releasing the compressed limb; at the moment of release, bandage the limb and cool it (to reduce the rapid absorption of toxins), give analgesia and alkaline solutions (alkalinizing the urine reduces myoglobin precipitation), monitor for hyperkalemia, and use hemodialysis if needed.

s8328 Resuscitation and post-resuscitation disease
s8329 **Resuscitation** — restoration of vital functions: opening the airway, artificial ventilation, closed-chest cardiac massage, defibrillation (in ventricular fibrillation), drugs (epinephrine).
s8330 **Post-resuscitation disease** (V. A. Negovsky) — a specific pathological state after successful recovery from clinical death:
s8331 **reperfusion injury** — when blood flow is restored, large amounts of reactive oxygen species are formed and damage membranes through lipid peroxidation; massive entry of Ca2+ into the cells;
s8332 cerebral edema and posthypoxic encephalopathy, delayed death of some neurons (apoptosis);
s8333 cardiac dysfunction, arrhythmias;
s8334 multiple organ failure (lungs, kidneys, liver);
s8335 translocation of endotoxins from the intestine, DIC, immunodepression.
s8336 Skill: calculating the shock index and assessing the severity of shock
s8337 Count the patient's pulse rate for 1 minute.
s8338 Measure the systolic arterial pressure.
s8339 Shock index = pulse / systolic BP.
s8340 Assess the result: 0.5–0.7 — normal; about 1.0 — moderately severe; 1.5 or more — severe shock.
s8341 Assess additional signs: skin color and temperature, level of consciousness, hourly diuresis (below 0.5 ml/kg/h — renal hypoperfusion), capillary refill time (more than 2 seconds).
s8342 Skill: assessing the level of consciousness using the Glasgow Coma Scale
s8343 Assess the eye-opening response (1–4 points).
s8344 Assess the verbal response (1–5 points).
s8345 Assess the motor response (1–6 points).
s8346 Add up the points (3–15).
s8347 Determine the degree of impaired consciousness: 15 — clear, 13–14 — obtundation, 9–12 — sopor, 8 or less — coma.

s8429 A 30-year-old man suffered second- and third-degree burns over 40 percent of his body surface at work. In the first minutes he was agitated and shouting, BP 140/90. 2 hours later he is apathetic and answers questions slowly, BP 85/50, pulse 128 beats/min, pale skin, cold sweat, hourly diuresis 15 ml. Blood: hematocrit 0.58, hemoglobin 190 g/l, K+ raised.
s8430 What condition has developed? Determine its stages. Explain the mechanism of the hemoconcentration, oliguria and hyperkalemia. Calculate the shock index.
s8431 Burn shock: first the erectile stage (pain impulses, sympathoadrenal excitation — restlessness, raised BP), then the torpid stage (CNS inhibition, hypotension, thready pulse). In the burned area capillary permeability increases sharply — plasma leaks into the tissue and out through the burn surface — hypovolemia and hemoconcentration (raised hematocrit and hemoglobin). Under the action of hypovolemia and catecholamines the renal vessels constrict and filtration decreases — oliguria (risk of shock kidney). Hyperkalemia — release of potassium from the breakdown of burned tissues and erythrocytes and reduced renal excretion. Shock index = 128 / 85 ≈ 1.5 — severe shock. Treatment — rapid infusion therapy of adequate volume, analgesia.
s8432 A 68-year-old woman has had a fever for 3 days (urinary tract infection). Her condition suddenly worsened: temperature 39.5 °C, BP 80/40, pulse 125 beats/min, respiratory rate 28, the skin at first warm and flushed, later cold and mottled. Scanty urine. Blood: leukocytes 22 × 10^9^/l, lactate raised, platelets decreased, creatinine raised. BP did not rise even after infusion.
s8433 What condition is this? Explain the pathogenesis. What are the “warm” and “cold” phases related to? What is the cause of the thrombocytopenia and the rise in lactate?
s8434 Septic shock (urosepsis). Endotoxin of gram-negative bacteria activates macrophages and endothelium — inflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6) are released; they activate inducible NO synthase — excess NO causes marked vasodilation (distributive shock, no response to infusion). The initial “warm” (hyperdynamic) phase — the peripheral vessels are dilated and cardiac output is compensatorily increased; then, because of myocardial depression and plasma leakage from the capillaries, it passes into the “cold” (hypodynamic) phase — peripheral vasoconstriction, mottled skin. Endothelial damage and activation of the clotting system — DIC (platelets are consumed). Tissue hypoperfusion and mitochondrial dysfunction — anaerobic glycolysis, lactic acidosis. Oliguria and raised creatinine — shock kidney; tachypnea — a response to acidosis and early shock lung: multiple organ failure is developing.
s8435 In winter a 7-year-old child fell into a frozen lake and was pulled out of the water after 20 minutes. No breathing or cardiac activity, pupils dilated, body temperature 28 °C. Resuscitation restored cardiac activity; over the following days cerebral edema and impaired lung and kidney function were observed, with gradual recovery.
s8436 Why was resuscitation successful even after 20 minutes? What condition are the post-recovery disturbances related to, and what is their mechanism?
s8437 At normal temperature clinical death lasts 4–6 minutes, but hypothermia (28 °C) sharply reduces the metabolism and oxygen demand of the tissues, especially the brain neurons; in children tissue resistance to hypoxia is higher, and the death was sudden (there was no prolonged agony, and energy reserves were preserved) — this is why clinical death was prolonged and resuscitation was successful. The later disturbances are post-resuscitation disease: reperfusion injury when blood flow is restored (reactive oxygen species, lipid peroxidation, entry of Ca2+ into cells), posthypoxic cerebral edema, lung injury due to aspiration and hypoxia (respiratory distress), renal ischemia — multiple organ failure. Such patients need prolonged observation in the intensive care unit.
