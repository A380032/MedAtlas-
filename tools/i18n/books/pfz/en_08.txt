s1431 6. Experimental part
s1432 Experiment 1: neuroparalytic arterial hyperemia in the rabbit ear (C. Bernard's experiment)
s1433 **Aim:** to observe the development of arterial hyperemia when the sympathetic vasoconstrictor influence is lost.
s1434 **Equipment:** a white (albino) rabbit, surgical instruments, a local anesthetic, a thermometer (electric thermometer), a light source.
s1435 Fix the rabbit on a special table; hold both ears against the light and compare the vascular network, color and temperature.
s1436 Under local anesthesia, incise the skin and tissues on one side of the neck, find the cervical sympathetic nerve lying next to the carotid artery and transect it.
s1437 After 5–10 minutes, compare both ears: in the ear on the side of transection, the vessels dilate, the ear reddens, more small vessels become visible and the temperature rises.
s1438 Record the results in the protocol.
s1439 **Analysis:** when the cervical sympathetic nerve is transected, the constant vasoconstrictor impulses to the vessels of the ear stop, the tone of arteriolar smooth muscle decreases and the arterioles dilate — **neuroparalytic arterial hyperemia** develops.
s1440 Experiment 2: venous hyperemia in the rabbit ear
s1441 Place a rubber ring or ligature at the base of the rabbit ear (where the vessels pass) so that it compresses only the veins and not the artery.
s1442 Observe for 10–15 minutes: the ear becomes bluish, the veins dilate and fill with blood, the temperature falls and the ear swells.
s1443 Remove the ring and observe the restoration of circulation.
s1444 Experiment 3: changes in the microcirculation in the frog mesentery
s1445 Under anesthesia (or after destruction of the spinal cord), fix the frog on a cork board, incise the abdominal wall from the side, bring out an intestinal loop, spread the mesentery over the opening and place it under the microscope.
s1446 In the normal state, observe blood flow in the arterioles, capillaries and venules: in the arterioles blood flows rapidly and pulsates, in the venules slowly and evenly; red blood cells are in the axis of the vessel, and there is a plasma layer next to the wall.
s1447 **Arterial hyperemia:** apply a drop of xylene (or warm physiological saline) to the mesentery — the arterioles dilate, previously invisible capillaries open and blood flow accelerates.
s1448 **Venous hyperemia:** tie a mesenteric vein with a ligature — the venules and capillaries dilate, blood flow slows, pendulum-like movement and stasis appear, and diapedesis of red blood cells is observed.

s1565 In a 35-year-old woman, bleeding was stopped by a tourniquet applied to the right leg for a long time (1.5 hours). After the tourniquet was removed, the skin of the leg became sharply red and warm, and the pulsation of the vessels increased. At the same time, the patient's arterial pressure fell and dizziness appeared.
s1566 What type of hyperemia developed in the leg? What is its mechanism? Why did arterial pressure fall?
s1567 After removal of the tourniquet, postischemic (reactive) arterial hyperemia developed. During ischemia, metabolites (adenosine, lactate, H+, K+, CO2) accumulated in the tissue and the tone of arteriolar smooth muscle decreased — the neuromyoparalytic (metabolic) mechanism. When blood flow was restored, a large volume of blood passed into the dilated vessels. Because a considerable part of the blood collected in the vessels of the leg, the circulating blood volume decreased relatively, arterial pressure fell and the blood supply of the brain deteriorated. That is why a tourniquet is released gradually and the patient's hemodynamics are monitored.
s1568 A 62-year-old patient has had mitral stenosis for several years. He complains of shortness of breath, cough and sometimes blood-streaked sputum. Examination of the sputum revealed hemosiderin-laden macrophages (“heart failure cells”).
s1569 What disorder has developed in the patient, and in which circulation? How do hemosiderin-laden macrophages appear? What is the outcome of this condition?
s1570 In mitral stenosis, the passage of blood from the left atrium into the left ventricle is impeded, and as a result venous hyperemia (congestion) develops in the pulmonary circulation. Increased pressure in the pulmonary capillaries leads to the exit of fluid and red blood cells into the alveoli (diapedesis) — that is why there is blood in the sputum. The red blood cells in the alveoli are phagocytosed by macrophages, and hemosiderin is formed from hemoglobin. Prolonged congestion and hypoxia lead to growth of connective tissue in the lungs — brown induration of the lung.
s1571 A 45-year-old woman, a shop assistant, works standing all day. Toward evening, heaviness and swelling appear in her legs. Examination revealed dilated, tortuous veins on the calves, a bluish-brown color of the skin in the ankle region and a trophic ulcer.
s1572 What circulatory disorder has developed? Explain the pathogenesis of the edema and the trophic ulcer. What dangerous complication can this condition cause?
s1573 As a result of venous valve insufficiency and gravity, chronic venous hyperemia has developed in the legs (varicose disease). Because the outflow of blood is impeded, hydrostatic pressure in the capillaries and venules rises — filtration predominates over reabsorption and edema appears. Prolonged congestion and edema lead to tissue hypoxia, diapedesis of red blood cells and accumulation of hemosiderin (brown pigmentation), impaired trophism of the skin and subcutaneous tissue, and a trophic ulcer. Slowing of blood flow increases the risk of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.
