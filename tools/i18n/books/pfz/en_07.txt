s1210 During an influenza epidemic in a kindergarten, 18 of 30 children fell ill. In 3 of them the disease was severe, with high fever and convulsions; in 2 children (one of whom was often ill and underweight) the disease ran a sluggish course without fever and was complicated by pneumonia. Twelve children did not fall ill at all.
s1211 How do you explain the different courses of the disease under the action of the same cause? Which forms of reactivity were observed? What played the decisive role in the children who did not fall ill?
s1212 The cause of the disease (the influenza virus) is the same, while the different courses depend on internal conditions — individual reactivity and resistance. The severe course with high fever and convulsions reflects hyperergic reactivity (in children, the immaturity and excitability of the nervous system create a predisposition to convulsions). The cases with a sluggish course without fever and with complications reflect hypoergic reactivity (low weight and frequent illness indicate a weakened immune response). The children who did not fall ill had high resistance: specific immunity resulting from vaccination or previous illness, and nonspecific resistance due to good nutrition and hardening.
s1213 A 52-year-old man, height 168 cm, body weight 98 kg, with a broad and short chest and an obtuse epigastric angle. Arterial pressure is 160/100 mm Hg; blood cholesterol and glucose are elevated. His father died of myocardial infarction at the age of 55.

s1216 In a student experiment in a pressure chamber, convulsions appeared at 9000 m in the control mouse. The mouse in narcotic sleep was “raised” to 11,500 m without convulsions, whereas in the mouse given caffeine convulsions began at 7500 m and agonal breathing developed rapidly.
s1217 Explain the results. How did reactivity and resistance change? What is the clinical significance of the result?
s1218 The cause is the same in all the mice — a decreased partial pressure of oxygen in the air. In the mouse in narcotic sleep, the central nervous system was inhibited, reactivity decreased, and metabolism and oxygen demand fell, so resistance to hypoxia increased. In the mouse given caffeine, the nervous system was excited and reactivity increased, but resistance decreased because oxygen consumption rose. This shows that reactivity and resistance do not always change in the same direction. Clinical significance: in patients at risk of hypoxia (after cardiac arrest, in severe brain injury), sedation and anesthesia are used to protect the brain; agitation, seizures and fever increase oxygen demand and aggravate hypoxia, so they must be eliminated.

s1296 **Hyperemia** (Greek hyper — excess, haima — blood) is increased blood filling of an organ or tissue. When the inflow of blood increases, **arterial (active)** hyperemia develops; when the outflow of blood is impeded, **venous (passive, congestive)** hyperemia develops.
s1297 3. Arterial hyperemia
s1298 **Arterial hyperemia** is an increased inflow of blood through the arteries into an organ or tissue and increased blood filling as a result of arteriolar dilatation, while the outflow of blood remains normal.
s1299 Types of arterial hyperemia
s1300 **Physiological arterial hyperemia** is a normal phenomenon observed when the activity of an organ increases:
s1301 **working (functional)** — in a working muscle, in the gastrointestinal tract during digestion, in the brain during mental work;
s1302 **reflex** — reddening of the skin under the action of heat;
s1303 **emotional** — reddening of the face from embarrassment or anger.
s1304 **Pathological arterial hyperemia** develops under the action of pathogenic factors, is not related to increased activity of the organ and is disproportionate to the stimulus.

s1342 Outcomes of arterial hyperemia
s1343 **Beneficial outcomes:** the supply of the tissue with oxygen and nutrients improves, metabolism, regeneration and phagocytosis increase, and metabolites are rapidly removed. That is why arterial hyperemia is used in physiotherapy (hot compresses, UHF, mustard plasters, cups).
s1344 **Harmful outcomes:** rupture of the wall of altered (atherosclerotic, aneurysmal) vessels and hemorrhage (for example, cerebral hemorrhage in a hypertensive crisis); increased intracranial pressure (headache); relative ischemia in other organs when a large part of the blood collects in one region.
s1345 Arterial hyperemia is often observed in the clinic: redness and heat in an inflammatory focus (rubor, calor), warm and moist skin in hyperthyroidism, “hot flashes” in menopause, flushing in carcinoid syndrome. Red, warm, pulsating tissue is always a sign of increased blood inflow.
