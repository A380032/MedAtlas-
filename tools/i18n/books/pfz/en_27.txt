s5589 A 35-year-old man lost blood from the femoral artery in a car accident. In hospital 1 hour after the accident: pulse 125 beats/min, arterial pressure 90/60 mm Hg, pale skin, cold sweat. Blood test: Hb 138 g/l, erythrocytes 4.4 × 10^12^/l, hematocrit 42 %. The next day: Hb 92 g/l, hematocrit 28 %. On day 6, reticulocytes 6 %.
s5590 Why is the first test close to normal? Explain the subsequent changes stage by stage.
s5591 In the first hour, the reflex (vascular) stage: plasma and erythrocytes have been lost in equal proportion, so although blood volume has decreased, hemoglobin and hematocrit per unit volume have not yet changed; at the same time, the hemodynamic signs (tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, cold sweat) indicate severe blood loss. The next day, the hydremic stage: tissue fluid moves into the blood vessels and restores circulating blood volume, the blood is diluted — hemoglobin and hematocrit fall (normochromic anemia). From day 4–5, the bone marrow stage: in response to hypoxia, renal erythropoietin increases and erythropoiesis is enhanced — reticulocytosis (6 %), the anemia is hyperregenerative.
s5592 A 12-year-old child has had episodes of jaundice and heaviness under the left costal margin since early childhood. His father has similar signs. The spleen is enlarged. Blood: Hb 95 g/l, color index 1.0, reticulocytes 8 %, microspherocytes in the smear, indirect bilirubin raised. Osmotic resistance of erythrocytes is decreased.
s5593 What is the disease? Explain its pathogenesis and the type of hemolysis. What treatment is effective?
s5594 Hereditary spherocytosis (Minkowski–Chauffard disease) — inherited in an autosomal dominant manner (the father is also affected). Because of a defect in erythrocyte membrane proteins (spectrin, ankyrin), membrane is lost, the erythrocyte becomes spherical and fragile, loses its deformability and, unable to pass through the splenic sinuses, is destroyed by macrophages — extravascular hemolysis. This is why splenomegaly and raised indirect bilirubin (jaundice) are seen; the bone marrow has increased erythropoiesis (reticulocytosis 8 %). Splenectomy almost stops the hemolysis, although the membrane defect persists.
s5595 A 25-year-old man was prescribed a sulfonamide drug for pneumonia. 2 days later his urine turned black, and weakness and jaundice appeared. Blood: Hb 78 g/l, haptoglobin markedly decreased, Heinz bodies in the smear. A similar episode had occurred previously after eating fava beans.
s5596 What condition has developed? Explain its mechanism.
s5597 An acute hemolytic crisis due to G6PD deficiency. With the enzyme deficiency, little NADPH is formed in the pentose phosphate pathway, reduced glutathione is not regenerated and erythrocytes are not protected from oxidative stress. Under the action of an oxidizing drug (sulfonamide) or substances in fava beans, hemoglobin is oxidized and precipitates (Heinz bodies), the membrane is damaged and the erythrocytes are destroyed mainly within the vessels: hemoglobinemia, hemoglobinuria (black urine), decreased haptoglobin; raised indirect bilirubin leads to jaundice. The oxidizing drug must be stopped and such drugs avoided.

s5632 The body has **no** physiological mechanism for actively excreting iron — iron balance is maintained only by regulating intestinal absorption. Daily loss (epithelial shedding, sweating) is 1–2 mg, with an additional 0.5–1 mg in menstruating women. **Hepcidin** (produced in the liver) degrades ferroportin and stops iron absorption from the intestine and its release from macrophages; hepcidin increases with iron overload and inflammation (IL-6) and decreases with iron deficiency and hypoxia.
s5633 **Funicular myelosis (subacute combined degeneration)** — a neurological complication specific to B~12~ deficiency: demyelination of the posterior and lateral columns of the spinal cord. Signs: paresthesias in the legs, loss of deep (vibration and joint-position) sensation, ataxia, spastic paresis, memory impairment. Folic acid deficiency does not cause such neurological signs.
s5634 Treating a patient with B~12~ deficiency with folic acid alone is dangerous: blood values may partly improve, but the neurological disturbances worsen and become irreversible. In megaloblastic anemia, B~12~ and folate levels should be determined first, and folic acid alone should not be given until B~12~ deficiency has been ruled out.
