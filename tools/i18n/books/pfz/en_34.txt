s6860 **Blood gases:** arterial pO~2~ is **normal**, but the oxygen capacity and oxygen content of the blood are decreased; the arteriovenous difference is decreased.
s6861 Circulatory hypoxia
s6862 Reduced blood flow to the tissues:
s6863 **ischemic** — reduced arterial blood flow (vascular spasm, thrombosis, blood loss, shock, heart failure);
s6864 **congestive** — impaired venous outflow (heart failure, venous thrombosis).
s6865 **Blood gases:** arterial pO~2~ and blood oxygen content normal (if the lungs are not damaged); because blood flows slowly in the capillaries, the tissue extracts more oxygen — the **arteriovenous difference increases**, venous pO~2~ decreases; cyanosis.
s6866 Tissue (histotoxic) hypoxia
s6867 The tissues cannot use oxygen:
s6868 inhibition of respiratory chain enzymes — **cyanides** (bind to the Fe^3+^ of cytochrome oxidase), sulfides, arsenic;
s6869 uncoupling of oxidation and phosphorylation — excess thyroid hormones, 2,4-dinitrophenol, some toxins;
s6870 impaired enzyme synthesis — deficiency of B vitamins (B~1~, B~2~, PP);
s6871 damage to mitochondrial membranes — ionizing radiation, lipid peroxidation, alcohol.
s6872 **Blood gases:** arterial pO~2~ normal, **venous pO~2~ increased** — oxygen remains unused, **arteriovenous difference decreased**; the skin is pink.
s6873 Load (exertional) and mixed hypoxia
s6874 **Exertional hypoxia** — during heavy physical work the oxygen demand exceeds the delivery capacity (oxygen debt). **Mixed** — several types together (e.g. circulatory, respiratory and tissue hypoxia in shock; hemic and circulatory in blood loss).

s6889 7. Adaptive reactions and consequences of hypoxia
s6890 Emergency adaptive mechanisms
s6891 **external respiration** — hyperventilation (stimulation of the chemoreceptors of the aortic and carotid bodies), opening of reserve alveoli;
s6892 **circulation** — tachycardia, increased stroke volume, release of blood from depots, redistribution of blood in favor of vital organs (brain, heart) (**centralization of the circulation**);
s6893 **blood** — release of erythrocytes from the spleen and other depots, enhanced oxyhemoglobin dissociation (acidosis, increased 2,3-diphosphoglycerate — the curve shifts to the right);
s6894 **tissue** — increased oxygen extraction, increased anaerobic glycolysis.
s6895 Long-term adaptive mechanisms
s6896 increased erythropoietin → **erythrocytosis** (in highlanders);
s6897 hypertrophy of the lungs and respiratory muscles, increased number of alveoli;
s6898 myocardial hypertrophy, increased capillary density (angiogenesis, the HIF-1 factor);
s6899 increased number of mitochondria and activity of respiratory enzymes, increased tissue resistance to oxygen deficiency.
s6900 Consequences of hypoxia
s6901 Tissues differ in their sensitivity to hypoxia: the most sensitive is the **cerebral cortex** (irreversible changes after 4–6 minutes of complete ischemia), followed by the myocardium, kidney and liver; the most resistant are bone, cartilage and connective tissue.
s6902 At the cellular level: decreased ATP → impaired ion pumps → entry of Na^+^ and water into the cell (cell swelling), Ca^2+^ accumulation → activation of phospholipases and proteases, lipid peroxidation → membrane damage; anaerobic glycolysis → lactic acidosis.
s6903 **Cyanosis** (bluish discoloration) appears when reduced (deoxygenated) hemoglobin in capillary blood exceeds 50 g/l. Therefore in severe anemia (low total hemoglobin) there may be no cyanosis even with deep hypoxia, while in polycythemia cyanosis may appear even with mild hypoxemia. In carbon monoxide poisoning the skin is pink, and in methemoglobinemia gray-bluish — the degree of cyanosis does not accurately reflect the severity of hypoxia.

s7007 Severe anemia
s7008 In winter, members of a family who had fallen asleep in a closed room with a coal stove burning were found in the morning with headache, nausea and severe weakness. One of them was unconscious, with pinkish-red skin and mucous membranes and shallow, rapid breathing. Arterial pO~2~ 92 mm Hg, carboxyhemoglobin 45 percent.
s7009 What type of hypoxia has developed? Explain its mechanism. Why is there no cyanosis and why is pO~2~ normal?
s7010 Carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning — hemic hypoxia. CO binds to hemoglobin about 200–300 times more strongly than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin — the oxygen capacity of the blood falls sharply; the dissociation curve of the remaining oxyhemoglobin shifts to the left, impeding oxygen delivery to the tissues; CO also partly inhibits cytochrome oxidase (a tissue component). Arterial pO~2~ is determined by the oxygen dissolved in plasma and remains normal because lung function is not impaired — which is why pulse oximetry and pO~2~ can be misleading. Because carboxyhemoglobin is bright red, the skin is pinkish-red and there is no cyanosis. Treatment — removal to fresh air, 100 percent oxygen (hyperbaric oxygenation if needed), which accelerates the dissociation of carboxyhemoglobin.
s7011 A 64-year-old man has smoked for 40 years and has had a productive cough and expiratory dyspnea for many years. His lips are bluish, the neck veins distended, and there is leg edema. Spirogram: VC 78 percent of predicted, FEV~1~ 42 percent, Tiffeneau index 48 percent. Arterial pO~2~ 55, pCO~2~ 54 mm Hg. Hemoglobin 178 g/l.
s7012 What is the type of ventilatory disorder? Determine the type of respiratory failure. How did the right ventricular failure and erythrocytosis develop?
s7013 Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: Tiffeneau index below 70 percent, FEV~1~ markedly decreased — obstructive type. Hypoxemia and hypercapnia — type II (hypercapnic) chronic decompensated respiratory failure (respiratory hypoxia, respiratory acidosis). Chronic alveolar hypoxia causes persistent spasm of the pulmonary arterioles (Euler–Liljestrand reflex) and then their remodeling — pulmonary hypertension; the right ventricle, overloaded by pressure, hypertrophies and decompensates — cor pulmonale: distended neck veins, edema. Hypoxia increases erythropoietin production in the kidney — secondary erythrocytosis (an adaptive reaction, but it increases blood viscosity); cyanosis is due to the large amount of reduced hemoglobin.
s7014 A 28-year-old mountaineer developed headache, insomnia, dyspnea and palpitations after a rapid ascent to 4500 m. At night his breathing is periodic: several deep breaths followed by a pause. Pulse 112 beats/min, respiratory rate 26 per minute, SaO~2~ 80 percent.
s7015 What type of hypoxia is this? How do blood gases change? Explain the adaptive reactions and the cause of the periodic breathing at night.
s7016 Exogenous hypobaric hypoxia (acute mountain sickness). Atmospheric pressure and the pO~2~ of the air are decreased — arterial pO~2~ and SaO~2~ are decreased. The chemoreceptors are stimulated and hyperventilation develops — a lot of CO~2~ is exhaled: hypocapnia and respiratory alkalosis; hypocapnia leads to constriction of the cerebral vessels and inhibition of the respiratory center. Emergency adaptation: hyperventilation, tachycardia, release of blood from depots, centralization of the circulation. Periodic (Cheyne–Stokes-type) breathing at night: during hyperventilation CO~2~ falls and the stimulus to the respiratory center is lost — apnea; during apnea CO~2~ accumulates and O~2~ falls, and the center is stimulated again. Long-term adaptation (over several weeks): erythropoietin and erythrocytosis, increased 2,3-diphosphoglycerate, increased capillary density and number of mitochondria. Gradual ascent (acclimatization) prevents mountain sickness.
