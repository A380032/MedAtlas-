s2572 A 3-year-old child has had recurrent severe bacterial infections (pneumonia, otitis, skin abscesses) since birth. Staphylococcus and Aspergillus were isolated from the abscesses. The neutrophil count in the blood is normal, and the neutrophils have preserved their ability for chemotaxis and engulfment, but the NBT test is negative.
s2573 What disease does the child have? Which stage of phagocytosis is impaired? Why are catalase-positive microbes particularly dangerous?
s2574 Chronic granulomatous disease — a hereditary defect of NADPH oxidase. Neutrophils engulf microbes, but because there is no “respiratory burst”, they cannot form the superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide; that is, the killing stage of phagocytosis is impaired, and the negative NBT test shows exactly this. Catalase-negative microbes “deliver” the hydrogen peroxide they produce into the phagolysosome, and myeloperoxidase uses it to kill the microbe. Catalase-positive microbes (staphylococcus, Aspergillus) break down their own peroxide, so in such patients they are not killed and cause recurrent abscesses and granulomas.
s2575 In a 60-year-old patient with diabetes mellitus, a small wound on a toe has not healed for a long time, and purulent inflammation is spreading to the surroundings. Glycated hemoglobin is 10%.
s2576 Why do inflammation and infections run a severe course in diabetes mellitus? Which stages of leukocyte function are impaired?
s2577 Prolonged hyperglycemia impairs several stages of leukocyte function: chemotaxis and adhesion of neutrophils weaken, phagocytosis and the “respiratory burst” decrease (NADPH deficiency — glucose is consumed by the polyol pathway), and opsonization is impaired. In addition, diabetic microangiopathy and macroangiopathy worsen the blood supply of the tissue, neuropathy causes the wound to go unnoticed, and regeneration and the formation of granulation tissue slow down. As a result, the infection is not delimited and the wound does not heal for a long time; control of glycemia is an important part of treatment.

s2624 1. The concept of allergy
s2625 **Allergy** (Greek allos — other, ergon — action) is an altered (enhanced) immune response of the organism on repeated exposure to an allergen, accompanied by injury of its own tissues. The term was proposed in 1906 by the Austrian pediatrician C. Pirquet.
s2626 Immunity and allergy are based on the same immune mechanisms; the difference lies in the result: in immunity the antigen is neutralized and the tissues are not injured, whereas in allergy the immune response injures the organism's own tissues.
s2627 **Sensitization** is an increase in the organism's sensitivity to an allergen after its first entry (formation of specific antibodies or sensitized T lymphocytes). Sensitization itself gives no clinical signs; it forms in 7–14 days and can persist for years.
s2628 **Active sensitization** — formation of the organism's own antibodies when an allergen is introduced.
s2629 **Passive sensitization** — when the serum (antibodies) or lymphocytes of a sensitized organism are transferred to another organism.
s2630 Allergens and their classification
s2631 **An allergen** is an antigen that causes an allergic reaction. Complete antigens (proteins, glycoproteins) and haptens (small molecules that are not antigens themselves but become allergens when bound to a protein: drugs, nickel, chromium) are distinguished.
