s3287 Consequences of hyperglycemia
s3288 **Glucosuria and osmotic diuresis** — when glucose exceeds the renal threshold it passes into the urine and takes water with it: polyuria, dehydration, thirst (polydipsia).
s3289 **Increased blood osmolarity** — dehydration of cells; in severe cases hyperosmolar coma.
s3290 **Glycation of proteins** — glucose binds non-enzymatically to the amino groups of proteins (glycated hemoglobin, collagen, crystallin); the basis of the long-term complications.
s3291 **Activation of the polyol (sorbitol) pathway** — in tissues that take up glucose independently of insulin (lens, nerves, kidney), sorbitol accumulates and damages the cells.

s3305 Signs of hypoglycemia
s3306 Hypoglycemia presents with two groups of signs:
s3307 **Adrenergic (autonomic) signs** — epinephrine release as a counter-insulin response: hunger, trembling, profuse sweating (cold sweat), tachycardia, palpitations, anxiety, pallor.
s3308 **Neuroglycopenic signs** — insufficient glucose supply to the brain: headache, impaired concentration, strange behavior (resembling drunkenness), visual disturbances, convulsions, loss of consciousness — **hypoglycemic coma**.
s3309 Care in hypoglycemia: if the patient is conscious — 15–20 g of rapidly absorbed carbohydrate (sugar, sweet juice); if unconscious — 40 % glucose solution intravenously or glucagon intramuscularly. In doubtful cases (when the type of coma is unclear) giving glucose is safer: it saves life in hypoglycemia and does not significantly worsen the condition in hyperglycemia.
s3310 5. Glucosuria
s3311 **Glucosuria** — the appearance of glucose in the urine. Normally, glucose filtered in the glomeruli is completely reabsorbed in the proximal tubules together with sodium (via the SGLT2 and SGLT1 transporters).
s3312 **Renal threshold** — the blood glucose concentration above which the tubular transporters become saturated and glucose begins to appear in the urine. In adults it is about **8.9–10 mmol/l**.
s3313 Table 10.6. Types of glucosuria
s3314 Hyperglycemic (extrarenal)
s3315 Blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold
s3316 Diabetes mellitus, alimentary and emotional hyperglycemia, after epinephrine injection
s3317 Renal
s3318 Blood glucose is normal, but tubular reabsorption is impaired (the renal threshold is lowered)
s3319 Hereditary renal glucosuria, Fanconi syndrome, pregnancy, phlorizin poisoning, treatment with SGLT2 inhibitors
s3320 SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) are modern drugs used to treat type 2 diabetes mellitus. They reduce glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubules, causing a “drug-induced renal glucosuria”, and thereby lower glycemia. These agents also have cardio- and renoprotective properties.
s3321 6. Glucose tolerance test
s3322 The glucose tolerance test assesses the body's ability to handle a glucose load.
s3323 Procedure: after 3 days of usual diet and an 8–14-hour overnight fast, fasting blood glucose is measured in the morning; the patient drinks 75 g of glucose dissolved in 250–300 ml of water within 5 minutes; after 2 hours (every 30 minutes in the extended version) glucose is measured again.

s3325 Glucose tolerance test curves (approximate, drawn for explanation)
s3326 Normally glucose peaks at 30–60 minutes (not exceeding 8–9 mmol/l) and returns to the baseline level by 2 hours (below 7.8 mmol/l). In impaired glucose tolerance the 2-hour value is 7.8–11.0 mmol/l, and in diabetes mellitus it is 11.1 mmol/l or higher.
s3327 Experiment 1: epinephrine hyperglycemia and glucosuria in the rabbit
s3328 Take blood from the rabbit's ear vein and determine the baseline glucose level (with a glucometer or the glucose oxidase method).
s3329 Place the rabbit in a metabolic cage or catheterize the bladder, and test the baseline urine for glucose (test strip or Nylander/Fehling test).
s3330 Inject 0.1 % epinephrine solution (0.1–0.2 ml/kg) subcutaneously.
s3331 Measure blood glucose again after 30, 60 and 90 minutes.
s3332 Once glycemia exceeds the renal threshold, test the urine for glucose.
s3333 **Expected results and analysis:** via beta2- and alpha-adrenoceptors in the liver, epinephrine stimulates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis and reduces insulin secretion — hyperglycemia develops.
s3334 When glucose exceeds the renal threshold, **hyperglycemic glucosuria** appears. This experiment is a model of emotional (stress) hyperglycemia.
s3335 Experiment 2: insulin hypoglycemia in the mouse
s3336 Fast two mice for 12–18 hours beforehand (their glycogen stores will be reduced).
s3337 Inject each of them intraperitoneally with insulin (at the dose given in the department's guidelines), and a third mouse with saline (control).
s3338 Observe the mice in a warm place and record the time at which signs appear: immobility, then restlessness, ruffled fur, loss of balance, convulsions, lying on the side.
s3339 As soon as convulsions begin, inject one mouse intraperitoneally with 0.5–1 ml of 40 % glucose solution; continue to observe the second one (with the teacher's permission if rescue is required).
s3340 Record the recovery of the glucose-treated mouse within a few minutes.
s3341 **Expected results and analysis:** insulin increases glucose uptake by tissues and stops glucose production in the liver; in the fasted mouse glycogen stores are low, so hypoglycemia develops quickly. The energy supply of neurons is impaired, and first excitation, then convulsions and coma develop. Giving glucose restores the energy supply of neurons and quickly rescues the animal — this proves that the cause of hypoglycemic convulsions is precisely the lack of glucose.
s3342 Experiment 3: qualitative detection of glucose in urine
s3343 Pour 2–3 ml of urine into a test tube.
s3344 Dip a test strip (glucose oxidase method) into the urine and, after the specified time, compare its color with the scale.
s3345 Alternative method — the Nylander test: when the reagent is added to urine and boiled, a black precipitate forms if glucose is present.
s3346 Compare normal urine, urine with added glucose and the urine of the experimental animal.
s3347 Skill: interpreting the glucose tolerance test

s3445 A 22-year-old man has type 1 diabetes mellitus. In the morning he took his usual dose of insulin but went to the gym without having breakfast. During the workout he developed intense hunger, trembling, cold sweat and palpitations, then began to behave inappropriately and lost consciousness; brief convulsions were observed.
s3446 What condition has developed? Explain the mechanism of the signs. What is the first aid?
s3447 Hypoglycemic coma. Insulin was taken, but no glucose was taken in (he did not eat), and physical exertion sharply increased glucose consumption by the muscles — hypoglycemia developed. Hunger, trembling, cold sweat and tachycardia are adrenergic signs: in response to hypoglycemia the sympathoadrenal system is activated and epinephrine is released. Changed behavior, loss of consciousness and convulsions are neuroglycopenic signs: brain neurons do not store glucose, and their function is impaired by lack of energy. First aid: because he is unconscious, give nothing by mouth; give 40 % glucose solution intravenously or glucagon intramuscularly; once he regains consciousness, give carbohydrate food.
s3448 Glucose was found in the urine of a 30-year-old pregnant woman. Fasting blood glucose is 4.6 mmol/l and the glucose tolerance test is normal.
s3449 Which type of glucosuria can be considered? Explain its mechanism.
s3450 Renal glucosuria. Blood glucose is normal and the tolerance test is not impaired, so there is no hyperglycemia. In pregnancy glomerular filtration increases and more glucose reaches the proximal tubules, while the tubular reabsorptive capacity is relatively reduced — the renal threshold falls and glucose appears in the urine even when blood levels are normal. Nevertheless, glycemia is still monitored to rule out diabetes of pregnancy (gestational diabetes).
s3451 A 40-year-old patient with a head injury has blood glucose of 11.5 mmol/l and glucose in the urine. He had no previous diabetes mellitus. After two weeks glycemia returned to normal.
s3452 What type of hyperglycemia is this? Explain the mechanism. Which classic experiment models this condition?
s3453 Neurogenic (and stress) hyperglycemia. Brain injury irritates the autonomic centers in the hypothalamus and medulla oblongata and activates the sympathoadrenal system and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis: epinephrine and cortisol release increases, and glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis are enhanced. Because glycemia exceeded the renal threshold, glucosuria appeared. After the acute period of the injury, the hyperglycemia resolved. This condition is modeled by C. Bernard's “sugar puncture” experiment: when a needle is inserted into the floor of the fourth ventricle, the animal develops hyperglycemia and glucosuria.
