s2704 4. Anaphylaxis and anaphylactic shock
s2705 **Anaphylaxis** (Greek ana — reverse, phylaxis — protection) — the systemic (generalized) form of a type I allergic reaction. It was discovered in 1902 by Ch. Richet and P. Portier: dogs that had received a small dose of sea anemone toxin died instead of being protected when it was injected a second time.
s2706 **Anaphylactic shock** — the most severe, life-threatening form of a type I allergic reaction.
s2707 Figure 8.2
s2708 Pathogenesis of anaphylactic shock (based on A.V. Ataman, 2000, Fig. 27)
s2709 Main mediators of anaphylactic shock and their effects:
s2710 **histamine** — dilates arterioles, increases venular permeability, constricts the bronchi, causes itching;
s2711 **leukotrienes C~4~, D~4~, E~4~** (formerly called the “slow-reacting substance of anaphylaxis”) — strong and prolonged bronchospasm, increased permeability;
s2712 **platelet-activating factor** — hypotension, bronchospasm, platelet aggregation;
s2713 **prostaglandin D~2~** — bronchospasm, vasodilation;
s2714 **tryptase** — a laboratory marker of mast cell activation.
s2715 In humans the main “target organs” of anaphylactic shock are the cardiovascular system (hypotension, collapse) and the airways (laryngeal edema, bronchospasm). In the guinea pig, by contrast, bronchospasm and asphyxia predominate; in the dog — spasm of the hepatic veins and pooling of blood in the portal system; in the rabbit — spasm of the pulmonary arteries. This interspecies difference is explained by the concept of the “shock organ”.
s2716 The first and main drug in anaphylactic shock is **epinephrine (adrenaline)** (0.1 % solution, 0.3–0.5 ml into the thigh muscle in adults, repeated after 5–15 minutes if needed). Via alpha-adrenoceptors it constricts vessels and raises arterial pressure; via beta2-receptors it dilates the bronchi and stops mediator release from mast cells. Additional measures: stop the allergen, lay the patient down with legs raised, oxygen, intravenous crystalloid solutions; antihistamines and glucocorticoids are second-line agents.
s2717 Local manifestations of allergy
s2718 **Hives (urticaria)** — itchy wheals on the skin; increased vascular permeability in the dermis.
s2719 **Quincke's edema (angioedema)** — circumscribed deep edema of the subcutaneous and submucosal tissue; risk of asphyxia if it develops in the larynx.
s2720 **Allergic rhinitis and conjunctivitis (pollinosis)** — seasonal, occurring when plants are in bloom.
s2721 **Atopic bronchial asthma** — bronchial hyperreactivity, bronchospasm, mucus hypersecretion and eosinophilic inflammation.
s2722 **Arthus phenomenon** — when an allergen is re-injected subcutaneously into a sensitized animal, immune complexes deposit at that site and hyperemia, edema and necrosis develop (type III).
s2723 5. Desensitization
s2724 **Desensitization (hyposensitization)** — reduction or elimination of the body's sensitivity to an allergen.
s2725 **Specific desensitization** — directed at a particular allergen:
s2726 **Besredka method (rapid desensitization)** — the allergen (e.g. a therapeutic serum) is first given in very small doses, then in gradually increasing doses at 20–30-minute intervals. The small doses gradually “bind” the IgE on mast cells and release mediators in small, safe portions; the full dose then does not cause shock. The effect is temporary.
s2727 **Allergen-specific immunotherapy (slow desensitization)** — the allergen is given in increasing doses over months and years. Mechanism: formation of “blocking” IgG antibodies (they capture the allergen before IgE does), a shift of the Th2 response towards Th1 and regulatory T cells, and reduced IgE synthesis.
s2728 **Nonspecific desensitization** — acting on the common mechanisms of allergic reactions: antihistamines, mast cell membrane stabilizers (cromoglicate), glucocorticoids, leukotriene receptor antagonists, adrenomimetics, monoclonal anti-IgE antibodies (omalizumab).

s2749 Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)
s2750 The causative agent of AIDS is the **human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)**, a member of the retrovirus family. The virus binds to the CD4 molecule (and the CCR5 or CXCR4 coreceptors) and mainly damages **CD4^+^ T helper cells**, as well as macrophages and dendritic cells.
s2751 Pathogenesis: the virus enters the cell → a DNA copy is made from RNA by reverse transcriptase → it is integrated into the cell genome by integrase → the virus replicates and CD4^+^ cells die → the number of CD4^+^ lymphocytes gradually falls → cellular and humoral immunity are profoundly impaired → opportunistic infections and tumors develop.
s2752 Experiment 1: anaphylactic shock in the guinea pig
s2753 **Aim:** to model and observe the systemic form of a type I allergic reaction — anaphylactic shock.
s2754 **Equipment:** guinea pigs (at least 2 sensitized and 1 control), horse serum (allergen), syringes, stopwatch.
s2755 **Sensitization** (performed in advance by the teacher): 14–21 days earlier, 0.1 ml of horse serum (sensitizing dose) was injected subcutaneously or intraperitoneally into the guinea pig.
s2756 Assess the baseline state of the sensitized and control (non-sensitized) animals: movement, respiratory rate, condition of the fur.
s2757 Inject both animals intravenously (or intracardially) with 0.5–1 ml of horse serum (challenge dose).
s2758 In the sensitized animal, record in sequence the signs that develop within a few minutes: restlessness, rubbing the nose, sneezing, ruffled fur, passage of urine and feces, difficulty breathing (expiratory dyspnea), trembling, convulsions, falling onto its side.
s2759 Record that no such changes are observed in the control animal.
s2760 On autopsy of the animal that died, observe the markedly distended (emphysematous), non-collapsing lungs — air is trapped in the alveoli because of bronchospasm.
s2761 **Analysis:** in the sensitized guinea pig, IgE (and IgG1) antibodies are bound to mast cells.
s2762 When the challenge dose is given, mast cell degranulation and release of mediators (mainly histamine) cause severe bronchiolar spasm and asphyxia — the guinea pig's “shock organ” is the lung.
s2763 Experiment 2: desensitization by the Besredka method
s2764 Before the challenge dose, inject a second sensitized guinea pig subcutaneously with a very small dose (0.01–0.05 ml) of horse serum.
s2765 After 20–30 minutes give a larger dose, then after another 20–30 minutes inject the full challenge dose intravenously.
s2766 Observe the animal — anaphylactic shock does not develop or is mild.
s2767 **Analysis:** the small doses gradually bind the antibodies on mast cells, and mediators are released in small, harmless amounts; when the full dose is given, not enough active antibodies remain to cause shock.
s2768 This is the basis of the Besredka method, which was used clinically when administering therapeutic sera (e.g. antitetanus or antivenom serum).
s2769 Skill: determining the type of allergic reaction
s2770 Determine the time of onset of the reaction: minutes (type I), hours (types II–III) or 1–3 days (type IV).
s2771 Assess the main clinical picture: bronchospasm and hypotension (I), hemolysis or cytopenia (II), vasculitis, glomerulonephritis, arthralgia (III), contact dermatitis or infiltrate (IV).
s2772 Determine the nature of the allergen: soluble protein, cell-surface antigen, hapten.
s2773 Take laboratory findings into account: blood IgE and eosinophils (I), Coombs test (II), immune complexes and decreased complement (III).
s2774 Write a conclusion and indicate the main direction of treatment.
s2775 Skill: emergency care algorithm for anaphylactic shock
s2776 Stop exposure to the allergen (stop the drug infusion, remove the bee sting).
s2777 Call for help; lay the patient on their back and raise the legs (semi-sitting position if breathing is difficult).
s2778 Inject epinephrine into the middle outer surface of the thigh muscle; if there is no effect, repeat after 5–15 minutes.
s2779 Ensure airway patency and give oxygen.
s2780 Establish venous access and infuse crystalloid solutions.
s2781 Give an antihistamine and a glucocorticoid (second line).
s2782 Monitor arterial pressure, pulse and breathing continuously; the patient must be observed for at least 24 hours (risk of a delayed wave).

s2864 Quincke's edema is a local manifestation of which type of allergic reaction?
s2865 A 28-year-old woman was given an anesthetic for local anesthesia at the dentist. After 5 minutes she complained of shortness of breath, tightness in the throat and itching all over the body. Red wheals appeared on the skin, her lips swelled, arterial pressure was 70/40 mm Hg, pulse 130 beats/min, breathing wheezy.
s2866 What condition has developed? Explain its pathogenesis. In what order is emergency care provided?
s2867 Anaphylactic shock — the systemic form of a type I (IgE-mediated) allergic reaction. The patient had previously been sensitized to this anesthetic: IgE specific to it is present on the surface of mast cells and basophils. On re-exposure the allergen binds to IgE and causes mast cell degranulation; histamine, leukotrienes and platelet-activating factor are released. They sharply dilate the vessels and increase their permeability (hypotension, reduced circulating blood volume, compensatory tachycardia), constrict the bronchi (wheezing, dyspnea), and cause edema (lips, larynx) and urticaria. Care: stop the anesthetic, immediately inject epinephrine into the thigh muscle, lay the patient down with legs raised, oxygen, intravenous crystalloid solutions; then an antihistamine and a glucocorticoid; observation for at least 24 hours.
