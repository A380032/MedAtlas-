s5674 **Folic acid** is found in green leaves, liver and legumes; it is quickly destroyed by cooking. The daily requirement is 200–400 µg; the store lasts only 3–4 months.
s5675 **Role in DNA synthesis:** folic acid (as tetrahydrofolate) carries one-carbon groups in the synthesis of thymidylate (the thymine nucleotide for DNA). B~12~, as methylcobalamin, takes the methyl group from methyltetrahydrofolate and converts homocysteine to methionine, thereby “freeing” tetrahydrofolate. In B~12~ deficiency, folic acid is “trapped” in the methyl form (the **folate trap**) and DNA synthesis is impaired.
s5676 In addition, B~12~ (as adenosylcobalamin) takes part in the conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA; in its deficiency methylmalonate accumulates and myelin synthesis is impaired — **neurological disturbances** are specific to B~12~ deficiency.
s5677 4. Megaloblastic anemias
s5678 **Megaloblastic anemias** — hyperchromic macrocytic anemias characterized by impaired DNA synthesis due to vitamin B~12~ or folic acid deficiency and a switch of erythropoiesis to the megaloblastic type.

s5693 As a result of autoimmune atrophic gastritis, the gastric parietal cells die: autoantibodies against **parietal cells** and **Castle's intrinsic factor** are formed → intrinsic factor and hydrochloric acid are not produced (achlorhydria) → B~12~ is not absorbed. s5694 Pathogenesis of megaloblastic anemia
s5695 DNA synthesis slows down while RNA and protein synthesis continue → **asynchrony of nuclear and cytoplasmic maturation** (the nucleus is “young”, the cytoplasm “mature”).
s5696 Large cells — **megaloblasts** — form in the bone marrow.
s5697 Most megaloblasts die in the bone marrow itself — **ineffective erythropoiesis** (intramedullary hemolysis); therefore indirect bilirubin and LDH rise.
s5698 The **megalocytes** released into the blood are large and saturated with hemoglobin, but fragile and short-lived.
s5699 Leukopoiesis and thrombopoiesis are also impaired — **pancytopenia**, hypersegmented neutrophils, large platelets.
s5700 All rapidly dividing cells (gastrointestinal epithelium) are affected — **Hunter's glossitis** (a smooth, “lacquered”, bright red tongue), dyspepsia.
s5701 **Blood picture:** hyperchromic (color index above 1.05, often 1.2–1.5) macrocytic (MCV above 100 fl) anemia; megalocytes (oval), megaloblasts; Howell–Jolly bodies, Cabot rings, basophilic stippling; aniso- and poikilocytosis; reticulocytes decreased (aregenerative); leukopenia, **hypersegmentation of neutrophils** (right nuclear shift), thrombocytopenia.
s5702 5. Other hyporegenerative anemias
s5703 **Aplastic anemia** — a sharp depression of the hematopoietic function of the bone marrow (hypoplasia or aplasia) with damage to stem cells. Causes: autoimmune (T lymphocytes damage the stem cells), drugs (chloramphenicol, cytostatics), benzene, radiation, viruses (hepatitis, parvovirus B19), hereditary (Fanconi anemia). Blood picture: pancytopenia (anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia), normochromic anemia, reticulocytes markedly decreased; the bone marrow is replaced by adipose tissue.
s5704 **Anemia of chronic disease** (anemia of inflammation) — in chronic infections, autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid arthritis) and tumors. Mechanism: under the action of IL-6, hepcidin increases → iron is “locked” in macrophages and its intestinal absorption decreases (functional iron deficiency); erythropoietin production and sensitivity to it decrease; erythrocyte lifespan is shortened. Blood picture: normo- or hypochromic anemia; serum iron decreased, but **ferritin normal or increased** (iron is present in the stores).
s5705 **Anemia in chronic kidney failure** — mainly due to reduced erythropoietin production; treatment — erythropoiesis-stimulating agents.

s5849 A 38-year-old woman has weakness, dizziness, hair loss and brittle nails; she craves chalk and likes the smell of acetone. Her periods last 8–9 days and are heavy. Blood test: Hb 78 g/l, erythrocytes 3.6 × 10^12^/l, MCV 68 fl, reticulocytes 0.8 %, serum iron 4.2 µmol/l, ferritin 6 µg/l.
s5850 What anemia is this? Calculate the color index. Explain the mechanism of the clinical signs.
s5851 Iron deficiency anemia due to chronic blood loss (heavy and prolonged periods — menorrhagia). Color index = (78 × 3) / 360 ≈ 0.65 — hypochromic; MCV 68 fl — microcytic; ferritin markedly decreased (stores exhausted), serum iron low; reticulocytes normal — hyporegenerative. Weakness and dizziness — the anemic syndrome (hypoxia). Hair loss, brittle nails and disturbed taste and smell (eating chalk, liking the smell of acetone) — the sideropenic syndrome: reduced activity of iron-containing enzymes in the tissues and impaired trophism of the epithelium and its derivatives. Treatment — iron preparations, plus identifying and eliminating the cause of the menorrhagia.
s5852 A 62-year-old man has weakness, dyspnea, numbness in the legs and an unsteady gait. His tongue is smooth, bright red and burning. The skin is slightly yellowish. Blood test: Hb 70 g/l, erythrocytes 1.7 × 10^12^/l, MCV 118 fl, leukocytes 3.1 × 10^9^/l, platelets 110 × 10^9^/l, reticulocytes 0.2 %; megalocytes, Howell–Jolly bodies and hypersegmented neutrophils in the smear. Indirect bilirubin is raised. Antibodies to parietal cells are positive.
s5853 What is the disease? Explain the pathogenesis of the signs.
s5854 Pernicious (Addison–Biermer) anemia — absence of intrinsic factor and failure of B~12~ absorption due to autoimmune atrophic gastritis. Color index = (70 × 3) / 170 ≈ 1.24 — hyperchromic, macrocytic. In B~12~ deficiency DNA synthesis is impaired and erythropoiesis switches to the megaloblastic type; megaloblasts die in the bone marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis) — indirect bilirubin rises and causes the yellowish skin; leuko- and thrombopoiesis are also impaired — pancytopenia, hypersegmentation of neutrophils. Hunter's glossitis — damage to the rapidly dividing epithelium of the tongue. Numbness in the legs and ataxia — funicular myelosis: accumulation of methylmalonyl-CoA and impaired myelin synthesis damage the posterior and lateral columns of the spinal cord. Treatment — lifelong parenteral (intramuscular) B~12~.
s5855 A 55-year-old woman has had rheumatoid arthritis for 10 years. Blood test: Hb 98 g/l, MCV 82 fl, serum iron 7 µmol/l, ferritin 240 µg/l, C-reactive protein raised.
s5856 What anemia is this? Why is serum iron low but ferritin high? Will iron preparations be effective?
s5857 Anemia of chronic disease (inflammation). In chronic inflammation, IL-6 increases hepcidin synthesis in the liver; hepcidin degrades ferroportin and stops the release of iron from macrophages and its absorption from the intestine. As a result, iron is “locked” in the stores (macrophages) — ferritin is high, but there is not enough iron in the blood for erythropoiesis — serum iron is low (functional iron deficiency). In addition, cytokines reduce erythropoietin production and sensitivity to it. Oral iron preparations are of little benefit because their absorption is blocked by hepcidin; the main treatment is to reduce the inflammatory activity (treat the underlying disease).
