s0537 The action of high temperature. Hyperthermia
s0538 **Hyperthermia** is a rise in body temperature resulting from insufficient heat loss relative to heat production. It occurs with high external temperature, high air humidity (sweat does not evaporate), heat-insulating clothing and heavy physical work.

s0553 **Sunstroke** is overheating, hyperemia and edema of the brain and its membranes resulting from the direct action of the sun's rays on the head. Its main signs are severe headache, vomiting and impaired consciousness.
s0554 **A burn** is the local action of high temperature. By depth, degrees I–IV are distinguished. With extensive burns (more than 15–20% of the body surface, and even less in children and the elderly), **burn disease** develops: it runs through the periods of burn shock, toxemia, septicotoxemia and recovery.
s0555 The action of low temperature. Hypothermia
s0556 **Hypothermia** is a fall in body temperature below normal (below 35 °C) resulting from heat loss exceeding heat production. In cold water the body cools much faster than in air, because the thermal conductivity of water is about 25 times higher than that of air.

s0579 **Artificial hypothermia** is used in medicine: in heart and brain operations, cooling the body to 28–32 °C reduces the oxygen demand of tissues and increases their resistance to hypoxia. s0580 **Frostbite** is the local action of low temperature. Its main mechanism is vascular spasm, slowing of blood flow, stasis and thrombosis, tissue ischemia and necrosis. The fingers, ears, nose and feet are most often affected.
s0581 3. Changes in atmospheric (barometric) pressure
s0582 Decreased atmospheric pressure. Mountain sickness and altitude sickness
s0583 When ascending to altitude, two factors act on the organism: **decreased atmospheric pressure (decompression)** and **decreased partial pressure of oxygen (pO~2~) in the air**. As P. Bert and I.M. Sechenov showed, the main pathogenic factor of altitude is the fall in pO2 and the resulting **hypoxic (exogenous) hypoxia**.

s0620 **Mountain sickness** develops gradually, several hours or days after ascent; **altitude sickness**, in contrast, arises rapidly (within minutes and seconds) when the sealing of an aircraft is suddenly lost.
s0621 Pathogenesis of mountain sickness: fall in pO2 → hypoxemia → stimulation of chemoreceptors → hyperventilation → hypocapnia and respiratory (gas) alkalosis → constriction of cerebral vessels → worsening of cerebral hypoxia. In severe cases **pulmonary edema** and **cerebral edema** develop.
s0622 **Changes related to decompression:** intestinal gases expand (at 6000 m the volume of gas roughly doubles) — flatulence, abdominal pain; pain in closed cavities (middle ear, maxillary sinus); above 7000–8000 m, nitrogen dissolved in the blood forms bubbles (gas embolism); at an altitude of about 19 km, water begins to boil at body temperature (“altitude emphysema”).
s0623 Mechanisms of adaptation (acclimatization): hyperventilation, tachycardia, release of red blood cells from blood depots (urgent); increased production of erythropoietin in the kidneys, increased red blood cell count and hemoglobin, increased 2,3-diphosphoglycerate in red blood cells, and an increased number of capillaries and mitochondria in tissues (long-term). Stepwise ascent into the mountains (N.N. Sirotinin's method) prevents mountain sickness.
s0624 Increased atmospheric pressure. Decompression sickness
s0625 Under water, pressure increases by about 1 atmosphere for every 10 m of depth. Under high pressure (divers, caisson workers), more gas dissolves in the blood and tissues — **saturation**. Nitrogen accumulates especially in lipid-rich tissues (nervous tissue, adipose tissue) and causes **nitrogen narcosis** (euphoria, then impaired coordination of movements and clouding of consciousness). A high partial pressure of oxygen leads to **oxygen toxicity** (formation of free radicals, injury of the lungs and central nervous system).
s0626 **Decompression (caisson) sickness** is gas embolism, tissue ischemia and compression of nerve endings resulting from the release of nitrogen dissolved in the blood and tissues in the form of bubbles (**desaturation**) during rapid transition from high to normal pressure. Signs: pain in joints and muscles, itching of the skin, shortness of breath, paralysis.
s0627 4. The action of electric current
s0628 Electric current acts on the organism in two ways: **specifically** (electrochemical, electrothermal, electromechanical action) and **nonspecifically** (injury by light, sound and mechanical force). The harmful effect of current depends on the following:
s0629 **current strength** (the main factor) — a current of more than 0.1 A passing through the heart can cause ventricular fibrillation;
s0630 **voltage** — a voltage above 40 V is considered dangerous;
s0631 **type and frequency of current** — alternating current with a frequency of 40–60 Hz is the most dangerous; high-frequency (UHF) current acts mainly as heat and is used in physiotherapy;
s0632 **skin resistance** — dry skin has a high resistance, while the resistance of wet skin falls sharply;
s0633 **the path of the current (“current loop”)** — paths through the heart and brain (hand — hand, left hand — legs) are the most dangerous;
s0634 **duration of exposure** and **state of the organism** (resistance changes with fatigue, alcohol and sleep).
s0635 Local changes: at the points of entry and exit of the current, **“current marks”** (electrical marks) — gray-yellowish, hard, painless spots; burns, tissue necrosis. Systemic changes: loss of consciousness, convulsions, respiratory arrest (paralysis of the respiratory center or tetanic contraction of the respiratory muscles), disturbance and arrest of the heart rhythm.
s0636 First aid for electrical injury: first separate the victim from the source of current without endangering the rescuer (switch off the current, push away with dry wood), then check breathing and circulation and, if necessary, start cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Even with an apparently mild injury, the patient must remain under observation because of the risk of delayed arrhythmias.
s0637 5. The action of ionizing radiation
s0638 Ionizing radiation (X-rays and gamma rays, alpha and beta particles, neutrons) passes through tissues and ionizes atoms and molecules. The absorbed dose is measured in **grays (Gy)** (1 Gy = 1 J/kg).
s0639 Mechanism of action:
s0640 **direct action** — direct injury of DNA, protein and enzyme molecules;
s0641 **indirect action** — radiolysis of water produces active free radicals (OH•, H•, hydrogen peroxide), which injure biomolecules. Because about 70% of the organism consists of water, the indirect action is the main one.

s0659 6. The action of mechanical factors
s0660 Mechanical factors (impact, compression, stretching, acceleration, vibration) disrupt the integrity of tissues: wounds, contusions, bone fractures. In severe mechanical injury, systemic reactions develop together with local changes — **traumatic shock** (discussed in detail in Topic 27).
s0661 **Crush syndrome (prolonged compression syndrome)** develops as a result of prolonged (several hours) compression of soft tissues (most often of the limbs). Pathogenesis: ischemia and muscle necrosis in the compressed tissues → when the pressure is removed, blood flow is restored and myoglobin, potassium and toxic products pass from the tissues into the blood → hyperkalemia (risk of arrhythmia and cardiac arrest), myoglobinuria and obstruction of the renal tubules → **acute kidney injury**; marked edema of the injured limb → plasma loss and hypovolemia.
s0662 In crush syndrome, it is recommended to apply a tourniquet to the limb and start intravenous infusion **before** the pressure is removed — this prevents a sudden release of potassium and toxins into the blood. In victims of earthquakes and accidents, this syndrome is one of the main causes of death.
s0663 **Acceleration** (aviation, space flight, transport) leads to redistribution of blood: with acceleration directed toward the head, blood pools in the legs, and cerebral ischemia and blackout are observed. With prolonged exposure, **noise** and **vibration** cause occupational diseases (deafness, vibration disease).
s0664 7. Chemical, biological and social factors
s0665 Chemical factors
s0666 The effect of chemical substances depends on their nature, dose, route of entry into the organism and the state of the organism. The action may be **local** (burns by acids and alkalis) or **systemic** (after absorption — resorptive). Most poisons act selectively on certain organs (organotropism):
s0667 carbon monoxide blocks hemoglobin, cyanides block the enzymes of tissue respiration (cytochrome oxidase), organophosphorus compounds inhibit cholinesterase, mercuric chloride injures the kidneys, and carbon tetrachloride injures the liver.
s0668 The organism detoxifies poisons mainly in the liver (oxidation, reduction, conjugation with glucuronic and sulfuric acids) and excretes them through the kidneys, intestine and lungs. In liver and kidney diseases the effect of poisons is enhanced.
s0669 Biological factors
s0670 Biological factors include viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, helminths and prions. Their pathogenicity is determined by **virulence** (the degree of ability to cause disease), **invasiveness** (the ability to penetrate and spread in tissues) and **toxigenicity** (production of exotoxins and endotoxins). The development of disease depends on the number of microorganisms (infectious dose), the route of entry and the resistance of the macroorganism.
s0671 Psychogenic and social factors
s0672 Prolonged mental strain (distress) activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal system and the sympathetic nervous system. This promotes the development of psychosomatic diseases such as arterial hypertension, ischemic heart disease and peptic ulcer. Social factors (standard of living, nutrition, education, availability of medical care) largely determine the morbidity and mortality of the population.
