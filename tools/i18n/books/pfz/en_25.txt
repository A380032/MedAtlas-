s5083 1. The concept of leukemia
s5084 **Leukemia (hemoblastosis)** — a neoplastic disease of the hematopoietic tissue, characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of tumor cells (a leukemic clone) derived from a single mutated hematopoietic cell in the bone marrow and other organs, and by displacement of normal hematopoiesis.
s5085 The three main features of leukemia:
s5086 **neoplastic nature** — leukemic cells have all the properties of tumor cells (uncontrolled proliferation, atypia, metastasis — leukemic infiltrates);
s5087 **clonal origin** — all leukemic cells are descendants of a single mutated cell;
s5088 **location** — the tumor starts in the bone marrow and spreads to the blood (leukemic cells do not have to be numerous in the blood).
s5089 The term “leukemia” (“white blood”) is historical: it refers to the whitish color the blood takes on when leukocytes are greatly increased (R. Virchow, 1845). However, only some leukemias have leukocytosis in the blood.

s5100 3. Pathogenesis of leukemias
s5101 According to the modern **clonal theory**, leukemia begins with a mutation (often a chromosomal translocation) in a single hematopoietic stem or progenitor cell. As a result of the mutation:
s5102 **proliferation** of the cell escapes control (oncogenes are activated);
s5103 **differentiation** (maturation) is blocked — in acute leukemias the cells “get stuck” at the blast stage;
s5104 **apoptosis** is impaired — the cells live long (especially in chronic lymphocytic leukemia).
s5105 As the leukemic clone develops, new mutations accumulate and the tumor becomes more malignant — **tumor progression**: transition from the monoclonal to the polyclonal stage, emergence of resistance to cytostatics, and development of a **blast crisis** in chronic leukemias.
s5106 An example of a common chromosomal change in leukemias is the **Philadelphia chromosome** t(9;22): the ABL gene on chromosome 9 fuses with the BCR gene on chromosome 22, producing a permanently active tyrosine kinase. It is found in almost all cases of chronic myeloid leukemia. Imatinib, which selectively blocks this kinase, was the first successful example of targeted therapy in cancer.

s5142 6. Chronic leukemias
s5143 Chronic myeloid leukemia
s5144 Chronic myeloid leukemia is a myeloproliferative disease arising from a stem cell and characterized by the Philadelphia chromosome.
s5145 **Peripheral blood:** high leukocytosis (50–200 × 10^9^/l or more); **all stages of granulocyte maturation** are present in the blood (myeloblasts, promyelocytes, myelocytes, metamyelocytes, band and segmented neutrophils) — a “marked left nuclear shift”, with **no** leukemic “gap”; **eosinophil–basophil association** (eosinophils and basophils increase together); thrombocytosis may be present at the onset; neutrophil alkaline phosphatase activity is reduced.
s5146 Clinical signs: marked enlargement of the spleen (splenomegaly), enlargement of the liver, weakness, sweating.
s5147 Table 16.5. Phases of chronic myeloid leukemia
s5148 Chronic (stable) phase
s5149 Few blasts in blood and bone marrow (below 10 percent); mature cells predominate; good response to treatment
s5150 Accelerated phase
s5151 Blasts and basophils increase, resistance to treatment appears
s5152 Blast crisis
s5153 Blasts above 20 percent; course resembling acute leukemia; poor prognosis
s5154 Chronic lymphocytic leukemia
s5155 Chronic lymphocytic leukemia is a tumor characterized by the accumulation of mature-looking but functionally defective B lymphocytes; it occurs mainly in the elderly (the most common leukemia in adults in Western countries).
s5156 **Peripheral blood:** leukocytosis; **absolute lymphocytosis** (lymphocytes up to 80–90 percent), mainly small mature lymphocytes; **Botkin–Gumprecht shadows** (smudge cells) — remnants of lymphocyte nuclei destroyed during smear preparation (because of the fragility of the lymphocytes).
s5157 Clinical signs: enlargement of lymph nodes in many regions (painless, soft-elastic), splenomegaly; recurrent infections due to hypogammaglobulinemia; autoimmune hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia.

s5310 A 4-year-old child has had weakness, pallor, fever, leg pain and bruises on the skin for 3 weeks. The cervical lymph nodes, liver and spleen are enlarged. Blood test: hemoglobin 72 g/l, platelets 25 × 10^9^/l, leukocytes 32 × 10^9^/l, blast cells 68 %, segmented neutrophils 8 %, no intermediate stages, lymphocytes 24 %.
s5311 What disease can be suspected? Explain the changes in the blood test and the clinical signs.
s5312 Acute leukemia; given the child's age and enlarged lymph nodes, probably acute lymphoblastic leukemia (bone marrow examination and immunophenotyping are needed for confirmation). The blood contains many blast cells and mature neutrophils, with no intermediate stages — the leukemic “gap”: the leukemic clone does not mature, while the mature neutrophils come from the remnants of normal hematopoiesis. Anemia and thrombocytopenia — the bone marrow is filled with blasts and normal erythro- and thrombopoiesis are displaced; pallor and weakness — the anemic syndrome; bruises — the hemorrhagic syndrome. Fever — infectious complications and intoxication. Bone pain — bone marrow infiltration; enlargement of the lymph nodes, liver and spleen — the hyperplastic (infiltrative) syndrome.
