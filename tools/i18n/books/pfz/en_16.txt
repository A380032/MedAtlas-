s3071 **Cachectic (starvation) edema**: protein deficiency — hypoalbuminemia; kwashiorkor in children.
s3072 **Inflammatory edema**: membranogenic, osmotic and oncotic factors (see topic 6).
s3073 **Allergic edema** (Quincke's edema, urticaria): a sharp increase in permeability under the action of histamine and other mediators.
s3074 **Toxic edema** (toxic pulmonary edema, edema after a snakebite): direct damage to the capillary endothelium.
s3075 Consequences of edema
s3076 Edema mechanically compresses tissues and impairs microcirculation and oxygen diffusion (the diffusion distance increases) — hypoxia, dystrophy and susceptibility to infection result; if prolonged, connective tissue overgrowth (sclerosis) develops. At the same time, inflammatory edema also has a protective role: it dilutes toxins and walls off the focus.

s3089 **Expected results and analysis:** the weight of the frog given the hypertonic solution increases noticeably and its lymph sac and legs swell; the weight of the control frog hardly changes. Accumulation of an osmotically active substance — sodium — in the tissue (lymph sac) raises osmotic pressure, and water moves along the osmotic gradient from the vessels and the external medium into the tissue. This confirms the role of the **osmotic factor** in edema formation. By performing the same experiment with a protein solution (e.g. gelatin), the **colloid-osmotic (oncotic)** factor can also be demonstrated.
s3090 The osmotic factor is used clinically in two ways: sodium retention aggravates edema (which is why table salt is restricted in cardiac and renal edema), while osmotically active agents (mannitol, hypertonic sodium chloride) are used in cerebral edema to draw water from the tissue into the vessels and reduce the edema.

s3180 A 1-year-old child has had liquid stools 10–12 times a day and vomiting for 2 days. The mother gave the child only boiled water to drink. On examination: the child is lethargic, not thirsty, the skin fold returns slowly, the fontanelle is sunken, muscle cramps are observed. Plasma sodium is 124 mmol/l.
s3181 What water–electrolyte disorder has developed? Explain its pathogenesis. Why is there no thirst?
s3182 Hypoosmolar (salt-deficit) dehydration. Water and sodium were lost with diarrhea and vomiting; because the losses were replaced only with plain water, sodium became more deficient than water — hyponatremia and plasma hypoosmolarity developed. Water moves along the osmotic gradient into the cells: extracellular fluid (and circulating blood volume) decreases further, and the cells, especially neurons, swell — lethargy and convulsions. Thirst is mainly triggered by a rise in plasma osmolarity; here osmolarity has fallen, so there is no thirst. Treatment — rehydration with saline solutions (oral rehydration solution or intravenous isotonic solutions).
s3183 3 weeks after tonsillitis, an 8-year-old child developed edema of the face and eyelids (especially in the morning), reduced urine output and “meat-washings”-colored urine. Arterial pressure 140/90 mm Hg. Urine: protein 1.2 g/l, many erythrocytes. Blood albumin is normal.
s3184 What type of edema is this? What are its leading factors? How does it differ from nephrotic edema?
s3185 Nephritic edema in acute glomerulonephritis. Leading factors: sodium and water retention due to reduced glomerular filtration (osmotic and neurohumoral factor — RAAS activation), as well as increased capillary permeability (membranogenic factor). Fluid retention has also led to arterial hypertension. Difference from nephrotic edema: proteinuria is moderate and blood albumin is normal, i.e. the oncotic factor is not leading; hematuria and hypertension are present. In nephrotic edema, by contrast, daily proteinuria exceeds 3.5 g, hypoalbuminemia is marked and the edema quickly progresses to anasarca.
s3186 A 55-year-old patient has chronic heart failure. Edema in the legs increases towards evening and decreases somewhat in the morning. The neck veins are distended and the liver is enlarged. s3187 Explain the pathogenesis of the edema. What are the changes after taking furosemide related to?
s3188 Cardiac edema: in right ventricular failure venous pressure rises and capillary hydrostatic pressure increases (leading factor). Reduced cardiac output lowers renal perfusion, RAAS is activated, and aldosterone and ADH secretion increase — sodium and water are retained, circulating blood volume increases and venous pressure rises further (a vicious circle). Therefore, during diuretic treatment potassium must be monitored and, if needed, replaced, or potassium-sparing diuretics added.
