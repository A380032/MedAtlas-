s4616 Modeling two-stage carcinogenesis (analysis using a scheme)
s4617 A small dose of a carcinogen is applied once to mouse skin (initiator) — no tumor develops.
s4618 Then a promoter (not itself carcinogenic, croton oil or phorbol esters) is applied to the same area for a long time — papillomas and then cancer develop.
s4619 If the promoter is applied **before** the initiator, or only the promoter is used, no tumor develops.
s4620 **Analysis:** initiation is an irreversible genetic change (DNA mutation); promotion is a process that stimulates the proliferation of initiated cells, requires prolonged and repeated exposure and is reversible up to a certain point. This experiment proves the multistage nature of carcinogenesis and explains why quitting smoking or treating chronic inflammation reduces the risk of tumors.

s4730 A 62-year-old man has smoked a pack of cigarettes a day for 40 years. In recent months: cough, hemoptysis, 8 kg weight loss, weakness. Blood sodium 124 mmol/l, urine osmolarity high. Bronchoscopy revealed small cell lung cancer.
s4731 Which carcinogenic factor is leading in the development of the tumor? What is the mechanism of the hyponatremia? What is the weight loss due to?
s4732 The leading factor is the chemical carcinogens in tobacco smoke (benzo(a)pyrene and other polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, nitrosamines). After metabolic activation in the liver and lungs, they cause mutations in the DNA of the bronchial epithelium (TP53, KRAS and others) — initiation; many years of repeated exposure and chronic inflammation provide promotion and progression. The hyponatremia is the paraneoplastic syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion: the tumor cells produce ectopic ADH, excess water is retained by the kidneys, the plasma is diluted and the urine is concentrated. The weight loss is tumor cachexia: anorexia and increased proteolysis and lipolysis under the action of TNF-alpha, IL-1 and IL-6, and the metabolic “trap” of the tumor.
s4733 A 35-year-old woman's mother died of breast cancer at the age of 42, and her maternal aunt died of ovarian cancer. Genetic testing found a mutation in the BRCA1 gene, but so far there is no tumor.
s4734 To which group of genes does BRCA1 belong? Why is there no tumor yet despite the mutation?
s4735 BRCA1 is a tumor suppressor gene (involved in repairing DNA double-strand breaks). In a hereditary mutation, one allele is defective from birth in all cells (the “first hit”). For a tumor to develop, the second allele must also be damaged somatically in a particular cell (the “second hit”) — Knudson's hypothesis. Therefore the tumor does not develop immediately, but it develops much earlier and with a much higher probability than in the general population. Such patients are advised to undergo regular screening (MRI, mammography) and preventive measures.
s4736 A 55-year-old patient has had chronic hepatitis B for 20 years, with liver cirrhosis. The latest examination found a sharp rise in blood alpha-fetoprotein and a focus typical of a tumor in the liver.
s4737 By what mechanisms has the tumor developed? To which type of tumor atypia does the rise in alpha-fetoprotein belong?
s4738 Hepatocellular carcinoma. Hepatitis B virus DNA integrates into the hepatocyte genome and causes insertional mutagenesis; the viral protein HBx blocks p53 and stimulates cell growth (viral carcinogenesis). In addition, chronic inflammation, cycles of necrosis and regeneration, and cirrhosis create conditions for the accumulation of mutations through repeated cell division (promotion). Alpha-fetoprotein is an embryonic protein; its renewed synthesis by tumor cells is a manifestation of antigenic (biochemical) atypia and is used as a tumor marker.

s4776 Neutrophils exist in three “pools” in the body: the **bone marrow pool** (maturing and reserve cells — the largest), the **circulating pool** (free in the bloodstream — measured in the blood test) and the **marginal (wall) pool** (close to the vessel wall, not measured in the blood test). Neutrophils circulate in the blood for only 6–10 hours and then move into the tissues.
s4777 Assessing the leukocyte differential only in percentages can lead to a wrong conclusion. For example, in leukopenia the percentage of lymphocytes may be increased while their absolute count is normal — this is **relative lymphocytosis**. Always calculate the absolute count: absolute count = total leukocyte count × percentage / 100.
s4778 Causes of eosinophilia — “**PAD**”: **P**arasites (helminths), **A**llergy (asthma, pollinosis, drugs), **D**ermatoses. Lymphocytosis — “**VTP**”: **V**iruses, **T**uberculosis, **P**ertussis (whooping cough).
s4779 **Disappearance of eosinophils (aneosinophilia)** is seen in acute infection and stress (effect of glucocorticoids), and their reappearance is a sign of recovery (the “dawn of recovery”). Prolonged absence of eosinophils and lymphopenia in severe pneumonia or sepsis are signs of a poor prognosis.
s4780 1. Hematopoiesis and leukopoiesis
s4781 **Hematopoiesis** — the formation of blood cells. In adults it takes place mainly in the red bone marrow of the flat bones and the epiphyses of the long bones. All blood cells arise from a **pluripotent (stem) cell**, which gives rise to progenitor cells of the myeloid and lymphoid lineages.
s4782 The main factors regulating leukopoiesis are the **colony-stimulating factors**: granulocyte (G-CSF), granulocyte-macrophage (GM-CSF) and macrophage (M-CSF), as well as interleukins (IL-3 — multipotential, IL-5 — for eosinophils, IL-7 — for lymphocytes).
