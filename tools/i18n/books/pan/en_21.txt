s2757 **By location: arterial** (coronary, cerebral, femoral and mesenteric arteries — usually over an atherosclerotic plaque, white or mixed, mural — leads to infarction), **venous (phlebothrombosis)** — most often in the **deep veins of the leg** (the source of pulmonary embolism), red, occlusive; in an inflamed vein — **thrombophlebitis**; **in the cardiac chambers** — **mural thrombi** (in myocardial infarction, aneurysm), thrombi in the auricles in atrial fibrillation, a free-lying **ball thrombus** in the atrium, **vegetations** on the valves (infective endocarditis) and a **propagating thrombus** (growing along the vessel toward the heart); capillary thrombosis.
s2758 **Special types: dilatation thrombus** — in aneurysms and varicose vessels; **marantic (stasis) thrombus** — in debilitated, cachectic elderly patients (because of slowed blood flow, most often in the dural venous sinuses and leg veins); **tumor thrombus** — when a tumor grows through the vessel wall and spreads along the vessel (in the renal vein in renal cell carcinoma); **septic thrombus** — a thrombus containing microbes (risk of purulent dissolution).

s2763 Outcomes of a thrombus
s2764 Favorable outcomes:
s2765 **aseptic autolysis (dissolution)** — by the fibrinolytic system (plasmin), especially in small and fresh thrombi; **thrombolytic therapy** (alteplase, streptokinase) is based on this;
s2766 **organization** — **granulation tissue** grows into the thrombotic mass from the wall and replaces it with connective tissue (beginning on days 5–6);
s2767 **canalization and vascularization** — clefts and new endothelium-lined channels form in the organized thrombus, and blood flow is partially restored (**recanalization**);
s2768 **calcification (petrification)** — in veins — **phleboliths** (vein stones).
s2769 Unfavorable outcomes:
s2770 **occlusion** of the vessel — in an artery, **infarction** or **gangrene**; in a vein, **venous congestion and edema** in the distal area;
s2771 **thromboembolism** — detachment of the thrombus or a fragment of it and its transport with the blood stream;
s2772 **septic (purulent) dissolution** — when the thrombus is infected with microbes; **thromboembolic sepsis**, purulent metastases;
s2773 **propagation** of the thrombus — its extension along the vessel.

s2778 DIC
s2779 **Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)** is not an independent disease but a **complication** of many severe conditions: numerous **microthrombi** (hyaline and fibrin thrombi) form in microcirculatory vessels, as a result of which platelets and coagulation factors are **consumed** (**consumptive coagulopathy**) and fibrinolysis is activated — **bleeding** develops. Therefore DIC is also called a “**thrombohemorrhagic syndrome**”.
s2780 **Causes:** obstetric complications (premature placental separation, amniotic fluid embolism, a dead fetus), **sepsis** (especially gram-negative, meningococcemia), **shock**, extensive trauma and burns, malignant tumors (cancer of the lung, pancreas, prostate, acute promyelocytic leukemia), hemolytic transfusion reactions, snake venom.
s2781 **Stages:** 1) hypercoagulation; 2) consumptive coagulopathy; 3) **activation of fibrinolysis and hypocoagulation** — bleeding; 4) recovery (or death).
s2782 **Morphology:** microthrombi in the microvessels of the kidneys, lungs, brain, liver, heart and adrenal glands; numerous diapedetic **hemorrhages** (petechiae, ecchymoses), small foci of **necrosis** and **infarcts**; **symmetric necrosis** of the renal cortex, pituitary necrosis (**Sheehan syndrome** — postpartum), bilateral hemorrhage into the adrenal glands (**Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome** — in meningococcemia). The result is **multiple organ failure**.
s2783 7.9. Embolism
s2784 **Embolism** (Greek emballein — to throw in) is the circulation in blood or lymph of **particles (emboli)** not normally found there and the **occlusion** of vessels by them. Emboli are solid, liquid or gaseous.
s2785 By the direction of movement of the embolus:
s2786 **orthograde** — with the blood flow (the most common): from the systemic veins and the right heart — into the **pulmonary artery** system; from the left heart and aorta — into the **arteries of the systemic circulation** (brain, kidney, spleen, intestine, legs); from the intestinal veins — into the **portal vein** system (to the liver);
s2787 **retrograde** — **against** the blood flow: a heavy embolus (for example, in the inferior vena cava system) descends under its own weight (into the hepatic and renal veins);
s2788 **paradoxical** — an embolus from the systemic veins bypasses the lungs and passes directly into the **arteries** of the systemic circulation through a **septal defect of the heart** (patent foramen ovale, ventricular septal defect) (for example, a leg vein thrombus occludes a cerebral artery).
s2789 Types of embolism
s2790 **1. Thromboembolism** — the most common embolism (about 95–99% of emboli): the embolus is a detached thrombus or a fragment of it.
s2791 **Pulmonary thromboembolism (PE)** is one of the most important causes of death in hospitalized patients. Its source in **95% of cases is thrombosis of the deep veins of the leg** (popliteal, femoral, iliac veins). The consequences depend on the size of the embolus:
s2792 **large emboli** — impact in the **trunk** of the pulmonary artery or at the bifurcation of its main branches (**“saddle” embolus**) — **sudden death** (acute right ventricular failure, “acute cor pulmonale”, cardiogenic shock); the **pulmocoronary reflex** (coronary artery spasm) is also important in the mechanism of death;
s2793 **medium-sized emboli** — in the medium branches of the pulmonary artery; if the blood supply from the bronchial arteries is adequate, infarction does not develop, only hemorrhage; against a background of **left ventricular failure** (venous congestion) — **hemorrhagic infarction**;
s2794 **multiple small emboli** — if recurrent, **pulmonary hypertension** and **chronic cor pulmonale**. **Systemic thromboembolism** — the source is mainly the **left heart** (mural thrombi in myocardial infarction — 2/3 of cases, left auricular thrombi in atrial fibrillation, left ventricular aneurysm, valvular vegetations — endocarditis), and also thrombi over atherosclerotic plaques in the aorta. The emboli impact in the arteries of the **brain** (ischemic stroke), **legs** (gangrene), **kidney, spleen and intestine** and cause **infarcts**.

s2801 **2. Fat embolism** — entry of fat droplets into vessels: **fractures of long bones** (yellow marrow fat), extensive crushing of subcutaneous fatty tissue, burns, erroneous intravascular injection of oily drugs. Fat droplets first occlude the **pulmonary capillaries**; if numerous, they pass through the lungs and reach the capillaries of the **brain and kidneys**. **Fat embolism syndrome** (1–3 days after injury): pulmonary insufficiency, neurological signs (numerous petechiae in the brain), anemia, thrombocytopenia, petechial skin rash. Fat is detected with **Sudan III** (on frozen sections).
s2802 **Bone marrow embolism** — fragments of bone marrow (hematopoietic cells and fat cells) in pulmonary vessels in bone fractures and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (rib fractures).

s2807 **3. Air embolism** — entry of **atmospheric air** into the vessels: injury to the neck veins (negative pressure inside them), surgery (cardiac, pulmonary, neurosurgery — in the sitting position), breaches of technique during intravascular injections and infusions, artificial pneumothorax, childbirth. Air bubbles accumulate in the **right heart**, form frothy blood and occlude the pulmonary capillaries. Usually **more than 100 mL** of air is needed to be clinically significant. At autopsy, to detect air embolism, the heart is opened **under water** (air bubbles escape).
s2808 **4. Gas embolism** — formation of bubbles from **dissolved gases** (mainly nitrogen) in the blood when atmospheric pressure falls rapidly — **caisson (decompression) disease**: in divers and caisson workers who surface rapidly and in pilots who climb rapidly to altitude in an unpressurized cabin. Gas bubbles occlude the vessels of joints and muscles (pain — “**the bends**”), the lungs (shortness of breath — “the chokes”), and the capillaries of the brain and spinal cord (paralysis); in the chronic form — **aseptic necrosis** of bones (femoral head). Gas also forms in tissue in gas gangrene.
s2809 **5. Tissue (cellular) embolism** — fragments of tissue or cells: **tumor cell embolism** — the basis of **metastases** (hematogenous and lymphogenous); fragments of tissue destroyed in trauma (brain, liver); **amniotic fluid embolism** — during childbirth, amniotic fluid (desquamated epithelium, lanugo hairs, meconium, fat) enters the maternal blood and occludes the pulmonary vessels — an extremely severe complication (mortality up to 80%): sudden dyspnea, cyanosis, shock, **DIC**.
s2810 **6. Microbial (bacterial) embolism** — clusters of microbes (bacterial emboli) or fragments of a purulently dissolved thrombus in the vascular lumen: in **sepsis**, **metastatic abscesses** in various organs (kidney, myocardium, brain) (for example, **embolic purulent nephritis**), septic infarcts.
s2811 **7. Foreign body embolism** — bullets, shell fragments, catheter fragments, calcium and cholesterol crystals (when an atherosclerotic plaque ruptures — cholesterol embolism).
s2812 **8. Parasite embolism** — echinococcus, ascarids.

s2817 7.10. Infarction
s2818 **Infarction** (Latin infarcire — to stuff) is **vascular (ischemic) necrosis** resulting from sudden cessation of blood supply (**ischemia**). The most common causes of infarction are **arterial thrombosis** (usually over an atherosclerotic plaque) and **embolism**; less often — prolonged **spasm**, external compression of a vessel, complete cessation of venous outflow (for example, torsion of the testis or ovary), intestinal obstruction.
s2819 Factors influencing the development of infarction:
s2820 **type of blood supply** — functional end arteries (heart, brain, kidney, spleen) — infarction develops easily; dual blood supply (lung — pulmonary and bronchial arteries; liver — hepatic artery and portal vein) — infarction is rare;
s2821 **rate of occlusion** — if it develops slowly, collaterals have time to open;
s2822 **sensitivity of the tissue to hypoxia** — neurons are irreversibly injured in 3–4 minutes, cardiomyocytes in 20–30 minutes; fibroblasts are much more resistant;
s2823 oxygen saturation of the blood (anemia, heart failure).
s2824 Types of infarction
s2825 **By shape:** corresponding to the vascular branching of the organ — **wedge-shaped (triangular)** (kidney, spleen, lung — the base faces the organ capsule, the apex faces the hilum; fibrin on the capsule — reactive inflammation), **irregular** (heart, brain, intestine — organs without a trunk-type vascular branching).
s2826 By color and structure:
s2827 **1. White (ischemic) infarct** — whitish-yellow, firm, sharply demarcated; in **solid** organs with a **single** (end-arterial) blood supply: the **spleen, kidney**, less often the heart and brain.
s2828 **2. White infarct with a red (hemorrhagic) rim** — a zone of hemorrhage around a whitish-yellow focus (**demarcation zone**); this zone results from paralytic vasodilation after perifocal spasm and diapedetic hemorrhage. It occurs in the **heart** (myocardial infarction) and **kidney**.
s2829 **3. Red (hemorrhagic) infarct** — the necrotic focus is soaked with blood, dark red and sharply demarcated. Conditions for its development: a **dual blood supply** (lung) against a background of **venous congestion** (left ventricular failure), loose (spongy) tissues; **venous occlusion** (torsion of the testis or ovary); **restoration of blood flow** (reperfusion) in previously ischemic tissue. It occurs in the **lung and intestine**, less often in the brain (hemorrhagic transformation).
s2830 **4. Septic (infected) infarct** — when a fragment of vegetations (microbial embolus) in infective endocarditis or microbes enter the necrotic focus; the infarct turns into an **abscess**.

s2835 Morphology and evolution of infarction
s2836 The main histologic appearance of an infarct is **ischemic coagulative necrosis** (in the brain — **liquefactive necrosis** — “gray softening”, Topic 2). Its evolution:
s2837 **First hours** — no gross changes; detected by histochemical methods (loss of glycogen and dehydrogenases). Histologic signs of necrosis appear **after 4–12 hours**.
s2838 **First days** — **demarcation inflammation** at the border of the focus: hyperemia, hemorrhage, neutrophil infiltration (days 1–3).
s2839 **Days 3–7** — macrophages phagocytose the dead tissue; **granulation tissue** begins to grow in from the periphery.
s2840 **Weeks to months** — the focus undergoes **organization** and is replaced by a **scar** (6–8 weeks in the myocardium). In the brain, necrotic foci are resorbed and a **cyst** forms (surrounded by a glial scar).
s2841 Infarcts in organs:
s2842 **Myocardial infarction** — the most common and most important; mainly in the **left ventricle** and interventricular septum; the cause is atherosclerosis and thrombosis of the coronary arteries. **Subendocardial**, **intramural**, **subepicardial** and **transmural** (involving the full thickness of the wall) infarcts are distinguished. Complications: cardiogenic shock, arrhythmias, acute heart failure (pulmonary edema), **cardiac rupture** (hemopericardium, tamponade — on days 3–7), acute aneurysm, mural thrombus and thromboembolism, fibrinous pericarditis (Dressler syndrome). Outcome — **large-focal cardiosclerosis**, chronic aneurysm.
