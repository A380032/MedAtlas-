s1816 The classic local signs of inflammation:
s1817 **redness** (rubor), **swelling** (tumor), **local heat** (calor), **pain** (dolor) — the Roman scholar **A.C. Celsus** (1st century AD);
s1818 **loss of function** (functio laesa) — added by R. Virchow (19th century).
s1819 These signs are based on vascular reactions: redness and heat — **arterial hyperemia**; swelling — **exudation**; pain — irritation and compression of nerve endings by mediators (bradykinin, prostaglandins) and edema.
s1820 **From history.** The protective role of **phagocytosis** in inflammation was discovered by the Russian scientist **I.I. Mechnikov** (1882; Nobel Prize, 1908). J. Cohnheim described the emigration of leukocytes through the vessel wall. T. Lewis established the effect of histamine on vascular permeability and laid the foundation for the doctrine of **mediators**.
s1821 Causes of inflammation
s1822 **Infections** (bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites and their toxins) — the most common cause;
s1823 **tissue necrosis** — ischemia (infarction), trauma, physical and chemical agents (burns, frostbite, radiation, acids, alkalis);
s1824 **foreign bodies** — dust particles, suture material, crystals (urates);
s1825 **immune reactions** (hypersensitivity) — allergy, autoimmune diseases.

s1840 5.2. Phases of inflammation
s1841 Inflammation consists of three closely interrelated phases:
s1842 **Alteration (damage)** — the initial phase: degeneration and necrosis of cells and intercellular substance; in this phase **mediators** are released and initiate inflammation.
s1843 **Exudation** — the reaction of the microcirculatory bed: increased vascular permeability, escape of the fluid part of the blood and of blood cells from the vessels (formation of **exudate**), phagocytosis.
s1844 **Proliferation (productive phase)** — proliferation of cells in the focus of inflammation (macrophages, fibroblasts, endothelium), formation of granulation tissue and restoration of the damaged tissue (repair).

s1899 5.4. Vascular reactions in acute inflammation
s1900 1. Changes in blood flow and vessel caliber:
s1901 **brief arteriolar spasm** (a few seconds, neuroreflex);
s1902 **arterial hyperemia** — arterioles dilate (histamine, NO), new capillaries open; **redness and heat**;
s1903 **venous hyperemia and slowing of blood flow** — fluid escapes because of increased permeability and the blood thickens; red cells accumulate (**stasis**); leukocytes line up along the vessel wall (**margination**). **2. Increased vascular permeability** is the most important feature of acute inflammation. Mechanisms:
s1904 **contraction of endothelial cells** — gaps appear between endothelial cells in venules; under the action of histamine, bradykinin and leukotrienes it develops **rapidly** (in 15–30 minutes) and is short-lived — the **most common** mechanism;
s1905 **direct injury to the endothelium** — burns, toxins: the endothelium undergoes necrosis and detaches; it begins immediately and lasts a long time;
s1906 **leukocyte-mediated injury** — enzymes and ROS of activated leukocytes damage the endothelium;
s1907 increased **transcytosis** (under the action of VEGF), the “leaky” wall of newly formed capillaries. **3. Reaction of lymphatic vessels** — lymph flow increases; secondary inflammation of lymphatic vessels (**lymphangitis**) and reactive inflammation of lymph nodes (**lymphadenitis**) may develop (for example, red streaks on the forearm and enlarged axillary lymph nodes after a hand wound).

s1932 5.5. Leukocyte reactions in acute inflammation
s1933 The main cells of acute inflammation are **neutrophils** (in the first 6–24 hours); later (from 24–48 hours) they are replaced by **monocytes-macrophages**. Neutrophils are numerous and respond quickly but live briefly (undergo apoptosis); macrophages live long and can proliferate. There are some exceptions: in viral infections lymphocytes arrive first, and in allergic reactions eosinophils predominate.
s1934 Escape of leukocytes from vessels (extravasation)
s1935 **Margination** — when blood flow slows, leukocytes move from the center of the vessel to the periphery (to the endothelial surface).
s1936 **Rolling** — leukocytes “roll” along the endothelium: endothelial **selectins** (E-selectin, P-selectin) bind **weakly** to carbohydrate ligands on the surface of leukocytes. Selectin expression is increased by histamine, TNF and IL-1.
s1937 **Firm adhesion** — **integrins** on the surface of leukocytes (LFA-1, VLA-4) are activated by chemokines and bind firmly to endothelial ligands (**ICAM-1, VCAM-1**).
s1938 **Transmigration (diapedesis)** — leukocytes pass mainly in **postcapillary venules** between endothelial cells (with the help of **PECAM-1 / CD31** molecules) and pierce the basement membrane with the help of collagenases.
s1939 **Chemotaxis** — movement of leukocytes toward increasing concentrations of **chemotactic factors** — to the focus of injury. Exogenous chemoattractants are bacterial peptides (N-formylmethionine); endogenous ones are **C5a, LTB~4~, IL-8** and other chemokines.
s1940 In children with **hereditary defects of leukocyte adhesion** (LAD-1 — defect of the beta-2 chain of integrins; LAD-2 — defect of the selectin ligand), leukocytes cannot leave the vessels: there are very many leukocytes in the blood, but **no pus forms** in the focus of inflammation; recurrent severe bacterial infections and delayed separation of the umbilical cord stump are observed.
s1941 Phagocytosis
s1942 **Phagocytosis** is the engulfment and digestion of foreign particles, microbes and tissue debris by special cells (phagocytes). I.I. Mechnikov divided phagocytes into **microphages** (neutrophils) and **macrophages**. Stages of phagocytosis:
s1943 **Recognition and attachment** — phagocyte receptors recognize microbes directly (mannose receptors, “scavenger” receptors, Toll-like receptors) or through **opsonins**. The main opsonins are **IgG** (via the Fc receptor), the **C3b** fragment of complement and mannose-binding lectin.
s1944 **Engulfment** — the phagocyte surrounds the microbe with pseudopodia, and a **phagosome** forms; it fuses with a lysosome to become a **phagolysosome**.
s1945 **Killing and degradation:** – **the oxygen-dependent mechanism** (the most effective) — the “**respiratory burst**”: NADPH oxidase (phagocyte oxidase) produces superoxide and H2O2; **myeloperoxidase**, in the presence of chloride ions, converts H2O2 into the potent bactericidal **hypochlorite (HOCl^−^)**; – **nitric oxide** (in macrophages) and peroxynitrite; – **the oxygen-independent mechanism** — lysosomal enzymes (lysozyme, elastase), defensins, cathelicidins, lactoferrin, major basic protein of eosinophils. 4. **Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)** — neutrophils form a net of chromatin and antimicrobial proteins that traps microbes (with neutrophil death). **Complete phagocytosis** — the engulfed microbe is killed and digested. **Incomplete phagocytosis (endocytobiosis)** — the microbe survives and even multiplies inside the phagocyte; this is characteristic of tubercle mycobacteria, gonococci, leishmaniae and the causative agent of leprosy. In such cases the phagocyte becomes a “vehicle” that spreads infection through the organism.
s1946 **Chronic granulomatous disease** — a hereditary defect of NADPH oxidase (most often X-linked): phagocytes engulf microbes, but because there is no “respiratory burst” they cannot kill catalase-positive microbes (staphylococcus, Aspergillus) — recurrent severe infections and granulomas. **Chédiak–Higashi syndrome** — impaired fusion of phagosomes and lysosomes (giant granules), albinism and infections.
s1947 5.6. General (systemic) manifestations of inflammation
s1948 Local inflammation affects the whole organism — the **acute phase reaction** (systemic inflammatory response). It develops under the action of cytokines (**TNF, IL-1, IL-6**) and prostaglandins:
s1949 **fever** — cytokines (endogenous pyrogens) increase PGE2 synthesis in the hypothalamus and raise the “set point” of the thermoregulatory center;
s1950 **acute phase proteins** — the liver increases synthesis of **C-reactive protein (CRP)**, fibrinogen, serum amyloid A (SAA) and hepcidin; fibrinogen makes red cells stick together in “stacks” (rouleaux) — the **ESR increases**; in chronic inflammation SAA leads to **AA amyloidosis**, and hepcidin to **anemia**;
s1951 **leukocytosis** — leukocytes increase in the blood (neutrophils in bacterial infections, a left shift — young forms); **lymphocytosis** in viral infections, **eosinophilia** in allergy and parasitic diseases; in some infections (typhoid fever, some viruses) — **leukopenia**;
s1952 tachycardia, changes in blood pressure, chills, loss of appetite, drowsiness, weakness;
s1953 in severe bacterial infection — **sepsis**: excessive release of cytokines leads to **septic shock**, DIC and multiple organ failure (Topic 7).

s1987 Serous inflammation
s1988 The exudate is a **serous fluid**: 2–5% protein (mainly albumins), a few neutrophils, desquamated epithelial or mesothelial cells. The exudate accumulates in **serous cavities** (serous pleurisy, pericarditis, peritonitis, arthritis), **mucous membranes** (serous catarrh), the **skin** (blisters in burns and viral infections — fluid accumulates beneath or within the epidermis) and the stroma of **parenchymal organs** (serous interstitial inflammation — liver, myocardium, kidney).
s1989 **The outcome** is usually **favorable**: the exudate is resorbed. But sometimes it is dangerous because of its location: cardiac tamponade in serous pericarditis; brain edema in serous inflammation of the meninges (serous meningitis); in the larynx — edema and asphyxia. If prolonged in serous cavities — adhesions; in parenchymal organs — diffuse sclerosis.
