s1265 **“Fatty degeneration of the myocardium”** (Sudan III). Small orange fat droplets inside cardiomyocytes, focally distributed; the cross-striation is lost. Conclusion: parenchymal fatty degeneration.
s1266 **“Mucoid swelling”** (toluidine blue; for example, a heart valve in rheumatic fever). The ground substance is swollen, and the collagen fibers are swollen and split; the tissue stains violet-red (metachromasia). Conclusion: mucoid swelling.
s1267 **“Hyalinosis of the arterioles of the kidney, spleen or brain”** (hematoxylin and eosin). The arteriolar walls are thickened, with pale pink, homogeneous hyaline masses beneath the endothelium; the lumen is narrowed or occluded; the surrounding tissue is atrophic and sclerotic. Conclusion: vascular hyalinosis (hypertension or diabetes).

s1346 A 48-year-old man has consumed alcohol regularly for 20 years. On examination the liver extends 5 cm below the costal margin; it is smooth and painless. s1349 A 5-year-old child was hospitalized with diphtheria. On the 12th day of the disease he died of sudden cardiac arrest. At autopsy the heart is slightly enlarged, its cavities are dilated, and the myocardium is flabby and clay-yellow; from the endocardial side, yellowish-white transverse stripes are seen in the papillary muscles.
s1350 1) What is the change in the heart called? 2) Explain its mechanism. 3) What causes the stripes, and with what stain are they confirmed?
s1351 1) Parenchymal fatty degeneration of the myocardium — “tiger heart” (in the setting of diphtheritic myocarditis). 2) Diphtheria exotoxin stops protein synthesis in cardiomyocytes and damages mitochondria: fatty acid oxidation decreases and the lipoprotein structures of membranes break down (decomposition) — small fat droplets appear in the cytoplasm. With Sudan III the fat inside cardiomyocytes stains orange.

s1401 **Hemosiderosis** — iron accumulates mainly in **macrophages** and usually **does not damage** the tissue. **Hemochromatosis** — iron accumulates in **parenchymal cells** and **damages** them: cirrhosis, diabetes, cardiomyopathy. The classic triad: **cirrhosis + diabetes + bronze skin**.
s1402 Determining the type of jaundice by laboratory tests: **“Hemolysis — indirect, Obstruction — direct, Liver — both”**. Colorless (pale) stools, beer-colored urine and itching skin mean that the bile duct is **obstructed** (obstructive jaundice).
s1403 **Physiological jaundice** in newborns (beginning on days 2–3 and resolving in 7–10 days) is due to the immaturity of the liver enzyme glucuronyltransferase. However, if unconjugated bilirubin is very high (hemolytic disease of the newborn), it crosses the blood–brain barrier, stains the subcortical nuclei of the brain yellow and damages them — **kernicterus (nuclear jaundice)**: severe neurological complications (Topic 11).
s1404 Distinguishing the three brown pigments is the task most often given in questions on microscopic slides: **Hemosiderin** — golden-brown, coarsely granular; **Perls-positive (blue)**; in hemorrhage and hemolysis. **Lipofuscin** — yellowish-brown, finely granular, **around the nucleus**; Perls-negative, Sudan-positive; in aging and atrophy. **Melanin** — brown-black; Perls-negative, Fontana–Masson-positive, bleached by hydrogen peroxide; in the skin, eye and melanocytic tumors.
s1405 The main criterion for distinguishing the types of calcification is **blood calcium** and the **state of the tissue**: **dystrophic** — normal calcium, damaged tissue (local); **metastatic** — hypercalcemia, healthy tissue (widespread; lung, stomach, kidney); **metabolic** — normal calcium, and the tissue is not damaged either.

s1416 **Pigments** (Latin pigmentum — paint) are substances that have their own color. They are **exogenous** (entering from outside — coal dust, tattoo ink) and **endogenous** (formed in the organism). s1417 Disorders of pigment metabolism appear in three forms: an **increase** in pigment, a **decrease (loss)** of pigment, and its **appearance** in a site where it is not normally found.
s1418 4.2. Hemoglobinogenic pigments
s1419 Hemoglobinogenic pigments are formed as a result of **physiological or pathologic breakdown of hemoglobin**. Red blood cells are normally broken down (after living about 120 days) mainly in macrophages of the spleen, liver and bone marrow. When hemoglobin is broken down, **heme** and **globin** separate; **iron** is released from heme and reused, and **bilirubin** is formed from the iron-free porphyrin ring.
