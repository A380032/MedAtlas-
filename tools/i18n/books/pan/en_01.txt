s0018 Remember
s0019 In pathological anatomy, changes are studied at **four levels**: **the organism and the organ** (gross), **the tissue and the cell** (microscopic — light microscope), **intracellular structures** (ultrastructural — electron microscope), and **molecules** (molecular pathology). For example, in sickle cell anemia the change begins at the molecular level: glutamic acid at position 6 of the hemoglobin beta chain is replaced by valine.
s0020 **Etiology** answers the question “**why?**”, **pathogenesis** — “**by what mechanism?**”, and **morphogenesis** — “**through what structural changes?**”. A single etiologic agent can cause different diseases through different pathogenetic pathways (for example, streptococcus — tonsillitis, rheumatic fever, glomerulonephritis), whereas different agents can cause the same morphologic change (for example, fatty liver — alcohol, obesity, diabetes, hypoxia).
s0021 Memory aid
s0022 **“Labile — Lining, Stable — liver, Permanent — Pump and brain”**: labile tissues border the external environment (skin, mucous membranes) and are constantly renewed; permanent tissues (heart, brain) cannot be restored once lost — which is why myocardial infarction and stroke heal with a scar.
s0023 Caution
s0024 In the Uzbek curriculum title, the expression “ruhiy zo‘riqish” is a rendering of the English word **stress**. In pathology, **cellular stress** does not mean a psychological state but any excessive load or injurious influence on the cell (hypoxia, heat, toxin, hormonal signal, etc.). The cell responds by synthesizing “stress proteins” (heat shock proteins, chaperones) and by adaptive reactions.
s0025 Causes of cell injury — **“GAIN CHIP”**: **G**enetic, **A**ging, **I**mmune, **N**utritional, **C**hemical, **H**ypoxia, **I**nfection, **P**hysical.
s0026 In **hypertrophy** the cells **enlarge**; in **hyperplasia** the **number of cells increases**. In organs made of nondividing cells (heart, skeletal muscle), an increased load produces only **hypertrophy**. In cells capable of division (uterus, prostate, thyroid gland), hypertrophy and hyperplasia often occur **together**.
s0027 The normal weight of the heart is about **250–350 g**, and the thickness of the left ventricular wall is **0.8–1.2 cm**. In severe hypertrophy the weight of the heart can increase **2–3 times or more**; a heart weighing over 1000 g is called a “**bovine heart**” (cor bovinum).
s0028 **True hypertrophy** must be distinguished from **false hypertrophy**. In false hypertrophy the organ enlarges not because of the parenchyma but because of the growth of fat, connective tissue or bone, while the parenchyma undergoes atrophy and function **declines** (for example, fatty infiltration of the heart, “pseudohypertrophy” of the calf muscles in Duchenne muscular dystrophy). Congenital enlargement of organs (gigantism) is not considered hypertrophy either.
s0029 Atrophy must be distinguished from **congenital malformations**: **agenesis** — the organ or its primordium is completely absent; **aplasia** — the organ remains rudimentary and lacks its typical structure; **hypoplasia** — the organ is small and underdeveloped from birth. Atrophy, in contrast, is the **subsequent** shrinkage of a **normally developed** organ.
s0030 In appearance, the surface of an atrophic organ is **smooth** (for example, in most atrophies of the liver and kidney) or **granular** (for example, the primary contracted kidney). Atrophy can be a **reversible** process: if the cause is removed early, the structure and function of the organ are restored. In severe atrophy (especially when sclerosis is added), reversal is not possible.
s0031 To remember the direction of metaplasia: **“Bronchus goes Squamous, Barrett goes Glandular”**. The airways put on a **shield** (squamous epithelium) against smoke, while the esophagus, like the stomach and intestine, produces **glands** (mucus) against acid. Both pave the way to cancer: in the bronchus — squamous cell carcinoma, in the esophagus — adenocarcinoma.
s0032 Some textbooks (M.S. Abdullakhodjaeva) discuss dysplasia in the chapter on adaptive processes, whereas the Robbins textbook places it in the chapter on **tumors** (neoplasia). Indeed, dysplasia is **not an adaptation**: it is a **precancerous process**. Mild dysplasia can regress if the cause is removed, whereas severe dysplasia often progresses to invasive cancer. Dysplasia is discussed in detail in Topic 9.
s0033 1.1. Content and tasks of pathological anatomy
s0034 **Pathology** (Greek pathos — suffering, disease; logos — study) is the science that studies the causes and mechanisms of disease and the resulting structural and functional changes in cells, tissues and organs. **Pathological anatomy** is the anatomy of the diseased organism: it studies the material (structural) basis of disease and serves as a bridge between theoretical medicine and the clinic.
s0035 Pathological anatomy consists of two parts:
s0036 **General pathological anatomy** — the general pathological processes that occur in any disease: cell injury and death, degenerations, circulatory disorders, inflammation, immunopathological processes, regeneration, adaptation and tumors. This manual is devoted to this part.
s0037 **Systemic (special) pathological anatomy** — the morphology of individual diseases (nosological entities): atherosclerosis, pneumonia, hepatitis, glomerulonephritis and others. **Tasks of pathological anatomy** (after M.S. Abdullakhodjaeva):
s0038 To study the morphologic changes that occur in organs, tissues, cells and intracellular structures in diseases and their complications.
s0039 To establish the causes and conditions of the development of disease — its **etiology**.
s0040 To study the mechanism of development of disease — its **pathogenesis**.
s0041 To establish the sequence in which structural changes appear during the disease — its **morphogenesis**.
s0042 To study the structural basis of protective, compensatory and adaptive processes.
s0043 To analyze changes in the picture of a disease under the influence of treatment or changing living conditions — **pathomorphosis**.
s0044 Research methods of pathological anatomy
s0045 **1. Autopsy** (Greek autopsia — seeing with one's own eyes; synonyms: necropsy, postmortem examination, dissection of the body) — examination of the dead body by dissection to identify the structural changes caused by disease. The significance of autopsy:
s0046 comparison of the clinical and pathological diagnoses and detection of diagnostic and treatment errors (clinicopathological conferences);
s0047 determination of the cause of death and its thanatogenesis (mechanism of death);
s0048 study of new and rare diseases and of pathomorphosis;
s0049 timely detection of infectious diseases and prevention of epidemics;
s0050 collection of statistical data on the structure of morbidity and mortality;
s0051 medical education and continuing training of physicians. **2. Biopsy** (Greek bios — life, opsis — vision) — removal of a piece of tissue from a living patient and its microscopic examination for diagnostic purposes. Types of biopsy:

s0080 Today the pathologist is not a “doctor of the dead” but **a treating physician of the living patient**: biopsies and surgical specimens make up most of the work of pathology departments. A cancer diagnosis is not made without histologic examination, and chemotherapy or the extent of surgery is often chosen according to the pathologist's report (tumor type, grade, receptor status, presence or absence of tumor at the margins).
s0081 Brief history
s0082 **Three periods** are distinguished in the development of pathological anatomy:
s0083 **Anatomic (macroscopic) period** — until the middle of the 19th century. **Avicenna (Ibn Sina)** in “The Canon of Medicine” (1020) linked the signs of disease with changes in the organs. The Italian anatomist **G.B. Morgagni**, in his work “On the Seats and Causes of Diseases Investigated by Anatomy” (1761), compared the results of about 700 autopsies with clinical signs — he is regarded as **the founder of pathological anatomy**. M. Bichat (pathology of tissues) and K. Rokitansky (more than 30,000 autopsies, the humoral theory of pathology) were major figures of this period.
s0084 **Microscopic period** — from the middle of the 19th to the middle of the 20th century. The German pathologist **R. Virchow** (1821–1902) created the theory of **cellular pathology** in 1855–1858: “every cell from a cell” (omnis cellula e cellula), and disease is the result of injury to cells. Experimental pathology developed.
s0085 **Ultramicroscopic and molecular period** — from the middle of the 20th century to the present: electron microscopy, histochemistry, immunomorphology, molecular genetics. This period is a direct continuation of Virchow's cellular pathology. In Russia, the first department of pathological anatomy was opened in 1849 at Moscow University (A.I. Polunin). Major representatives of the Moscow school were A.I. Abrikosov, I.V. Davydovsky, A.I. Strukov, A.P. Avtsyn and V.V. Serov; of the St. Petersburg school — M.M. Rudnev and N.N. Anichkov.
s0086 **In Uzbekistan, pathological anatomy** took shape as an independent discipline in 1919 with the opening of the medical faculty at Turkestan State University. The first head of the department was Professor **V.V. Vasilevsky** (1920–1930). **The founder of the Uzbek school of pathologists** is Professor **G.N. Terekhov** (head of the department at Tashkent State Medical Institute in 1930–1966). Later the department was headed by Associate Professor P.J. Tulaganov and Professor **M.S. Abdullakhodjaeva** (1969–1990). Scientists of the department made a major contribution to the study of sprue (chillashir), visceral leishmaniasis, pellagra, brucellosis, ulcerative colitis, hemorrhagic fever and geographic pathology. Departments of pathological anatomy were opened in Samarkand in 1932 (S.F. Tatarenchik) and at the Central Asian Medical Pediatric Institute in 1972 (V.A. Alimov). Today regional pathology bureaus and the Republican Center of Pathological Anatomy operate in the country.
s0087 1.2. General doctrine of disease
s0088 **Disease** is a disruption of normal life activity resulting from damage to the structures of the organism and impairment of its functions under the influence of external and internal injurious (pathogenic) factors. In the words of I.V. Davydovsky, disease is the organism's adaptation, of a particular form and degree, to its living conditions: along with damage, it also displays protective, compensatory and adaptive processes.

s0111 Three stages are distinguished in the development of a disease (D.S. Sarkisov): **1)** formation of the disease (predisease, latent period) — structural changes are balanced by compensatory reactions, and there are no clinical signs; **2)** the full-blown (clinical) period of the disease — compensation fails and signs appear; **3)** recovery or another outcome.
s0112 **Etiologic factors** are divided into two groups: **genetic** (mutations, chromosomal abnormalities, hereditary predisposition) and **acquired** (infection, nutrition, chemical and physical factors). Most common diseases (atherosclerosis, hypertension, diabetes mellitus) are **multifactorial**, resulting from the combined effect of hereditary predisposition and the environment.
s0113 1.3. The cell as the structural unit of health and disease
s0114 According to Virchow's doctrine, the smallest living unit of the organism is the cell, and disease begins with injury to cells. The cell organelles work in connection with one another, and each of them can be a “target” in a particular pathological process.
