s4483 Pathogenesis and morphology:
s4484 **hemolysis → anemia** → hypoxia → **heart failure** and liver damage (decreased protein synthesis — hypoproteinemia) → **generalized edema** (hydrops): subcutaneous edema, ascites, hydrothorax, hydropericardium;
s4485 **extramedullary hematopoiesis** in response to anemia — the liver and spleen enlarge (**hepatosplenomegaly**), with foci of erythropoiesis in them; many young red cells in the blood — **erythroblastosis** (erythroblastosis fetalis);
s4486 the placenta is enlarged, edematous and pale;
s4487 **hemolytic jaundice**: unconjugated bilirubin increases, crosses the blood–brain barrier and stains yellow and damages the **subcortical nuclei of the brain** (basal ganglia, thalamus, cerebellum, brainstem nuclei) — **kernicterus (nuclear jaundice)**: severe neurological sequelae in survivors (CP, deafness, intellectual disability). **Clinical forms:** intrauterine death; **generalized hydrops** (the most severe); **hemolytic jaundice** (risk of kernicterus); the **anemic** form (mild).
s4488 Causes of **nonimmune hydrops**: **cardiovascular defects** and arrhythmias, **chromosomal diseases** (Turner — cystic hygroma, trisomies 21 and 18), fetal anemia (**thalassemia**, **parvovirus B19** — intranuclear inclusions in erythroid precursors), infections, twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome.
s4489 Prevention of Rh hemolytic disease — giving the Rh-negative mother **anti-D immunoglobulin within 72 hours** after the birth of each Rh-positive child (or after abortion, and at the 28th week of pregnancy): it destroys fetal red cells that have entered the maternal blood before sensitization. In severe cases, intrauterine transfusion and, after birth, exchange transfusion and phototherapy are used.

s4496 Sudden infant death syndrome
s4497 **Sudden infant death syndrome** is the **sudden, unexpected** death of a child **under 1 year of age** that remains unexplained even after a complete autopsy, examination of the death scene and review of the clinical history. It occurs most often in infants **2–4 months** old, **during sleep** (at night). **Risk factors:** putting the infant to sleep **prone (on the stomach)**, a soft mattress, overheating, maternal smoking and drug abuse during pregnancy, prematurity, low birth weight, male sex, not breastfeeding, bed sharing with parents. Mechanism — immaturity of the brainstem (respiratory and arousal centers), a defect of the serotonergic system. Nonspecific changes at autopsy: **petechiae** in the pleura, pericardium and thymus, pulmonary edema and congestion, gliosis in the brainstem.
s4498 The “**Back to Sleep**” campaign (putting the infant to sleep only on the back, on a firm surface, with no soft objects nearby) reduced the frequency of this syndrome several-fold.
s4499 11.7. Childhood tumors
s4500 Tumors in children differ from those in adults: **benign tumors and tumor-like lesions** are more common; among malignant tumors, **leukemias**, tumors of the **central nervous system**, **lymphomas** and tumors of the **embryonal (blastoma)** type predominate; carcinomas are rare. Most malignant tumors appear microscopically as **small, round, blue cell** tumors (neuroblastoma, Wilms tumor, retinoblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, lymphoma); they are associated with **genetic** factors and respond well to modern treatment.
s4501 Benign tumors and tumor-like lesions
s4502 **Hamartomas** and **choristomas** (Topic 9).
s4503 **Hemangiomas** — the most common tumor of children; most often in the skin (face, head), of the **capillary** type; most **regress spontaneously**. **Lymphangiomas** — neck, axilla (cystic hygroma).
s4504 **Teratomas** — most often in the **sacrococcygeal** region (**sacrococcygeal teratoma**, more often in girls), and also in the ovary, testis and mediastinum; most are mature (benign), but they may contain immature (malignant) components.

s4509 Malignant tumors
s4510 **Neuroblastoma** — a tumor arising from neural crest cells of the sympathetic nervous system and **adrenal medulla**; the most common **extracranial solid tumor** of early childhood (median age 18 months). In 40% of cases it arises **in the adrenal gland**, the rest along the sympathetic chain (abdomen, mediastinum). **Grossly:** a soft, gray-pink nodule of variable size with foci of hemorrhage, necrosis and **calcification**. **Microscopically: sheets of small, round cells with dark nuclei**, a delicate fibrillar matrix (neuropil) and **Homer Wright pseudorosettes** (cells arranged in a ring around central neuropil); the degree of maturation varies — ganglioneuroblastoma, **ganglioneuroma** (mature, benign). **Urinary catecholamine metabolites** (vanillylmandelic and homovanillic acids) are increased — a diagnostic sign. Prognosis depends on age (good under 1.5 years), stage and **amplification of the MYCN oncogene** (unfavorable); spontaneous regression or maturation is possible in infants.
s4511 **Wilms tumor (nephroblastoma)** — the most common **primary renal tumor** of children (at 2–5 years of age). In some cases it is associated with syndromes (**WAGR** — Wilms tumor, aniridia, genital anomalies, intellectual disability — deletion of the WT1 gene; Denys–Drash and Beckwith–Wiedemann syndromes). **Grossly:** a large, solitary, sharply demarcated nodule, soft and uniform on the cut surface, **gray-yellowish (tan)**, with foci of hemorrhage and cysts. **Microscopically — triphasic structure: blastema** (sheets of small blue cells), an **epithelial** component (embryonic tubules and glomeruli) and **stroma** (fibroblast-like or myxoid, sometimes skeletal muscle). **Anaplasia** (large, hyperchromatic nuclei, atypical mitoses; TP53 mutation) — unfavorable prognosis. Clinically — a large abdominal mass. With modern treatment **more than 90%** of patients are cured.
s4512 **Retinoblastoma** — the most common malignant tumor of the retina; ***RB** gene mutation (“two hits”, Topic 9): familial (40%; often bilateral, early, with a later risk of osteosarcoma) and sporadic forms. Clinically — leukocoria (“cat's eye” — the pupil shines white), strabismus. Microscopically — small blue cells, Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes*.

s4521 11.8. Clinicopathological section
s4522 Determining the cause of perinatal death
s4523 In perinatal death the autopsy is performed together with examination of the **placenta**, because placental changes (chorioamnionitis, infarcts, abruption, umbilical cord anomalies) often indicate the cause of fetal death. The pathological diagnosis states **maternal** (preeclampsia, diabetes, infection), **placental** and **fetal** factors. The time of fetal death is estimated from the degree of **maceration** (skin slippage, softening of tissues — postmortem autolysis in utero).
s4524 Example: “Underlying disease: neonatal respiratory distress syndrome — hyaline membrane disease of the lungs (gestational age 29 weeks, weight 1200 g). Complications: hemorrhage into the lateral ventricles of the brain. Background condition: maternal diabetes mellitus; acute chorioamnionitis.”
s4525 **Neonatal screening** — in all newborns, blood is taken from the heel in the first days after birth to detect **phenylketonuria**, **congenital hypothyroidism**, cystic fibrosis, galactosemia and other treatable hereditary diseases. Early diagnosis and diet or hormone therapy prevent intellectual disability.
s4526 Terms of heredity and genetics
s4527 **Gene** — a sequence of nucleotides in DNA that encodes a particular RNA molecule.
s4528 **Genome** — the totality of genetic information in a cell of an organism.
s4529 **Genotype** — the genetic makeup of an organism (the set of alleles at one or several loci).
s4530 **Phenotype** — the set of external and internal traits of an organism formed under the influence of the genotype and the environment.
s4531 **Allele** — one of the alternative forms of a gene; located at the same locus of homologous chromosomes.
s4532 **Locus** — a region of a chromosome with a particular genetic determinant.
s4533 **Homozygote** — a cell or organism with two identical alleles at a particular locus of homologous chromosomes.
s4534 **Heterozygote** — a cell or organism with two different alleles at a particular locus of homologous chromosomes.
s4535 **Hemizygosity** — presence of a gene in only one copy (for example, genes on the X chromosome in males).
s4536 **Dominance** — expression in a heterozygote of the trait of only one allele.
s4537 **Recessiveness** — expression of the trait of an allele only in the homozygous state.
s4538 **Autosome** — any chromosome that is not a sex chromosome; humans have 22 pairs of autosomes.
s4539 **Autosomal dominant inheritance** — a mode of inheritance in which one mutant allele on an autosome is enough for the trait (disease) to be expressed.
s4540 **Autosomal recessive inheritance** — a mode of inheritance in which the mutant allele must be inherited from both parents for the trait (disease) to be expressed.
s4541 **Holandric inheritance** — Y-linked inheritance (only from father to son).
s4542 **Homologous chromosomes** — paired chromosomes with the same set of genes.
s4543 **Centromere** — the part of a chromosome to which the spindle fiber attaches and which ensures the distribution of chromosomes to the daughter cells.
s4544 **Chromatin** — the complex of DNA and associated proteins in the nucleus; **heterochromatin** — its dense, transcriptionally inactive part.
s4545 **Gamete** — a mature germ cell; **haploid** — a cell with a single set of chromosomes; **somatic cells** — cells of non-germ tissues.
s4546 **Mutation** — a heritable change in genetic information; **mutagenesis** — the process of formation of mutations; **mutagens** — physical, chemical and biological agents that cause mutations.
s4547 **Chromosomal aberration (anomaly)** — the general name of any chromosomal mutation: deletion, translocation, inversion, duplication and others.
s4548 **Deletion** — loss of part of a chromosome; **duplication** — doubling of part of a chromosome.
s4549 **Aneuploidy** — a chromosome set with one or more chromosomes fewer or more than normal (trisomy, monosomy).
s4550 **Amplification** — an increase in the number of copies of a gene (for example, amplification of oncogenes in tumors).
s4551 **Crossing over** — exchange of segments between homologous chromosomes in meiosis.
s4552 **Replication** — doubling of DNA; **transcription** — synthesis of RNA on a DNA template; **translation** — synthesis of a protein polypeptide chain on ribosomes.
s4553 **Genetic code** — the system for recording the sequence of amino acids in a protein by nucleotide triplets (codons) in DNA (RNA); **codon** — a nucleotide triplet that specifies one amino acid or the end of translation.
s4554 **Exon** — the coding part of a gene retained in mature mRNA; **intron** — the noncoding part removed during splicing; **splicing** — the process of removing introns from RNA.
s4555 **Promoter** — the region of DNA to which RNA polymerase binds to begin transcription; **enhancer** — a region of DNA that increases transcription.
s4556 **DNA repair** — correction of a damaged DNA molecule.
s4557 **Reverse transcriptase** — an enzyme that catalyzes DNA synthesis on an RNA template (in retroviruses).
s4558 **Retroviruses** — RNA viruses that encode reverse transcriptase and integrate into the chromosome (provirus).
s4559 **Proto-oncogenes** — normal genes that, as a result of mutation or activation, confer malignant tumor properties on a cell; **oncogenes** — activated genes capable of transforming a cell into a tumor cell.
s4560 **Clone** — a set of genetically identical cells derived asexually from a single ancestral cell.
s4561 **Monoclonal antibodies** — antibodies specific for a single antigen produced by hybridomas; **hybridoma** — a cell formed by fusion of a myeloma cell with an immunized lymphocyte.
s4562 **Microsatellites** — short tandemly repeated sequences in DNA; used in genetic fingerprinting (personal identification) and in tumor diagnosis.
s4563 **Sequencing** — determination of the sequence of monomers in a nucleic acid or protein molecule.
s4564 **Blotting** — a method of transferring DNA, RNA or protein molecules to a membrane after electrophoresis.
s4565 **Vaccine** — an antigenic preparation used to induce immunity against an infection.
s4566 **Vector** — a DNA molecule (plasmid, virus) used to introduce genetic information into a cell.
s4567 **Plasmid** — a circular DNA molecule that replicates independently of the cell chromosome.
s4568 Terms of environmental pathology
s4569 **Adaptation** — adjustment of an organism to particular environmental conditions by means of a set of morphologic, physiological and behavioral features.
s4570 **Allergy** — hypersensitivity of the organism to allergens (chemicals polluting the air and water, food, drugs, plant pollen); one of the most widespread diseases.
s4571 **Benzo(a)pyrene** — a potent carcinogen of the group of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons; found in industrial gases, vehicle exhaust and tobacco smoke.
s4572 **Carcinogenic substances** — chemicals that cause cancer.
s4573 **Dioxins** — among the most toxic substances known; formed during combustion of chlorine-containing substances; they have carcinogenic and teratogenic effects.
s4574 **Suspended dust (particulate matter)** — fine solid particles in the air; they accumulate in the lungs (pneumoconioses) and carry heavy metals and carcinogens.
s4575 **Heavy metals** — metals with a density greater than 4.5 g/cm3 (mercury, lead, cadmium, arsenic and others); they accumulate in the body and damage the nervous system, kidneys and hematopoiesis.
s4576 **Mercury** — a heavy metal; its organic compounds (methylmercury) are especially dangerous; entering the body through fish, they damage the nervous system (Minamata disease).
s4577 **Lead** — a heavy metal; especially dangerous for children and pregnant women: anemia, damage to the nervous system and kidneys, delayed mental development in children.
s4578 **Cadmium** — a heavy metal; damages the kidneys and bones.
