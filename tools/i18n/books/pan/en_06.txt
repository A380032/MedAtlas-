s0716 2.5. Apoptosis
s0717 **Apoptosis** (Greek apoptosis — “falling off of leaves”) is **programmed cell death**: the cell destroys itself by degrading its own DNA and nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins with special intracellular enzymes (caspases). The membrane of the apoptotic cell **remains intact**, and the cell fragments (apoptotic bodies) are rapidly engulfed by macrophages, so **no inflammation develops**. Apoptosis was described as a distinct type of death in 1972 by J. Kerr, A. Wyllie and A. Currie.
s0718 Causes of apoptosis
s0719 **Physiological apoptosis** — elimination of unneeded, aged or dangerous cells:
s0720 programmed death of cells during embryogenesis (implantation, organogenesis, disappearance of the tissue between the fingers);
s0721 involution of hormone-dependent tissues when the hormone decreases (endometrium in the menstrual cycle, ovary in menopause, breast after cessation of lactation);
s0722 maintenance of cell numbers in continuously renewing cell populations (intestinal crypts, lymphocytes);
s0723 elimination of inflammatory cells (neutrophils, lymphocytes) that have completed their task;
s0724 elimination in the thymus of **self-reactive lymphocytes** directed against self antigens (immune tolerance);
s0725 death of virus-infected and tumor cells under the action of cytotoxic T lymphocytes.
s0726 Pathologic apoptosis:
s0727 irreparable **DNA damage** (radiation, chemotherapy drugs) — via p53;
s0728 **accumulation of misfolded proteins** (ER stress) — neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer and Parkinson diseases);
s0729 some **infections** — viral hepatitis (Councilman bodies), HIV infection;
s0730 **atrophy of organs** after duct obstruction (obstruction of the pancreatic, salivary gland or renal ducts).
s0731 Morphology of apoptosis
s0732 **Cell shrinkage** — the cytoplasm becomes dense, and the organelles are tightly packed (but keep their normal structure).
s0733 **Chromatin condensation** — the most characteristic feature: chromatin aggregates densely beneath the nuclear membrane, and the nucleus breaks into fragments (**karyorrhexis**).
s0734 **Membrane blebs** and fragmentation of the cell into membrane-bound **apoptotic bodies** containing cytoplasm, organelles and nuclear fragments.
s0735 **Phagocytosis of apoptotic bodies by macrophages**. In a hematoxylin and eosin–stained slide, an apoptotic cell is a **single** cell separated from its surroundings by a clear space, **round or oval, with deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm** and dense nuclear fragments. Because there is no inflammatory reaction and the process is completed rapidly (within a few hours), apoptosis is difficult to see in histologic slides.

s0740 Mechanisms of apoptosis
s0741 Apoptosis consists of two phases: **initiation** — activation of caspases, and **execution** — breakdown of the cell. **Caspases** are proteases with cysteine in their active site that cleave proteins after aspartic acid (cysteine-aspartic proteases). There are two pathways for initiating apoptosis:
s0742 **1. The intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway** is the main mechanism of apoptosis. The permeability of the outer mitochondrial membrane is regulated by the **BCL2 family of proteins**:
s0743 **anti-apoptotic** proteins — **BCL2**, BCL-XL, MCL1: keep the mitochondrial membrane intact; they are produced in response to growth factors and survival signals;
s0744 **pro-apoptotic** (effector) proteins — **BAX and BAK**: oligomerize and form channels in the mitochondrial membrane;
s0745 **regulated “sensor” proteins of apoptosis** — BIM, BID, BAD, PUMA, NOXA (BH3-only proteins): when stress is sensed (DNA damage, absence of growth factors, misfolded proteins), they activate BAX/BAK and block BCL2. If the cell loses survival signals or is injured, **cytochrome c** leaks into the cytoplasm through BAX/BAK channels, binds the APAF-1 protein to form the **apoptosome** and activates **caspase-9**.
s0746 **2. The extrinsic (death receptor) pathway.** The **death receptors** on the cell surface belong to the TNF receptor family: **Fas (CD95)** and TNFR1. **Fas ligand (FasL)** on the surface of cytotoxic T lymphocytes binds to the Fas receptor and brings together three receptor molecules; their cytoplasmic “death domains” activate **caspase-8** through the adapter protein FADD. This pathway is important in the elimination of self-reactive lymphocytes and in killing by cytotoxic T lymphocytes.
s0747 **3. The execution phase.** Caspase-9 and caspase-8 activate the **executioner caspases (caspase-3 and caspase-6)**. They activate a DNase that cleaves DNA between nucleosomes (into fragments of 180–200 base pairs — a “ladder” pattern on electrophoresis) and degrade the proteins of the nuclear matrix and cytoskeleton.
s0748 **4. Removal of apoptotic cells.** **Phosphatidylserine**, normally located in the inner leaflet of the membrane, flips to the outer leaflet in an apoptotic cell — this is an “**eat me**” signal for macrophages. Apoptotic bodies are rapidly phagocytosed without inflammation.
s0749 Both **decreased** and **increased** apoptosis lead to disease. **Too little apoptosis:** in follicular lymphoma the t(14;18) translocation causes **overproduction of BCL2** — lymphocytes do not die and accumulate; with TP53 mutation, damaged cells are not eliminated — tumors; with defects of Fas or FasL, self-reactive lymphocytes survive — autoimmune diseases. **Too much apoptosis:** neurodegenerative diseases, ischemic injury, viral infections. A drug that blocks BCL2 — **venetoclax** — is used to treat chronic lymphocytic leukemia.

s0779 2.6. Other pathways of cell death and autophagy
s0780 **Necroptosis** — programmed (regulated) necrosis: morphologically it resembles necrosis (cell swelling, membrane rupture, inflammation) but is genetically programmed. It is initiated via TNFR1, is caspase-independent and involves the **RIPK1 and RIPK3 kinases** and the MLKL protein. It is seen in pancreatitis, reperfusion injury, neurodegenerative diseases and some viral infections (a defense against viruses that block caspases).
s0781 **Pyroptosis** — an inflammatory, fever-inducing cell death associated with **inflammasome** activation, caspase-1 and IL-1 production in intracellular microbial infection (Greek pyr — fire).
s0782 **Ferroptosis** — death resulting from iron-dependent lipid peroxidation; it develops when the glutathione system is insufficient.
s0783 **Autophagy** — digestion by the cell of its own organelles and cytoplasm in lysosomes. It is primarily a **survival mechanism** (a source of energy in starvation, clearance of damaged organelles), but excessive autophagy can lead to cell death. Defects in autophagy genes are associated with Crohn disease, neurodegenerative diseases and tumors.
s0784 2.7. Death of the organism and postmortem changes
s0785 **Death** is the irreversible cessation of the vital activity of the organism. By cause, death is divided into **natural** (physiological — from old age), **violent** (trauma, poisoning, homicide, suicide) and death **from disease**. Sudden, unexpected death is called **sudden death** (most often in cardiovascular diseases).
s0786 Stages of death:
s0787 **Agony** — the state before death: disordered fading of functions.
s0788 **Clinical death** — breathing and cardiac activity have stopped, but there are no irreversible changes in the tissues yet. At normal temperature it lasts **5–6 minutes** (the time the cerebral cortex tolerates hypoxia); during this period resuscitation can restore the organism.
s0789 **Biological death** — irreversible changes begin in the tissues, first of all in the brain; resuscitation is ineffective. In modern medicine **brain death** (irreversible cessation of all functions of the brain, including the brainstem) is recognized as the death of the person.

s0804 2.8. Clinicopathological section
s0805 Ischemia–reperfusion: is a quickly opened vessel not always beneficial?
s0806 In a patient presenting with myocardial infarction, the vessel is opened in the first hours (stenting or thrombolysis). This greatly reduces the necrotic focus — cardiomyocytes that are still reversibly injured are saved. However, as soon as blood flow is restored, some cells die because of **reperfusion injury** (free radicals, Ca2+ overload, leukocytes), and reperfusion arrhythmias appear. Microscopically, reperfused myocardium shows **hemorrhages** and **contraction band necrosis** (hypercontracted cardiomyocytes with transverse “bands”).
s0807 Types of necrosis in the clinic
s0808 **Coagulative necrosis:** infarcts of the heart, kidney and spleen — clinically pain and impaired organ function.
s0809 **Liquefactive necrosis:** ischemic stroke — a brain cyst after several weeks; abscesses — removal of pus (drainage) is necessary.
s0810 **Caseous necrosis:** tuberculosis — the caseous mass is discharged through a bronchus, forming a **cavity**, and the patient spreads mycobacteria to others.
s0811 **Fat necrosis:** acute pancreatitis — blood calcium falls (it is used up in soap formation), which is a sign of a severe course.
s0812 **Gangrene:** diabetic foot — if it progresses to wet gangrene, amputation and prevention of sepsis are necessary.
s0813 **Chemical liver necrosis.** In a patient who has taken a large dose of paracetamol (for example, 10–15 g), there are few symptoms on the first day, but on days 2–3 jaundice, a sharp rise in ALT and AST (several thousand units) and liver failure develop. Liver biopsy shows **central (centrilobular) necrosis** — because the cytochrome P-450 enzymes that form the toxic metabolite are located mainly in the center of the lobule, and this area is the least supplied with oxygen.
s0814 **Gangrene** (Greek gangraina — fire) — necrosis of tissues in contact with the external environment that turns black as a result of the formation of iron sulfide.
s0815 **Infarction** (Latin infarcire — to stuff, to fill in) — tissue necrosis resulting from impaired circulation (ischemia).
s0816 **Karyopyknosis** — shrinkage of the nucleus, condensation of chromatin.
s0817 **Karyorrhexis** — fragmentation of the nucleus.
s0818 **Karyolysis** — dissolution of the nucleus.
s0819 **Malacia** (Greek malakos — soft) — softening of dead tissue (for example, in the brain).
s0820 **Marantic necrosis** — bedsores occurring in exhausted elderly people with signs of cachexia and marasmus.
s0821 **Mummification** (Latin mumificatio — drying) — drying and hardening of dead tissue.
s0822 **Necrobiosis** (Greek nekros — dead, bios — life) — irreversible degenerative changes preceding necrosis; a process of slow dying.
s0823 **Necrosis** (Greek nekros — dead) — death in a living organism with cessation of the vital activity of cells and tissues.
s0824 **Noma** (Greek nome — gnawing sore, “water cancer”) — wet gangrene of the facial soft tissues in debilitated children (often after measles and other severe infections).
s0825 **Pathobiosis** — necrobiosis prolonged in time.
s0826 **Plasmolysis** — hydrolytic breakdown and dissolution of the cytoplasm.
s0827 **Plasmorrhexis** — fragmentation of the cytoplasm.
s0828 **Sequestrum** (Latin sequestrum — separated) — a part of dead tissue that does not undergo autolysis, is not replaced by connective tissue and lies freely among living tissues.
s0829 **“Ischemic infarction of the spleen”.** A whitish-yellow, firm, triangular (wedge-shaped) focus beneath the splenic capsule; its base faces the capsule and its apex faces the hilum of the spleen; a red rim (demarcation zone) around the focus; fibrin deposits on the capsule. Conclusion: coagulative necrosis — white infarct.
s0830 **“Hemorrhagic (red) infarction of the lung”.** A dark red, firm, wedge-shaped focus in the lung tissue; its base faces the pleura, the pleura is dull, with fibrinous deposits. Conclusion: red infarct (Topic 7).
s0831 **“Gangrene of the leg”.** The foot and toes are black-bluish; in dry gangrene — wrinkled, hard, separated from healthy tissue by a demarcation line; in wet gangrene — swollen, soft, without demarcation. Conclusion: gangrene (specify the type).
s0832 **“Gangrenous appendicitis”.** The vermiform appendix is thickened, dirty green or black, its serosa is dull, with fibrinopurulent deposits. Conclusion: a form of intestinal gangrene (wet gangrene).
s0833 **“Caseous necrosis of a lymph node”.** The lymph node is enlarged and firm; on the cut surface — a white-yellow, dry, crumbly, cheese-like mass. Conclusion: caseous necrosis (tuberculosis).
s0834 **“Caseous necrosis of the kidney (renal tuberculosis)”.** The kidney structure is destroyed, the cortex and medulla are indistinguishable, with cavities (caverns) filled with a cheese-like mass. Conclusion: caseous necrosis.
s0835 **“Petrifications in the lung”.** Small, stone-hard, whitish, round foci in the lung tissue; the knife grates when cutting. Conclusion: an outcome of necrosis — petrification (for example, the Ghon focus).
s0836 **“Gray softening of the brain (ischemic infarction)”.** A soft, mushy, gray focus with indistinct borders in the brain tissue. Conclusion: liquefactive necrosis.
s0837 **“Necrosis of the renal tubular epithelium”** (hematoxylin and eosin; for example, in mercuric chloride poisoning or shock). In the convoluted tubules of the renal cortex the epithelial cells lack nuclei (**karyolysis**), in some cells the nucleus is shrunken (pyknosis) or fragmented (karyorrhexis); the cytoplasm is homogeneous and eosinophilic (plasmocoagulation) or broken into fragments (plasmorrhexis); cell debris in the tubular lumen. The glomeruli are preserved. Conclusion: necrotic nephrosis (acute tubular necrosis).
