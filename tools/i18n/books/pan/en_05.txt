s0575 Ischemic and hypoxic injury
s0576 In **hypoxia** (reduced oxygen with preserved blood flow), glycolysis continues and injury develops more slowly. In **ischemia** (cessation of blood flow), glycolytic substrates are not delivered either and metabolites (lactic acid) are not removed — therefore injury is faster and more severe.
s0577 **Ischemia–reperfusion injury.** Sometimes, when blood flow is restored to ischemic but still viable tissue (thrombolysis, stenting, surgery), injury paradoxically **increases**. The causes:
s0578 in the presence of returning oxygen, large amounts of **free radicals** are generated in damaged mitochondria and leukocytes;
s0579 **Ca^2+^ overload** increases;
s0580 **inflammation** — leukocytes and plasma proteins enter the injured area and cause additional injury;
s0581 the **complement** system is activated (IgM antibodies are deposited in ischemic tissue).
s0582 Chemical (toxic) injury
s0583 Chemicals injure the cell in two ways:
s0584 **Direct action** — by binding to molecular components: mercuric chloride binds to the sulfhydryl groups of membrane proteins; cyanide blocks mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase; chemotherapy drugs are directly cytotoxic.
s0585 **Conversion to toxic metabolites** — mainly by the **cytochrome P-450** enzymes of the smooth ER of the liver. For example, **carbon tetrachloride (CCl~4~)** is converted in the liver to the highly reactive free radical CCl3: lipids undergo peroxidation, the ER swells, protein synthesis (including lipoproteins) decreases, fat accumulates in hepatocytes and necrosis develops. In large doses, the toxic metabolite of **paracetamol** (acetaminophen) depletes glutathione stores and causes centrilobular necrosis of the liver.
s0586 **N-acetylcysteine** is used as an antidote in paracetamol poisoning — it restores glutathione stores. In people who consume alcohol, cytochrome P-450 enzymes are induced and glutathione is low, so even an ordinary dose of paracetamol can damage their liver.
s0587 2.3. Morphology of reversible injury
s0588 Reversible injury has two main morphologic manifestations:
s0589 **1. Cellular swelling** (hydropic change, vacuolar degeneration) — entry of water into the cell as a result of failure of the ion pumps. It is the **first** sign of almost all forms of injury.
s0590 Grossly: the organ is slightly enlarged and heavier, its color is pale, and the cut surface is dull and looks “boiled” (in older literature — “cloudy swelling”, granular degeneration).
s0591 Microscopically: the cells are enlarged, with small clear vacuoles in the cytoplasm (distended segments of the ER) — **vacuolar (hydropic) degeneration**. This condition is discussed in detail in Topic 3. **2. Fatty change (fatty degeneration)** — small and large lipid vacuoles in the cytoplasm. It is seen most often in cells involved in lipid metabolism — hepatocytes and cardiomyocytes (Topic 3).
s0592 Ultrastructural changes of reversible injury:
s0593 changes in the **plasma membrane** — formation of blebs (blebbing), loss of microvilli, loosening of intercellular attachments.
s0594 changes in **mitochondria** — swelling, appearance of small amorphous densities.
s0595 **dilatation of the ER**, detachment of ribosomes and breakdown of polysomes.
s0596 changes in the **nucleus** — clumping of chromatin granules.
s0597 **myelin figures** in the cytoplasm — layered phospholipid structures derived from damaged membranes.

s0600 2.4. Necrosis
s0601 **Necrosis** (Greek nekros — dead) is the death of cells, tissue or part of an organ **in a living organism**, with complete cessation of their vital activity. Necrosis is the end result of irreversible injury and develops as a result of **denaturation** of cellular components and **enzymatic digestion** of the dead cell. The enzymes that digest the cell come from the lysosomes of the dead cell itself (**autolysis**) and from leukocytes attracted by inflammation (**heterolysis**).
s0602 **Autolysis must be distinguished from necrosis. Autolysis** (self-digestion) is the breakdown of cells in the dead body after death under the action of their own enzymes; there is **no inflammatory reaction**. Autolysis develops fastest in organs rich in enzymes (pancreas, gastric mucosa, adrenal medulla).
s0603 Stages of necrosis
s0604 **Paranecrosis** — reversible changes (resembling necrosis but still able to regress).
s0605 **Necrobiosis** — irreversible degenerative changes; catabolic reactions predominate over anabolic ones.
s0606 **Cell death** — its exact time is difficult to establish.
s0607 **Autolysis (and heterolysis)** — breakdown of the dead cell's substrate by hydrolytic enzymes and inflammatory cells.

s0653 Clinicomorphologic forms of necrosis
s0654 **1. Coagulative (dry) necrosis** is the most common form. The dead tissue **preserves its structural outlines** for several days: proteins and enzymes are denatured and proteolysis stops. The cells have no nuclei and their cytoplasm is eosinophilic, but the outlines of cells and tissue remain visible as “ghosts”. It occurs in organs rich in protein and poor in water — the heart, kidney, spleen and liver. In all organs except the brain, **ischemia** leads to coagulative necrosis.
s0655 Grossly: the focus is dry, firm, gray-yellow or whitish, and separated from the surroundings by a red rim (demarcation zone).
s0656 Special types of coagulative necrosis:
s0657 **ischemic infarction** (myocardium, kidney, spleen);
s0658 **waxy (Zenker) necrosis** — in the rectus abdominis and thigh adductor muscles in typhoid fever and other severe infections; the muscle is whitish and looks like wax;
s0659 **caseous (cheese-like) necrosis**;
s0660 **fibrinoid necrosis** (discussed separately below).
s0661 Figure 2.4
s0662 Ischemic infarction of the spleen: a whitish, firm, triangular focus beneath the capsule; on the cut surface its base faces the capsule (TMA study guide, 2018)
s0663 Figure 2.3
s0664 Coagulative necrosis of the kidney: A — wedge-shaped renal infarct (yellow); B — border of the infarct: normal kidney on the left (N), necrotic cells on the right (I) — cell outlines are preserved, nuclei are absent; an inflammatory infiltrate between them (Robbins, 9th ed., Fig. 2-11)
s0665 **2. Liquefactive (wet) necrosis** — the dead tissue **liquefies and dissolves**. It occurs in tissues rich in water and enzymes and poor in protein. Main examples:
s0666 in the **brain** after hypoxia and ischemia (ischemic stroke) — the focus is called **“gray softening”**, and later a **cyst** forms in its place;
s0667 in **bacterial (sometimes fungal) infections** — leukocyte enzymes dissolve the tissue and form **pus** (abscess).

s0669 Liquefactive necrosis of the brain: in the area of cerebral infarction the tissue softens and dissolves (Robbins, 9th ed., Fig. 2-12)
s0670 **3. Caseous (cheese-like) necrosis** is a special type of coagulative necrosis. Grossly it is a **white-yellow, dry, crumbly, cheese-like** mass. It occurs mainly in **tuberculosis**, rarely in fungal infections (histoplasmosis), syphilis (gumma) and lymphogranulomatosis. Microscopically, the caseous focus appears as a **structureless, granular, pink mass**: the cell outlines are **not preserved** (unlike coagulative necrosis), and it is surrounded by **granulomatous inflammation** (epithelioid cells, Pirogov–Langhans giant cells, lymphocytes).

s0673 Figure 2.7
s0674 Renal tuberculosis — caseous necrosis: the kidney structure is destroyed, with cavities (caverns) filled with a cheese-like mass (TMA study guide, 2018)
s0675 **4. Fat necrosis (steatonecrosis)** — focal destruction of adipose tissue. It is seen mainly in **acute pancreatitis**: activated pancreatic lipases escape and break down the triglycerides of the adipose tissue of the peritoneum, omentum and mesentery. The released fatty acids combine with calcium to form **calcium soaps** (**saponification**) — grossly, **chalky white spots** are seen. Microscopically: the shadowy outlines of fat cells, basophilic calcium deposits between them and inflammation. **Traumatic fat necrosis** of the breast may be clinically mistaken for cancer.
s0676 **5. Fibrinoid necrosis** — necrosis of connective tissue and of the vessel wall. It usually occurs in **immune reactions** (antigen–antibody complexes deposited in the vessel wall together with fibrin) — vasculitis, rheumatic diseases, and also in **malignant hypertension**. With hematoxylin and eosin, a **bright pink, homogeneous, fibrin-like** mass is seen in the vessel wall. This process is also discussed in Topic 3 as the irreversible stage of fibrinoid swelling.

s0681 **6. Gangrene** (Greek gangraina — “gnawing sore”) is necrosis of tissues **in contact with the external environment** (skin, intestine, lung, limbs, uterus). Because iron sulfide (a breakdown product of hemoglobin — a compound of hemoglobin iron and hydrogen sulfide from the air) forms in the necrotic focus, the tissue becomes **black or gray-black**.

s0707 Outcomes of necrosis
s0708 **Demarcation inflammation** develops around the necrotic focus: the vessels dilate (hyperemia), and there is edema and leukocyte infiltration. This zone separates the dead tissue from the living tissue. Further outcomes:
s0709 **Favorable outcomes:**
s0710 – **organization** — the necrotic focus is replaced by granulation tissue, and a **scar** forms (for example, postinfarction cardiosclerosis); – **encapsulation** — the focus is surrounded by a connective tissue capsule; – **petrification** (calcification) — calcium salts are deposited in the necrotic focus (dystrophic calcification, Topic 4); for example, the **Ghon focus** remaining from the primary tuberculous focus; – **ossification** — bone tissue forms in the focus; – **cyst** formation — in place of liquefactive necrosis (for example, in the brain); – **mutilation** — separation of the necrotic part (in dry gangrene of a finger). • **Unfavorable outcomes: purulent dissolution** — suppurative breakdown of the necrotic focus, which can lead to sepsis; loss of function of the organ containing the necrotic focus (cardiac rupture, paralysis after stroke).

s0715 **The significance of necrosis** depends on its location and size. In vital organs (heart, brain), even a small necrosis can cause death. Substances released from the necrotic focus are **detected in the blood** and used in diagnosis: in myocardial necrosis — **troponin I and T**, creatine kinase-MB; in liver necrosis — **ALT and AST** (transaminases); in pancreatitis — **amylase and lipase**; in skeletal muscle necrosis (rhabdomyolysis) — creatine kinase and myoglobin. In addition, extensive necrosis leads to a **resorptive syndrome** — fever, leukocytosis and an increased ESR.
