s0464 Case 1
s0465 A 58-year-old man had suffered from arterial hypertension for 15 years and was not treated regularly. In the last year he became short of breath when climbing stairs, and edema appeared in his legs. He died of chronic heart failure. At autopsy: heart weight 610 g, left ventricular wall 2.4 cm, the cavities are dilated, the apex of the heart is rounded, and the myocardium is flabby.
s0466 1) What process was observed in the heart? 2) What is its type and stage? 3) State the mechanism and microscopic signs of hypertrophy.
s0467 1) Hypertrophy of the heart (mainly of the left ventricle). 2) Compensatory (work) hypertrophy caused by arterial hypertension (increased peripheral resistance). Dilatation of the cavities, rounding of the apex and heart failure indicate the decompensation stage (eccentric hypertrophy, myogenic dilatation). 3) Mechanical stretch and hormonal factors (angiotensin II, adrenergic agonists) activate signaling pathways and increase the synthesis of structural proteins and organelles; cardiomyocytes do not divide, they only enlarge. Microscopically, the cardiomyocytes are thickened, with large, hyperchromatic, box-shaped nuclei; in decompensation — degeneration, death of cardiomyocytes and interstitial sclerosis.
s0468 Case 2
s0469 A 72-year-old woman was treated for a long time for gastric cancer and died. At autopsy the subcutaneous fat layer is almost absent, and the remaining adipose tissue is orange. The heart weighs 180 g and is small, the subepicardial coronary arteries are tortuous, and the myocardium is brown. The liver weighs 950 g, its capsule is wrinkled, and the cut surface is brown.
s0470 1) What generalized process was observed? 2) What are the changes in the heart and liver called? 3) What causes the brown color of the organs and the orange color of the adipose tissue?
s0471 1) Cachexia — generalized atrophy, in this case cancer cachexia (tumor intoxication, TNF and other cytokines, impaired absorption of nutrients). 2) Brown atrophy of the heart and liver. 3) The brown color is due to the accumulation of lipofuscin pigment, formed from undigested residues of autophagy in atrophic cells. The orange color of the adipose tissue is due to the increased concentration of lipochrome pigment.
s0472 Case 3
s0473 A 45-year-old man has smoked a pack of cigarettes a day for 25 years. Because of chronic bronchitis, bronchoscopy was performed and a biopsy was taken from the bronchial mucosa. Histologic examination: one part of the bronchus is lined by pseudostratified ciliated epithelium and another part by stratified squamous epithelium; there is no cellular atypia.
s0474 1) Formulate the histologic conclusion. 2) Explain the mechanism of this process. 3) What are its clinical significance and prognosis?
s0475 1) Squamous metaplasia of the bronchial epithelium without atypia. 2) Under the influence of tobacco smoke, the stem (basal) cells of the bronchial epithelium are reprogrammed and differentiate into the more resistant stratified squamous epithelium instead of ciliated epithelium. 3) In metaplasia, mucus secretion and ciliary function are lost — mucociliary clearance is impaired, and bronchitis and infections become more frequent. Metaplasia is a precancerous background process: if smoking continues, dysplasia and squamous cell lung carcinoma may develop. If smoking stops, the metaplasia can regress, so the patient is strongly advised to quit smoking.

s0516 the features of ischemic, ischemia–reperfusion and toxic injury;
s0517 the morphologic signs of reversible injury (cellular swelling, fatty change);
s0518 the definition, stages, microscopic signs, etiologic and clinicomorphologic types and outcomes of necrosis;
s0519 the definition, causes, mechanisms and morphology of apoptosis and how it differs from necrosis;
s0520 the concept of other pathways of cell death (necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis) and of autophagy;
s0521 death of the organism and postmortem changes.
s0522 identify the signs of necrosis in a microscopic slide (changes in the nucleus and cytoplasm);
s0523 describe gross specimens of ischemic infarction of the spleen, hemorrhagic infarction of the lung, gangrene of the leg, gangrenous appendicitis, caseous necrosis of a lymph node and of the kidney, and calcified foci (petrifications) in the lung;
s0524 describe microscopic slides of necrosis of the renal tubular epithelium, caseous necrosis and Councilman bodies;
s0525 distinguish necrosis from apoptosis and relate the types of necrosis to clinical situations.
s0526 **Reversible injury** — if the injurious agent is removed, the cell returns to normal. Its two main morphologic signs are **cellular swelling** and **fatty change**. The signs of **irreversible injury** are **severe mitochondrial dysfunction** (complete loss of the ability to produce ATP, amorphous densities in the matrix) and **profound disruption** of **membranes** (plasma and lysosomal membranes). These two phenomena are the cell's “point of no return”.
s0527 Enzymes that protect against free radicals — **“CSG”**: **C**atalase, **S**uperoxide dismutase, **G**lutathione peroxidase. Remember the formation of the hydroxyl radical (^•^OH) as “**iron + peroxide = Fenton**”: this is why liver and heart cells are injured in hemochromatosis (iron overload).
s0528 Necrosis is always a **pathologic** process: it involves **a group of cells**, membrane integrity is lost, intracellular contents leak out and **provoke inflammation**. Apoptosis, in contrast, is often **physiological**, occurs in individual cells, the membrane is preserved and it **does not provoke** inflammation.
s0529 Order of nuclear changes — **“Pyk-Rhex-Lys”**: **Pyk**nosis (shrinks) → karyo**rrhex**is (fragments) → karyo**lys**is (dissolves). Likewise for the cytoplasm: **coagulation → rhexis → lysis**.
s0530 To remember the caspases of apoptosis: **“Intrinsic — 9, Extrinsic — 8, Executioners — 3 and 6”**. Imagine the intrinsic pathway as a **“mitochondrial gate”**: BCL2 is the gatekeeper (keeps it closed), BAX/BAK are the gate breakers, and cytochrome c is the “messenger of death” that comes out through the gate.
s0531 The textbooks of Strukov and Abdullakhodjaeva discuss two main types of cell death — necrosis and apoptosis. Recent editions of Robbins add **necroptosis, pyroptosis and ferroptosis** to them. When “types of cell death” are asked at the exam, it is advisable first to describe necrosis and apoptosis fully and then briefly mention the modern types.
s0532 At autopsy it is important to distinguish a **postmortem blood clot** from a **thrombus** formed during life: a thrombus is **dull, dry, friable, layered** and **attached** to the vessel wall; a postmortem clot is **smooth, shiny, elastic**, not attached to the wall, follows the shape of the vessel and separates into layers resembling “chicken fat” (yellow, plasma part) and “currant jelly” (red, red cell part) (Topic 7).
s0533 2.1. General laws of cell injury
s0534 When an injurious agent acting on a cell exceeds its adaptive capacity, **cell injury** begins. Its outcome is determined by four factors:
s0535 **The type, duration and severity of the injurious agent.** A small dose of toxin or brief ischemia causes reversible injury; a large dose or prolonged ischemia leads to cell death.
s0536 **The type, state and adaptability of the cell.** Skeletal muscle tolerates complete ischemia for 2–3 hours, a cardiomyocyte for 20–30 minutes, and a neuron for only 3–5 minutes.
s0537 **The genetic features of the cell.** For example, polymorphism of cytochrome P-450 enzymes determines different responses to the same drug dose in different people.
s0538 **Several interdependent systems of the cell are injured at the same time:** membranes, aerobic respiration (ATP production), protein synthesis, the cytoskeleton and the genetic apparatus.
s0539 Biochemical changes in the cell appear **before** morphologic changes: function is impaired within a few minutes, ultrastructural changes appear after several tens of minutes, changes visible by light microscopy after hours, and gross changes much later. For example, in myocardial ischemia a cardiomyocyte is irreversibly injured in 20–60 minutes, the light-microscopic signs of necrosis are detected after 4–12 hours, and the gross signs after 12–24 hours.
s0540 This is why a patient who died suddenly may have no gross or even microscopic signs of myocardial infarction. In such cases the pathologist uses histochemical methods: **loss of glycogen** in injured cardiomyocytes (PAS reaction) and failure to stain with **triphenyltetrazolium chloride** (loss of dehydrogenase activity) are the earliest signs of ischemia. Clinically, **troponins** and creatine kinase-MB, which are released into the blood when the cardiomyocyte membrane is disrupted, are the main markers of myocardial necrosis.
s0541 2.2. Mechanisms of cell injury
s0542 ATP depletion
s0543 ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is produced mainly in mitochondria by oxidative phosphorylation. **Hypoxia and ischemia** and mitochondrial poisons (cyanide) rapidly deplete ATP stores. A fall in ATP to 5–10% leads to the following consequences:
s0544 the **Na^+^/K^+^-ATPase** (sodium pump) slows down — Na^+^ and water enter the cell and K^+^ leaves: **cellular swelling**, dilatation of the ER;
s0545 **anaerobic glycolysis** increases — glycogen stores are exhausted, lactic acid accumulates and the **pH falls**: nuclear chromatin clumps (condensation) and enzyme activity changes;
s0546 the **Ca^2+^ pump** fails — calcium increases in the cytoplasm;
s0547 **ribosomes** detach from the rough ER and polysomes break down — **protein synthesis decreases**;
s0548 misfolded proteins accumulate and trigger “ER stress” (the unfolded protein response) and apoptosis.
s0549 Mitochondrial damage
s0550 Mitochondria are damaged by increased cytosolic Ca^2+^, free radicals and hypoxia. A high-conductance channel opens in the inner membrane — the **mitochondrial permeability transition pore** — the membrane potential is lost and oxidative phosphorylation stops. In addition, **cytochrome c** and other proteins leak from the intermembrane space into the cytoplasm and initiate apoptosis. Thus mitochondrial damage leads the cell to death by one of two pathways: ATP exhaustion — **necrosis**, release of cytochrome c — **apoptosis**.
s0551 Disturbance of calcium homeostasis
s0552 Normally the concentration of free cytosolic Ca^2+^ is 10,000 times lower than outside the cell. Under the influence of ischemia and toxins, Ca^2+^ enters the cytoplasm from outside the cell and from intracellular stores (mitochondria, ER). Excess Ca^2+^ activates the following enzymes:
s0553 **phospholipases** — degrade membranes;
s0554 **proteases** — degrade membrane and cytoskeletal proteins;
s0555 **endonucleases** — degrade DNA and chromatin;
s0556 **ATPases** — accelerate ATP depletion. In addition, Ca^2+^ opens the mitochondrial permeability pore and initiates apoptosis.
s0557 Free radicals (oxidative stress)
s0558 **Free radicals** are highly reactive molecules that have an unpaired electron in their outer orbit. The most important in cell injury are **reactive oxygen species (ROS)**: the superoxide anion (O~2~^−^), hydrogen peroxide (H~2~O~2~) and the most reactive — the hydroxyl radical (^•^OH).
s0559 Sources of free radicals:
s0560 normal metabolism — incomplete reduction of oxygen in mitochondria;
s0561 ionizing radiation (hydrolyzes water to form ^•^OH);
s0562 the “respiratory burst” of neutrophils and macrophages in inflammation (NADPH oxidase);
s0563 metabolism of drugs and chemicals (CCl~4~, paracetamol (acetaminophen));
s0564 transition metals — iron and copper (Fenton reaction: Fe^2+^ + H~2~O~2~ → Fe^3+^ + ^•^OH + OH^−^);
s0565 nitric oxide (NO) — forms peroxynitrite.
s0566 Harmful effects of free radicals:
s0567 **Lipid peroxidation in membranes** — a chain reaction with unsaturated fatty acids.
s0568 **Oxidation of proteins** — protein folding is disrupted, enzyme activity is lost and degradation in proteasomes increases.
s0569 **DNA damage** — single-strand breaks, mutations; contributes to cellular aging and malignant transformation. **The antioxidant defense system:** antioxidant vitamins (vitamins E, A, C, beta-carotene, glutathione), iron- and copper-binding proteins (transferrin, ferritin, lactoferrin, ceruloplasmin) and enzymes — **catalase** (breaks down H~2~O~2~), **superoxide dismutase** (converts O~2~^−^ to H~2~O~2~), **glutathione peroxidase**.
s0570 Membrane damage and DNA damage
s0571 Membrane damage is the final stage in most types of injury. Its causes are ROS (lipid peroxidation), decreased phospholipid synthesis, activation of phospholipases and cytoskeletal disruption. As a result:
s0572 damage to the **mitochondrial membrane** — ATP production stops and apoptotic factors are released;
s0573 damage to the **plasma membrane** — osmotic balance is lost and intracellular contents leak out;
s0574 damage to the **lysosomal membrane** — enzymes (RNase, DNase, proteases, phosphatases) are released into the cytoplasm and cause the cell to **“digest itself”** — this is the basis of the morphology of necrosis. **DNA damage** (by radiation, chemotherapy drugs, ROS) activates the **p53** protein: it arrests the cell cycle in the G~1~ phase and gives time for DNA repair. If the damage cannot be repaired, p53 initiates **apoptosis**.
