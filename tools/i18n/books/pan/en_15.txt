s2005 Fibrinous inflammation
s2006 The exudate contains much **fibrinogen**, which is converted to **fibrin** in the damaged (necrotic) tissue. Tissue thromboplastin accelerates fibrin formation. Fibrinous inflammation develops mainly in **mucous and serous membranes**, less often in the thickness of an organ (in the lung). A **whitish-gray membrane (film)** forms on the surface of the mucous or serous membrane. By the depth of necrosis and the type of lining epithelium, there are two types:
s2007 **1. Croupous inflammation** (Scottish croup — membrane) — necrosis is **superficial**, the fibrin membrane is **thin** and **loosely** attached to the underlying tissue; it **detaches easily**, leaving a superficial defect. It occurs in mucous membranes lined by simple (columnar) epithelium or in serous membranes:
s2008 **in the trachea and bronchi** — croupous tracheitis and bronchitis in diphtheria: the membranes can detach and block the lumen of the bronchi — **true croup** (risk of asphyxia);
s2009 **in the lung** — **croupous (lobar) pneumonia** (pneumococcus; a whole lobe is affected);
s2010 **in serous membranes** — fibrinous pleurisy, peritonitis, **pericarditis**: the pericardial surface is rough and covered with hairlike fibrin threads — the **“hairy heart”** (cor villosum); on auscultation — a **pericardial friction rub**; causes — uremia (chronic kidney disease), rheumatic fever, myocardial infarction, tuberculosis.

s2012 Fibrinous epicarditis: the epicardium is dull and rough, with hairlike gray-yellowish fibrin deposits (TMA study guide, 2018)

s2015 **2. Diphtheritic inflammation** (Greek diphthera — leather membrane) — necrosis is **deep**, the fibrin membrane is **thick**, penetrates deep into the necrotic tissue and is **firmly** attached to the underlying tissue; the membrane **detaches with difficulty**, leaving a deep **ulcer**. It occurs in mucous membranes lined by **stratified squamous epithelium** (oral cavity, pharynx, **tonsils**, esophagus, vagina, cervix) and in places where the subepithelial tissue is looser (**colon** — dysentery).

s2022 **Outcomes of fibrinous inflammation:** in mucous membranes the membranes detach and **ulcers** form (superficial in croupous, deep in diphtheritic — healing with a scar). In serous membranes, fibrin can be resorbed or undergo **organization**: **adhesions** (scar bands) form between the serous layers; in the pericardium, complete obliteration of the cavity (**obliteration**) and calcification may occur — the “**armored heart**” (constrictive pericarditis). In the lung, organization of fibrin — **carnification** (“turning into flesh”).
s2023 Purulent inflammation
s2024 **Neutrophils** predominate in the exudate. **Pus** is a thick, cloudy, yellow-green fluid containing living and dead neutrophils (**pus corpuscles**), lymphocytes, macrophages, tissue debris, microbes and protein-rich fluid. An important property of purulent exudate is its ability to **dissolve tissue** (**histolysis**): the proteolytic enzymes of neutrophils dissolve the tissue and form a cavity.
s2025 Purulent inflammation is caused by **pyogenic (pus-forming)** bacteria: staphylococcus, streptococcus, gonococcus, meningococcus, Fraenkel diplococcus (pneumococcus), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli; less often aseptic suppuration (chemicals — turpentine).
s2026 Types of purulent inflammation:
s2027 **1. Abscess** — **localized** purulent inflammation of tissue in which the tissue dissolves and a **cavity filled with pus** forms. Around the abscess a **pyogenic capsule (pyogenic membrane)** forms — a layer of granulation tissue; it is rich in capillaries, through which leukocytes continuously enter the abscess cavity, and it absorbs pus. In an **acute abscess** the wall consists of one layer — the pyogenic membrane (granulation tissue); in a **chronic abscess** the wall has two layers: the inner **pyogenic** layer and the outer **fibrous** layer (mature connective tissue).
s2028 An abscess can occur in any organ (lung, liver, brain, kidney, subcutaneous tissue). Numerous small abscesses are called **apostematous** inflammation (for example, **apostematous nephritis** — many small abscesses in the kidney in sepsis). An abscess may rupture spontaneously onto the skin surface or into a cavity (forming a fistula).

s2033 **2. Phlegmon** — nonlocalized, **diffuse** purulent inflammation: the purulent exudate spreads between tissues, along fatty tissue, fasciae and neurovascular bundles, soaking and separating the tissues. Mainly in **soft tissues** — subcutaneous fatty tissue, between muscles, the mediastinum (**mediastinitis**), around the kidney (**paranephritis**), around the rectum (**paraproctitis**), and also in the wall of hollow organs (**phlegmonous appendicitis**, phlegmonous cholecystitis). **Soft phlegmon** — the necrotic foci liquefy; **hard phlegmon** — the tissue undergoes coagulative necrosis and sloughs off. Phlegmon often progresses to **sepsis**.
s2034 **3. Empyema** — accumulation of pus in a **preexisting natural cavity** or hollow organ: **pleural empyema** (pyothorax), empyema of the gallbladder, empyema of the appendix, empyema of the maxillary sinus, pericardial empyema (pyopericardium). Long-standing empyema may end in obliteration of the cavity.
s2035 **General features and outcomes of purulent inflammation:** usually an acute course; severe **intoxication**, fever, neutrophilic leukocytosis. If the pus is discharged (by rupture or surgery) — healing with a scar. Unfavorable outcomes: spread of pus to neighboring tissues (fistulas), erosion of vessel walls (**arrosive bleeding**), spread through lymph and blood — **sepsis**, **AA amyloidosis** (in chronic suppuration, Topic 8).

s2042 Putrefactive (ichorous) inflammation
s2043 It develops when **anaerobic putrefactive microorganisms** (clostridia and others) enter the focus of inflammation. The tissue putrefies, **foul-smelling** gases are formed, and the exudate becomes dirty green. It usually occurs in wounds with heavy contamination and destroyed tissue (war and road traffic injuries, military wounds); it is close to anaerobic (gas) gangrene. It runs a course with severe intoxication and has an unfavorable prognosis.
s2044 Hemorrhagic inflammation
s2045 The exudate contains many **red blood cells** and is red or brown. The cause is a **sharp increase** in the permeability of microcirculatory vessels and damage to the vessel wall. It is characteristic of highly dangerous infections: **plague, anthrax (Siberian ulcer)**, and also **severe forms of influenza**
s2046 (hemorrhagic pneumonia, hemorrhagic tracheobronchitis) and smallpox. The prognosis is usually serious. It often combines with other exudative types (serous-hemorrhagic, purulent-hemorrhagic).
s2047 Catarrhal inflammation
s2048 **Catarrhal inflammation (catarrh)** (Greek katarrheo — I flow down) develops **only in mucous membranes**: the exudate **flows down** from the mucosal surface and contains much **mucus** (hyperfunction of goblet cells and glands). By the type of exudate, **serous, mucous and purulent** catarrh are distinguished, and they can pass into one another (for example, in a common cold: first serous, then mucous, then mucopurulent discharge).
s2049 Causes: infections (**ARVI**, influenza), allergy (**allergic rhinitis**), thermal and chemical influences, endogenous intoxication (uremic catarrh).
s2050 **Acute catarrh** usually **resolves completely** in 2–3 weeks. **Chronic catarrh** is accompanied by **atrophy** (atrophic catarrh) or **hypertrophy** (hypertrophic catarrh, polyps) of the mucosa.

s2055 5.8. Outcomes of acute inflammation
s2056 **Complete resolution** — if the injury was limited or brief: the mediators are neutralized, the exudate is resorbed through the lymphatics, neutrophils undergo apoptosis, macrophages clear the debris, and the parenchyma regenerates. For example, complete restoration of lung tissue in croupous (lobar) pneumonia.
s2057 **Scar formation (fibrosis)** — if much tissue was destroyed, nonregenerating tissue was damaged or much fibrin accumulated: the necrotic focus and exudate are replaced by granulation tissue, which turns into a scar (organization).
s2058 **Abscess formation** — in infection with pyogenic bacteria.
s2059 **Progression to chronic inflammation** — if the agent is not eliminated or the healing process is disturbed (Topic 6). **An ulcer (ulcus)** is a **local defect** of the surface of an organ or tissue formed by sloughing of necrotic and inflamed tissue. It occurs most often in the mucosa of the mouth, stomach, intestine and genitourinary tract and in the skin of the lower leg (in the elderly with impaired circulation and in people with diabetes). The most characteristic example is **gastric and duodenal ulcer**: in the acute phase there is neutrophilic inflammation at the ulcer edge; in the chronic phase layers of fibrinoid necrosis,
s2060 granulation tissue and scar form at its floor. An **erosion**, in contrast, is a defect only of the mucosal surface (not reaching the muscularis mucosae).

s2063 5.9. Clinicopathological section
s2064 Analysis of exudate — the key to diagnosis
s2065 Analysis of fluid (aspirate) obtained from the pleural or peritoneal cavity helps establish the diagnosis:
s2066 **clear, protein-poor fluid** — **transudate** (heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis);
s2067 **cloudy, protein-rich, lymphocytic exudate** — tuberculous pleurisy or tumor metastases (cytologic examination, search for tubercle mycobacteria);
s2068 **purulent, neutrophilic exudate** — empyema (drainage is necessary);
s2069 **hemorrhagic exudate** — tumor (mesothelioma, metastases), tuberculosis, pulmonary infarction, trauma.
s2070 Fibrinous pericarditis — a sign of uremia
s2071 In the final stage of chronic kidney disease (uremia), nitrogenous wastes accumulated in the blood are excreted through the serous membranes and damage them: **fibrinous pericarditis** (the pericardial friction rub is called the “death knell of uremia”), fibrinous pleurisy, fibrinous and catarrhal inflammation of the mucous membranes (uremic gastritis, colitis). Such a patient needs urgent **hemodialysis**.
s2072 In **diphtheria** the main danger is not the local membranes but the **exotoxin**: it damages the myocardium (**toxic myocarditis**, fatty degeneration — “tiger heart”, Topic 3), the peripheral nerves (**polyneuritis**, paralysis of the soft palate) and the adrenal glands. The cause of early death is **true croup (asphyxia)**; the cause of late death (in the 2nd week) is **cardiac arrest**. Therefore the basis of treatment is **diphtheria antiserum** (antitoxin), and of prevention — **vaccination** (DTP).
s2073 Principle of treating abscess and phlegmon
s2074 If the pus cavity is localized (abscess, empyema), antibiotics cannot penetrate adequately through the pyogenic capsule — the main treatment is **surgical evacuation of pus (drainage)**: “Ubi pus, ibi evacua” — “where there is pus, evacuate it”. In phlegmon, wide incisions and antibiotics are needed, because the process spreads rapidly and can progress to sepsis.
s2075 **Inflammation** (Latin inflammatio) — a vascular-mesenchymal reaction that develops in response to various injuries and is aimed at eliminating the pathogenic agent.
s2076 **Alteration** (Latin alteratio — change) — damage to tissues in the form of degeneration and necrosis of various degrees.
s2077 **Exudation** (Latin exudatio) — escape of the fluid part of blood plasma and formed elements from the vessels.
s2078 **Proliferation** (Latin proliferatio — growth) — multiplication of capillary endothelium and connective tissue cells.
s2079 **Pinocytosis** — uptake of tissue fluid by a cell (macrophage, endothelium).
s2080 **Endocytobiosis** — incomplete phagocytosis: survival of a microbe inside the phagocyte.
s2081 **Abscess** (Latin abscessus — boil, purulent sore) — localized purulent inflammation forming a cavity. An acute abscess is separated from the surrounding tissue by a pyogenic membrane; the wall of a chronic abscess has two layers — pyogenic (inner) and fibrous (outer).
s2082 **Phlegmon** (Greek phlegmone — inflammation) — diffuse purulent inflammation.
s2083 **Empyema** (Greek en — inside, pyon — pus) — accumulation of pus in natural cavities or hollow organs (pleura, appendix, gallbladder, maxillary sinus).
s2084 **Catarrhal inflammation** (Greek katarrheo — I flow down) — inflammation of a mucous membrane in which the exudate flows down together with mucus.
s2085 **Croupous inflammation** (Scottish croup — membrane) — fibrinous inflammation with superficial necrosis, impregnation of the necrotic masses with fibrin and formation of an easily detachable membrane.
s2086 **Diphtheritic inflammation** (Greek diphthera — leather membrane) — fibrinous inflammation with deep necrosis in mucous membranes and formation of a thick membrane that is difficult to detach.
s2087 **Angina (tonsillitis)** (Latin ango — I strangle, I squeeze) — inflammation of the pharynx (tonsils).
s2088 **Sialadenitis** (Greek sialon — saliva, aden — gland) — inflammation of the salivary glands.
s2089 **Ichorosis** (Greek ichor — putrid fluid) — putrefactive (foul-smelling) inflammation.
s2090 **“Fibrinous pericarditis (‘hairy heart’)”.** The epicardium is dull and rough and covered with hairlike gray-yellowish fibrin threads. Conclusion: croupous (fibrinous) inflammation.
s2091 **“Fibrinous pleurisy”.** A gray-whitish, easily detachable fibrin membrane on the surface of the visceral pleura; the pleura is dull. Conclusion: croupous inflammation.
