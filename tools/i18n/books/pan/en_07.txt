s0944 A 64-year-old man has suffered for many years from diabetes mellitus and atherosclerosis of the leg arteries. A month ago the big toe of his right foot became black, dry and wrinkled and was clearly demarcated from healthy tissue. Over the last 3 days the foot has become swollen and bluish-black, a foul odor has appeared, and his temperature is 39 °C.
s0945 1) What process was present a month ago? 2) What is the current condition called, and why did it change? 3) State the differences between the two conditions and their outcomes.
s0946 1) Dry gangrene — because of atherosclerosis, arterial blood flow gradually stopped and the tissue lost water; no infection was added; there is a clear demarcation line. 2) Wet gangrene — putrefactive bacteria have been added to the necrotic focus; in diabetes, tissue edema and weakened immunity predispose to this. 3) In dry gangrene the tissue is hard and mummified, demarcation is clear, there is no intoxication, and the outcome may be mutilation (spontaneous separation). In wet gangrene the tissue is swollen, soft and foul-smelling, there is no demarcation and intoxication is severe; urgent amputation is necessary because of the risk of sepsis.
s0947 A 55-year-old woman developed severe, band-like pain in the upper abdomen after heavy drinking. Blood amylase and lipase were sharply increased, and the calcium level was decreased. s0949 1) Acute (hemorrhagic) pancreatitis (pancreatic necrosis). 2) Fat necrosis (steatonecrosis): activated pancreatic lipases break down the membranes of fat cells and the triglycerides, and the released fatty acids combine with calcium to form calcium soaps (saponification) — these are the chalky white spots. s1002 In degeneration, substances that are **not normally present** accumulate in the cell or intercellular substance, or **normally present substances** accumulate in excess or in an unusual location; sometimes normal substances disappear (for example, loss of glycogen in cardiomyocytes). Most degenerations are **reversible** processes, but profound changes can lead to necrosis (necrobiosis).
s1003 The **Robbins** textbook hardly uses the term “dystrophy”: these processes are discussed as reversible cell injury (cellular swelling, fatty change), **intracellular accumulations**, **hyaline change**, amyloidosis and pathologic calcification. Moreover, the English word dystrophy is used mainly for muscular dystrophies (Duchenne). In CIS and Uzbek textbooks (Strukov, Abdullakhodjaeva), however, the classification of degenerations (dystrophies) is fully preserved, and it is exactly this classification that is asked at the exam.
s1004 Remember the four mechanisms as **“IDAT” — “I Do Avoid Trash”**: **I**nfiltration (excess enters), **D**ecomposition (breaks down), **A**bnormal (perverted) synthesis (an abnormal substance), **T**ransformation (turns into another substance).
s1005 In older textbooks “**granular degeneration**” (“cloudy swelling”) was given as a separate type. Electron microscopy showed that the cytoplasmic “granules” are swollen mitochondria and fragments of the ER, i.e., an expression of the cell's **functional strain (hyperplasia of organelles)** or early injury. Therefore A.I. Strukov and V.V. Serov do not include it among the degenerations. The gross appearance — the organ is enlarged, dull and looks “boiled” — corresponds to “**cellular swelling**” in Robbins.
s1006 “**Tiger heart**” is **parenchymal** fatty degeneration of the myocardium (fat inside cardiomyocytes). “**Fatty infiltration of the heart**” (adiposity of the heart) is **stromal-vascular** fatty degeneration (growth of adipose tissue beneath the epicardium and in the myocardial stroma). Confusing the two is one of the most common mistakes at the exam.
s1007 Stages of connective tissue disorganization: **1) mucoid swelling** (superficial, **reversible**) → **2) fibrinoid swelling** (deep, **irreversible**) → **3) fibrinoid necrosis** → **4) hyalinosis** or sclerosis. This sequence is most evident in rheumatic diseases (rheumatic fever, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis) and in arterial hypertension.
s1008 Distinguishing by stains: **“Mucoid — Metachromasia, Fibrinoid — Fibrin reaction, Hyaline — Homogeneous”**. In mucoid swelling toluidine blue gives a violet-red color; in fibrinoid the reactions for fibrin are positive and it is yellow with van Gieson; hyaline is a dense, homogeneous, pale pink mass, yellow or red with van Gieson.
s1009 In older textbooks (Strukov), the degree of obesity was determined by the **percentage of excess weight over ideal weight**: grade I — 20–29%, II — 30–49%, III — 50–99%, IV — 100% or more. Current clinical practice uses the **WHO BMI classification**; it is useful to know both for the exam.
s1010 3.1. The concept of degeneration (dystrophy)
s1011 **Degeneration (dystrophy)** (Greek dys — disorder, trophe — nourishment) is a complex pathological process in which structural changes appear in cells and intercellular substance as a result of **disturbed tissue (cellular) metabolism**. Degeneration is one of the types of **alteration** (damage).
s1012 Degeneration is based on a disturbance of **trophism**. **Trophism** is the set of mechanisms that ensure the metabolism and structural organization of a tissue. The mechanisms that maintain trophism are divided into two groups:
s1013 **cellular mechanisms** — the cell's own structures and their regulation (cellular autoregulation);
s1014 **extracellular mechanisms** — transport (blood and lymphatic vessels, microcirculation), the intercellular substance, and the integrative systems (neuroendocrine and neurohumoral regulation). Accordingly, the direct causes of degeneration are: disturbed cellular autoregulation (hyperfunction, toxins, radiation, hereditary enzyme deficiency — **enzymopathy**), disturbed transport systems (**hypoxia** — the most common cause of degeneration), and disturbed regulation by the integrative systems (diseases of the endocrine and nervous systems).
s1015 In degeneration, substances that are **not normally present** accumulate in the cell or intercellular substance, or **normally present substances** accumulate in excess or in an unusual location; sometimes normal
s1016 substances disappear (for example, loss of glycogen in cardiomyocytes). Most degenerations are **reversible** processes, but profound changes can lead to necrosis (necrobiosis).
