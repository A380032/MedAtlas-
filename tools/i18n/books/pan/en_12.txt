s1548 4.6. Disorders of nucleoprotein metabolism
s1549 Nucleoproteins consist of protein and nucleic acids. When their metabolism is disturbed, the end product of **purine bases** — **uric acid** — is produced in excess or excreted insufficiently (**hyperuricemia**). Uric acid and its salts (**urates**, mainly sodium urate) are deposited in tissues in the form of crystals.
s1550 Gout
s1551 **Gout** (Greek podagra: pous — foot, agra — trap, “foot trap”) is a disease characterized by deposition of **sodium urate crystals** in the joints and other tissues as a result of hyperuricemia.
s1552 **Primary gout** — a hereditary disorder of purine metabolism (most often reduced renal excretion of uric acid; rarely enzyme defects, for example HGPRT deficiency in Lesch–Nyhan syndrome). Mainly in **men over 30 years of age**.
s1553 **Secondary gout** — massive cell breakdown (leukemias, lymphomas, especially during **chemotherapy** of tumors — “tumor lysis syndrome”), chronic kidney disease, some drugs (thiazide diuretics). Attacks are triggered by **purine-rich food** (meat, offal), **alcohol** (especially beer), chilling, trauma and starvation.
s1554 Morphology:
s1555 **Acute gouty arthritis** — most often the **metatarsophalangeal joint of the big toe** (the first attack occurs here in **90%** of cases). In the synovial fluid, **needle-shaped urate crystals** are seen inside neutrophils; in polarized light they are **negatively birefringent** (yellow). Clinically — sudden, extremely severe pain beginning at night, with redness and swelling of the joint.
s1556 **Chronic gouty arthritis** — urate deposits in the articular cartilage, synovium, tendons and periarticular tissues; joint deformity, bone erosions (a “punched-out” sign on X-ray).
s1557 **Gouty nodules (tophi)** — the pathognomonic sign of gout: an **inflammatory reaction** around a collection of urate crystals — macrophages, lymphocytes, **foreign body giant cells** and fibroblasts (a granuloma). Tophi occur in the auricle, around joints, at the elbow and knee and in tendons; if the skin thins and ruptures, a chalky white mass is discharged.
s1558 **Gouty nephropathy** — urate crystals in the renal tubules and interstitium, uric acid stones, chronic interstitial nephritis; **kidney failure** is an important cause of death in patients with gout.

s1563 Uric acid stone disease and uric acid infarct
s1564 **Uric acid stone disease** — formation of **urate stones** in the kidneys and urinary tract; hyperuricemia and acidic urine (pH below 5.5) predispose to it. Urate stones are **radiolucent** (X-ray negative).
s1565 **Uric acid infarct** — deposits of **sodium and ammonium urate** in the kidneys (especially in the medullary papillae and collecting ducts) of newborns who **lived longer than the first 2 days** of life. On the cut surface, **yellowish-red streaks** directed toward the papillae are seen in the medulla. This is an **adaptive (physiological) state** related to the intense cell turnover of the newborn period and has no pathologic significance. The word “infarct” is used here in the sense of **stuffing** (Latin infarcire) — there is no necrosis.
s1566 In forensic medical practice, finding a uric acid infarct in the kidney of a newborn shows that the infant **was born alive and lived for at least 2 days**.
s1567 4.7. Mineral degenerations
s1568 Minerals are part of cell structures, enzymes, hormones and pigments and maintain acid–base balance. To detect minerals in tissues, microincineration (spectrography), histochemical reactions and X-ray microspectral analysis are used.
s1569 Disorders of calcium metabolism — calcification (calcinosis)
s1570 **Calcification (calcinosis, petrification)** is the **precipitation** of calcium salts (mainly **calcium phosphate** — hydroxyapatite, less often calcium carbonate) from a dissolved state and their accumulation in tissues. With hematoxylin and eosin, calcium is **basophilic (blue-violet)**, **amorphous or granular**; it stains **black** with the **von Kossa** method (silver nitrate) and red with alizarin red. Grossly — whitish, stone-hard foci; the knife grates when cutting.
s1571 By mechanism, three types of calcification are distinguished:
s1572 **1. Dystrophic calcification** — deposition of calcium salts in **injured, necrotic or altered tissues** when **blood calcium is normal**. The cause is **local** physicochemical changes: alkalinization of the tissue, increased alkaline phosphatase activity, changes in collagen and elastic fibers, and phospholipids in the membranes of dead cells (a nucleation site for calcium crystallization). Calcium salts first accumulate in mitochondria and membrane vesicles. As a result **petrifications** form. Examples:
s1573 foci of caseous necrosis (in tuberculosis — the **Ghon focus**, calcified lymph nodes);
s1574 **atherosclerotic plaques** (atherocalcinosis);
s1575 **heart valves** — rheumatic defects, **calcific aortic stenosis** in the elderly;
s1576 infarcts, old thrombi (**phleboliths** — vein stones), dead parasites (echinococcus, trichinellae);
s1577 a dead fetus — **lithopedion** (“stone child”);
s1578 in some tumors — **psammoma bodies** (concentrically laminated calcium granules: papillary thyroid carcinoma, serous carcinoma of the ovary, meningioma).

s1583 **2. Metastatic calcification (calcium metastases)** — widespread deposition of calcium salts in **normal (healthy) tissues** as a result of **hypercalcemia**. Causes of hypercalcemia:
s1584 **excess parathyroid hormone** — parathyroid adenoma (**hyperparathyroidism**), production of a parathyroid hormone–related protein by tumors;
s1585 **bone destruction** — bone metastases, multiple myeloma, leukemias, prolonged immobilization, Paget disease;
s1586 **vitamin D intoxication**, sarcoidosis (macrophages activate vitamin D);
s1587 **chronic kidney disease** — phosphate retention develops, with secondary hyperparathyroidism. Calcium salts are deposited most often in organs that **excrete acid** and whose tissue is therefore **more alkaline**: the **lungs** (alveolar walls), the **gastric** mucosa (fundic glands), the **kidneys** (tubules — **nephrocalcinosis**), and also the **myocardium**, arterial walls and pulmonary veins. Calcium first accumulates inside cells — in mitochondria and phagolysosomes — and then in the intercellular substance.
s1588 Figure 4.10
s1589 Calcium metastases in the myocardium: A — calcified muscle fibers (black), microscopic view; B — calcium salts in mitochondrial cristae (electron micrograph) (Strukov, Serov, Fig. 2-23a)
s1590 **3. Metabolic (interstitial) calcification** — deposition of calcium when blood calcium is normal and there is no evident local tissue damage. The cause is the inability of tissue buffer systems to hold calcium (disturbed calcium–phosphorus metabolism). There are two types: **systemic** (generalized) — in the skin, subcutaneous tissue, tendons, fasciae and muscles; **limited** — calcium plates in the skin and subcutaneous tissue, most often of the fingers and toes (“**calcium gout**”). It is caused by disorders of calcium–phosphorus metabolism and diseases such as scleroderma.
s1591 **Changes in the calcium content of bone: osteoporosis** — a decrease in bone mass, the bones become brittle (old age, menopause, glucocorticoids, immobilization); **osteomalacia** and **rickets** — insufficient mineralization of the bone matrix in vitamin D deficiency.
s1592 Disorders of copper metabolism — Wilson–Konovalov disease
s1593 **Hepatocerebral degeneration (Wilson–Konovalov disease)** is an autosomal recessive disease: because of mutation of the **ATP7B** gene, excretion of copper into the bile and its incorporation into **ceruloplasmin** are impaired. Copper accumulates in the liver, then in the brain (especially the **subcortical nuclei** — the lentiform nucleus), the cornea and the kidneys.
s1594 **Liver**: fatty degeneration, chronic hepatitis, **liver cirrhosis** (sometimes acute liver failure);
s1595 **Brain**: death of neurons in the subcortical nuclei, cysts — tremor, movement disorders, mental changes;
s1596 **Eye**: a green-brown ring at the edge of the cornea — the **Kayser–Fleischer ring**. Serum ceruloplasmin is decreased, and urinary copper is increased. Copper in tissue is detected with **rhodanine**. Treatment — copper-chelating drugs (penicillamine) and liver transplantation.
s1597 Disorders of potassium metabolism
s1598 **Hyperkalemia** — kidney failure, Addison disease, extensive tissue necrosis (rhabdomyolysis), tumor lysis; risk of **cardiac arrest** (asystole).
s1599 **Hypokalemia** — diarrhea, vomiting, diuretics, hyperaldosteronism; muscle weakness, arrhythmias, **hypokalemic (vacuolar) nephropathy** in the kidney; **paroxysmal myoplegia** — episodic muscle paralysis in a hereditary disorder of potassium metabolism.
s1600 4.8. Stone formation
s1601 **Stones (concretions, calculi)** are **dense (hard) structures** formed from substances contained in fluids in the cavities of hollow organs or in the excretory ducts of glands. Stones form most often in the **gallbladder and bile ducts** (**cholelithiasis** — gallstone disease) and the **urinary tract** (**urolithiasis** — urinary stone disease), and less often in the salivary glands, pancreatic ducts, bronchi (**broncholiths**), intestine (**coproliths**) and vessels (**phleboliths** — calcified thrombi).
s1602 Causes and mechanism of stone formation:
s1603 **general factors** — metabolic disorders (of cholesterol, calcium, uric acid, oxalate, cystine), hereditary predisposition, dietary features, obesity;
s1604 **local factors** — **stasis** of secretions (impaired outflow from a cavity), **inflammation** (desquamated mucosal cells, bacteria and proteins become the **organic matrix** of the stone and a crystallization center), and changes in the composition and pH of secretions. Stone formation consists of two processes: formation of an **organic matrix (nucleus)** and **crystallization of salts** (precipitation) on it.

s1638 4.9. Clinicopathological section
s1639 One hemorrhage — three different pigments
s1640 A patient with a cerebral hemorrhage died of pneumonia 3 months later. At autopsy, in place of the hemorrhage a **cyst** with **rust-brown** walls filled with yellow-brown fluid was found. Microscopically: macrophages laden with **hemosiderin** (blue with the Perls reaction) in the cyst wall; in the central part — **orange hematoidin crystals** lying outside cells; in the glia around the cyst — **foamy macrophages** that have engulfed lipids (“granular bodies”). This picture reflects the age of the hemorrhage and the stages of its resorption.
s1641 Jaundice — where is the “breakdown”?
s1642 **A young woman** has anemia, an enlarged spleen, increased **unconjugated** bilirubin in the blood and no bilirubin in the urine — **hemolytic jaundice** (for example, hereditary spherocytosis). Prolonged hemolysis can lead to **pigment gallstones**.
s1643 **A student** develops jaundice after fever and weakness, ALT is 1500 U/L, and both fractions are increased in the blood — **hepatocellular jaundice** (viral hepatitis).
s1644 **A 65-year-old man** has gradually increasing painless jaundice, itching of the skin, colorless stools and an enlarged gallbladder (Courvoisier sign) — **obstructive jaundice**; the cause is most often **cancer of the head of the pancreas**.
s1645 **Metastatic calcification** is a serious problem in patients with chronic kidney disease on dialysis: calcification of vessel walls (cardiovascular complications), calcification of skin vessels (**calciphylaxis** — skin necrosis and ulcers). Therefore serum calcium, phosphorus and parathyroid hormone are regularly monitored in such patients.
s1646 **Pigments** (Latin pigmentum — paint) — substances that have their own color; they are exogenous and endogenous.
s1647 **Hemosiderin** — an iron-containing golden-brown pigment formed in macrophages during the breakdown of hemoglobin.
s1648 **Hemosiderosis** — excessive accumulation of hemosiderin in tissues (local or generalized).
s1649 **Hemochromatosis** — a disease characterized by accumulation of iron in parenchymal cells, which it damages (“bronze diabetes”).
s1650 **Hematoidin** — an iron-free orange crystalline pigment formed under anaerobic conditions in the center of a hemorrhage.
s1651 **Icterus (jaundice)** — yellow coloring of tissues as a result of hyperbilirubinemia.
s1652 **Kernicterus (nuclear jaundice)** — staining and damage of the subcortical nuclei of the brain by bilirubin in newborns.
s1653 **Siderophage** — a macrophage that has accumulated hemosiderin.
