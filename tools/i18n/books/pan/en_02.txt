s0161 1.4. Cellular responses to stress and injurious agents
s0162 A normal cell functions within a limited range of function and structure — in a state of **homeostasis**. Depending on the strength and duration of the load or injurious agent acting on it, the cell gives one of the following responses:
s0163 **Adaptation** — in response to physiological load or some pathological stimuli, the cell reaches a new steady state and preserves its viability: hypertrophy, hyperplasia, atrophy, metaplasia.
s0164 **Reversible injury** — when the limits of adaptation are exceeded or the stimulus is mild, functional and morphologic changes appear in the cell, but the cell recovers if the agent is removed.
s0165 **Irreversible injury and cell death** — if the stimulus is strong or prolonged, the cell passes the “point of no return” and dies by **necrosis** or **apoptosis** (Topic 2). In addition, chronic mild stimuli lead to intracellular **accumulation** of various substances (degenerations — Topics 3 and 4), **pathologic calcification** and **cellular aging**.

s0189 Causes of cell injury
s0190 **Oxygen deprivation (hypoxia)** is the most common cause. **Ischemia** (reduced arterial blood flow) is the most dangerous form of hypoxia: it stops not only the delivery of oxygen but also the delivery of nutrients and the removal of metabolites. Other causes of hypoxia are respiratory failure, anemia and carbon monoxide poisoning.
s0191 **Physical agents** — mechanical trauma, extremes of temperature, sudden changes in atmospheric pressure, ionizing radiation, electric current.
s0192 **Chemical agents and drugs** — poisons (arsenic, cyanide, mercury salts), alcohol, narcotic drugs, air pollutants, even high concentrations of physiological substances (glucose, table salt, oxygen), and toxic effects of drugs.
s0193 **Infectious agents** — prions, viruses, rickettsiae, bacteria, fungi, parasites.
s0194 **Immunologic reactions** — autoimmune diseases, allergic reactions, excessive inflammation against microbes.
s0195 **Genetic derangements** — congenital malformations (Down syndrome), enzyme deficiencies (storage diseases), misfolded proteins.
s0196 **Nutritional imbalances** — protein-energy malnutrition, vitamin deficiencies and, conversely, overnutrition (obesity, atherosclerosis).
s0197 **Aging** — decline of the replicative and reparative capacity of the cell.
s0198 1.5. Hypertrophy
s0199 **Hypertrophy** (Greek hyper — excess, trophe — nourishment) is an increase in the size of a tissue or organ due to **enlargement of its cells**. No new cells appear in a hypertrophied organ; the existing ones enlarge. Cell enlargement is due to increased synthesis of structural proteins and organelles (myofibrils, mitochondria).
s0200 **Mechanism.** Mechanical stretch (load), agonists (alpha-adrenergic agonists, angiotensin) and growth factors (IGF-1) act on membrane receptors and activate intracellular signaling pathways (PI3K/AKT, G protein–coupled receptors). As a result, transcription factors (GATA4, NFAT, MEF2) activate the genes of structural proteins. In hypertrophied cardiomyocytes, the synthesis of **fetal-type** proteins (for example, the beta isoform of the myosin heavy chain, natriuretic peptide) resumes.

s0219 Three stages are distinguished in the morphogenesis of **compensatory hypertrophy of the heart** (F.Z. Meerson):
s0220 **Emergency (injury) stage** — the load increases suddenly, the myocardium works under strain, and dystrophic changes and energy deficiency appear in cardiomyocytes.
s0221 **Compensation (stable hyperfunction) stage** — the number of myofibrils and mitochondria increases and the cells enlarge; the heart wall thickens, while its cavity narrows or stays unchanged — **concentric hypertrophy**. Function is adequately preserved.
s0222 **Decompensation (exhaustion) stage** — the capillaries can no longer supply the enlarged cells with enough blood; hypoxia, degeneration, death of cardiomyocytes and sclerosis develop. The cardiac cavities dilate (**myogenic dilatation**) — **eccentric hypertrophy**; heart failure begins.

s0231 1.6. Hyperplasia
s0232 **Hyperplasia** (Greek plasis — formation) is an **increase in the number of cells** in a tissue or organ. It occurs only in tissues made of cells capable of division. Hyperplasia develops through growth factor–driven proliferation of mature cells and, sometimes, through the formation of new cells from tissue stem cells.
s0233 Physiological hyperplasia:
s0234 **hormonal** — the glandular epithelium of the breast at puberty and in pregnancy, the uterus in pregnancy;
s0235 **compensatory** — after removal of part of the liver (for example, in living-donor transplantation), the remaining part grows back to its original size. The daily regeneration of Prometheus's liver in Greek myth is cited as an allusion to this phenomenon. **Pathologic hyperplasia** is most often the result of excessive action of hormones or growth factors:
s0236 **glandular hyperplasia of the endometrium** — when the balance between estrogen and progesterone is disturbed and estrogen predominates; clinically it manifests as acyclic uterine bleeding;
s0237 **benign prostatic hyperplasia** — under the influence of androgens (dihydrotestosterone);
s0238 **hyperplasia of the thyroid gland** — in Graves disease (stimulating antibodies to TSH receptors);
s0239 hyperplasia of fibroblasts and blood vessels in **wound healing** (under the influence of growth factors);
s0240 **viral hyperplasia** — warts and mucosal papillomas caused by papillomaviruses.
s0241 Pathologic hyperplasia is a **controlled** process: it stops when the hormonal or other stimulus disappears. This is its main difference from a tumor. However, because there is much cell division in the area of hyperplasia, the likelihood of mutations increases, so it can become **fertile soil for tumor development**. Women with atypical endometrial hyperplasia have a much higher risk of endometrial cancer; hyperplasias caused by papillomaviruses are also associated with cancer. Benign prostatic hyperplasia, however, is not considered a precancerous condition.

s0248 1.7. Atrophy
s0249 **Atrophy** (Greek a — negation, trophe — nourishment) is a reduction in the size of cells, tissues and organs **during life**, with weakening or cessation of their function. Atrophic cells have fewer structural proteins, mitochondria, myofilaments and less ER; the cell is smaller but alive. Atrophy can also be accompanied by a decrease in the number of cells.
s0250 **Mechanism:** decreased protein synthesis and increased protein degradation. Degradation occurs mainly through the **ubiquitin–proteasome system**: in nutrient deficiency and disuse, cellular proteins are “tagged” with ubiquitin and degraded in proteasomes. Atrophy is often accompanied by increased **autophagy** (“self-eating”): the cell digests its own organelles in autophagic vacuoles to obtain energy for survival. Undigested residues accumulate as the pigment **lipofuscin** — the organ becomes **brown** (**brown atrophy**).
s0251 Types of atrophy
s0252 **Physiological atrophy** occurs throughout life: obliteration of the umbilical arteries and the ductus arteriosus (Botallo's duct), involution of the thymus after puberty, and atrophy of the gonads, skin, bones (osteoporosis) and brain in old age (senile atrophy).
s0253 **Pathologic atrophy** occurs at any age and can be reversible or irreversible. By extent, it is **generalized** or **local**.
s0254 **Generalized atrophy — cachexia** (exhaustion, wasting). Its types:
s0255 **Alimentary** — starvation, impaired absorption of nutrients. In starvation the fat depots (subcutaneous tissue, omentum) atrophy first, then the skeletal muscles and internal organs (liver, heart), and the brain last of all — which is why a person dying of starvation remains conscious until the final moments.
s0256 **Cancer cachexia** — especially in tumors of the digestive tract; caused by tumor intoxication and cytokines (tumor necrosis factor — TNF).
s0257 **Dyshormonal** — pituitary insufficiency (Simmonds disease — pituitary cachexia), myxedema.
s0258 **Cerebral** — damage to the hypothalamus (tumor, inflammation).
s0259 In **chronic infections** (tuberculosis, brucellosis, chronic dysentery) and other severe chronic diseases. In cachexia the subcutaneous fat layer almost disappears, and the remaining adipose tissue becomes orange-brown due to the accumulation of **lipochrome**; the fat of the epicardium and bone marrow is soaked with serous fluid and becomes mucoid (**serous atrophy of adipose tissue**); the heart and liver become small and brown (**brown atrophy**).
