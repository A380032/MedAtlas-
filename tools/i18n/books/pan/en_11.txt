s1447 Hemosiderin and hemosiderosis
s1448 **Hemosiderin** is an iron-containing **golden-yellow or yellowish-brown granular** pigment. It is formed in **macrophages** (siderophages): the macrophage engulfs red blood cells (erythrophagocytosis), hemoglobin is broken down in lysosomes, and the released iron is converted to ferritin and then to hemosiderin. Cells that form hemosiderin are called **sideroblasts**, and macrophages that have accumulated it — **siderophages**. Hemosiderin stains **blue-green** with the **Perls reaction** (potassium ferrocyanide + hydrochloric acid) — **Prussian blue** is formed.
s1449 **Local hemosiderosis** — accumulation of hemosiderin in a limited area of tissue, in a focus of **hemorrhage**. Examples:
s1450 **a bruise (hematoma)**: its color changes from red-bluish (hemoglobin) → green (biliverdin) → yellow (bilirubin) → golden-brown (hemosiderin);
s1451 a **“rust-colored” cyst** in place of a **cerebral hemorrhage** — hemosiderin-laden macrophages in its wall and hematoidin crystals in the center;
s1452 **brown induration of the lung** — chronic venous congestion of the lung in left ventricular failure, especially in **mitral stenosis**: red blood cells pass into the alveoli by diapedesis, and alveolar macrophages engulf them and accumulate hemosiderin — these cells are called **“heart failure cells”** (heart defect cells); the lung becomes firm (**induration** — sclerosis) and brown (Topic 7).

s1454 Pulmonary hemosiderosis (Perls reaction): blue pigment granules in the cytoplasm of histiocytes and alveolar epithelium (sideroblasts and siderophages) (Strukov, Serov, Fig. 2-19)
s1455 Figure 4.1

s1457 **Generalized hemosiderosis** — accumulation of hemosiderin in many organs as a result of **excessive intravascular breakdown of red blood cells** (**hemolysis**). Hemosiderin accumulates in macrophages of the spleen, liver, bone marrow and lymph nodes (the **reticuloendothelial system**), and also in the epithelium of the liver, kidney and lung. The organs become **rust-brown**. Causes:
s1458 **hemolytic anemias** (hereditary: sickle cell anemia, thalassemia, spherocytosis; acquired: autoimmune);
s1459 **infections** — malaria, sepsis, relapsing fever;
s1460 poisoning with **hemolytic poisons** (Berthollet salt (potassium chlorate), phosphorus, mushrooms);
s1461 incompatible blood **transfusion**, multiple transfusions;
s1462 hemolytic disease of the newborn (Rh incompatibility).
s1463 Hemochromatosis
s1464 **Hemochromatosis** is **excessive accumulation of iron** in the organism, with deposition of this iron in **parenchymal cells** (hepatocytes, cardiomyocytes, pancreatic cells), which it **damages** (through free radicals — the Fenton reaction, Topic 2). Whereas the body normally contains 2–6 g of iron, in hemochromatosis it can exceed 50 g.
s1465 **Primary (hereditary) hemochromatosis** is an autosomal recessive disease, most often due to mutation of the **HFE** gene (C282Y). As a result, **hepcidin** (the liver hormone that regulates iron absorption)
s1466 decreases, and intestinal iron absorption increases continuously. It occurs more often and earlier in men (women lose iron through menstruation and pregnancy). • **Secondary hemochromatosis** — multiple transfusions (thalassemia), ineffective erythropoiesis, excessive iron intake, chronic liver diseases. **The clinicomorphologic triad:** 1) **pigment cirrhosis of the liver** (the liver is brown, with micronodular cirrhosis); 2) **diabetes mellitus** (damage to the pancreatic islets); 3) **skin pigmentation** (bronze color — hemosiderin and melanin). Therefore it is also called “**bronze diabetes**”. In addition, cardiomyopathy (arrhythmia, heart failure), hypogonadism and arthropathy are observed; the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma rises sharply.
s1467 Figure 4.3
s1468 Hereditary hemochromatosis (Perls reaction — Prussian blue): iron in hepatocytes in the form of blue granules; the parenchymal architecture is still normal (Robbins, 9th ed., Fig. 18-25)
s1469 If detected early, hemochromatosis is a disease that can be completely prevented by regular **bloodletting (phlebotomy)** — that is why serum ferritin and transferrin saturation are checked in patients' family members. In secondary hemochromatosis (thalassemia), iron-chelating drugs (**deferoxamine**) are used.
s1470 Bilirubin and jaundice
s1471 **Bilirubin** is the main end product of hemoglobin breakdown, a red-yellow pigment that **contains no iron**. The stages of its metabolism:
s1472 In macrophages, **biliverdin** and then **bilirubin** are formed from heme.
s1473 Water-insoluble **unconjugated (indirect)** bilirubin binds to **albumin** in the blood and is carried to the liver.
s1474 In hepatocytes it is **conjugated with glucuronic acid** by the enzyme **glucuronyltransferase** — water-soluble **conjugated (direct)** bilirubin is formed.
s1475 Conjugated bilirubin is excreted with bile into the intestine, where it is converted to **stercobilinogen** (stool color); part of it is absorbed and excreted in the urine as **urobilinogen**.
s1476 **Jaundice (icterus)** is the yellow coloring of the skin, sclera, mucous membranes, serous membranes and internal organs resulting from increased bilirubin in the blood (**hyperbilirubinemia**). Clinical jaundice becomes visible when blood bilirubin exceeds **34 µmol/L (2 mg/dL)** (normally up to 20 µmol/L).

s1496 **Hematoidin** is an iron-free pigment chemically close to bilirubin, forming **bright orange crystals** (rhomboid plates or needles). It forms **outside cells**, in places that oxygen does not reach — in the center of old hematomas and infarcts. Hematoidin and hemosiderin often occur together: hematoidin in the center of the focus, hemosiderin at the periphery.
s1497 **Hematins** are an oxidized form of hemoglobin (heme), **dark brown or black** fine granules or crystals:
s1498 **hemomelanin (malarial pigment)** — formed by the malaria plasmodium breaking down hemoglobin in the red blood cell; it accumulates in macrophages of the spleen, liver, bone marrow and brain and gives the organs a **gray-black (slate)** color;
s1499 **hydrochloric acid hematin (hematin hydrochloride)** — formed by the action of hydrochloric acid on hemoglobin in hemorrhages and erosions of the gastric mucosa; it makes the floor of the erosion black, and the vomitus looks like **“coffee grounds”**;
s1500 **formalin pigment** — an artifact (dark brown needles) formed when blood-rich tissues are fixed in acidic formalin; it must be distinguished from pathologic pigments. **Porphyrins** are iron-free precursors of heme. In hereditary disorders of heme synthesis (**porphyrias**), porphyrins accumulate: the skin becomes extremely sensitive to light (blisters, ulcers, scars), the urine turns **red**, and the teeth and bones become reddish-brown; in acute intermittent porphyria, attacks of abdominal pain and neurological disturbances occur.
s1501 4.3. Proteinogenic (tyrosinogenic) pigments
s1502 These pigments include **melanin, adrenochrome** and the **pigment of enterochromaffin cells**.
s1503 **Melanin** (Greek melas — black) is a **brown-black** pigment. It is synthesized in **melanosomes** of **melanocytes** (melaninocytes) of neuroectodermal origin from the amino acid **tyrosine** via dihydroxyphenylalanine (**DOPA**), with the participation of the enzyme **tyrosinase**. Melanocytes are located in the basal layer of the epidermis, hair follicles, the iris and retina, the pia mater and the substantia nigra of the brain. Melanin synthesis is increased by pituitary melanocyte-stimulating hormone (MSH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and also by ultraviolet rays. Melanin is also often found in **melanophages** (macrophages that have engulfed melanin).
s1504 Melanin is detected with histochemical reactions: the **DOPA reaction** (melanocyte activity) and the **Fontana–Masson** method (black color with silver nitrate); it is **Perls-negative** and is **bleached** by oxidizing agents (hydrogen peroxide) — this distinguishes it from hemosiderin and lipofuscin.

s1529 **Adrenochrome** is an oxidation product of epinephrine (adrenaline) in the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla; it gives the **chromaffin reaction** (brown color with chromium salts). **Pheochromocytoma** (a catecholamine-producing tumor with attacks of arterial hypertension) develops from these cells.

s1531 4.4. Lipidogenic pigments (lipopigments)
s1532 Lipidogenic pigments are fat-protein (lipid-protein) substances; they include **lipofuscin, lipochrome, ceroid** and the **vitamin D pigment**.
s1533 **Lipofuscin** (Latin fuscus — brown) is the **“aging pigment”**, the **“wear-and-tear pigment”**. It is formed during **autophagy**: undigested membrane lipids undergo peroxidation under the action of free radicals and, together with proteins, form insoluble residual bodies (**telolysosomes**). Lipofuscin is a **yellowish-brown, finely granular** pigment, often located **around the nucleus**
s1534 (perinuclear). It stains orange with Sudan III and blue-green with the Schmorl reaction; Perls-negative.
s1535 Lipofuscin **does not itself damage the cell**, but it is a sign of **free radical injury** and cellular **aging**. It accumulates in large amounts in the **heart, liver, neurons** and skeletal muscles in the elderly, in cachexia and in chronic diseases — the organ develops **brown atrophy** (Topic 1).
s1536 Figure 4.5
s1537 Lipofuscin granules in a cardiomyocyte: A — light microscopy (arrow — yellowish-brown granules around the nucleus); B — electron microscopy (perinuclear, intralysosomal location) (Robbins, 9th ed., Fig. 2-33)
s1538 **Lipochrome** — **carotenoids** (pigments taken in with food) that give a yellow color to adipose tissue, the adrenal cortex, blood serum and the corpus luteum of the ovary. In cachexia, when adipose tissue decreases, the concentration of lipochrome increases and the remaining fat becomes **orange**; the yellowish tint of the skin in diabetes mellitus (xanthosis) is also related to this pigment.
s1539 **Ceroid** — a pigment close to lipofuscin formed as a result of oxidation of fats in macrophages (for example, in vitamin E deficiency, in the intestinal mucosa, in endometriosis).
s1540 Distinguishing the three brown pigments is the task most often given in questions on microscopic slides:
s1541 **Hemosiderin** — golden-brown, coarsely granular; **Perls-positive (blue)**; in hemorrhage and hemolysis.
s1542 **Lipofuscin** — yellowish-brown, finely granular, **around the nucleus**; Perls-negative, Sudan-positive; in aging and atrophy.
s1543 **Melanin** — brown-black; Perls-negative, Fontana–Masson-positive, bleached by hydrogen peroxide; in the skin, eye and melanocytic tumors.
s1544 4.5. Exogenous pigments
s1545 **Anthracosis** (Greek anthrax — coal) — **coal dust** that enters through the respiratory tract, is engulfed by alveolar macrophages and accumulates in the lung tissue and hilar lymph nodes. In city dwellers and smokers the lungs have black spots beneath the pleura, and the lymph nodes are black. In coal miners large amounts of dust accumulate and fibrosis develops — **anthracotic pneumoconiosis**; if silicon is added to the dust — **anthracosilicosis**.
s1546 **Tattoo** — ink introduced into the dermis of the skin is engulfed by dermal macrophages and persists for life.
s1547 **Argyria** — gray-bluish discoloration of the skin with prolonged intake of silver compounds.
