s0235 The bacterial ribosome is **70S** (30S + 50S), the human ribosome is **80S**. Aminoglycosides and tetracyclines act on 30S, macrolides and chloramphenicol (levomycetin) on 50S, stopping only bacterial protein synthesis.
s0236 The **LPS (endotoxin)** of gram-negative bacteria is released when the cell breaks down and causes fever, a fall in blood pressure, DIC syndrome and septic shock. Gram-positive bacteria, in contrast, mainly release exotoxins (proteins).
s0237 The most important and most commonly mishandled step is **decolorizing with alcohol**. If left in alcohol for too long, Gram+ cells also lose their color and give a false Gram− result; if decolorized too briefly, Gram− cells stay purple and give a false Gram+ result.
s0238 Remember the variants by their root: **SERO**var — **sero**logy (antigen); **PHAGO**var — **phag**e; **FERMENT**ovar — **ferment**; **CHEMO**var — **chem**ical.
s0239 Permanent structures of the bacterial cell
s0240 A bacterium is a **prokaryote**: it has no nucleus enclosed by a nuclear membrane and no membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi).
s0241 Figure 2.1
s0242 Structure of the bacterial cell: capsule, cell wall, periplasmic space and membrane, nucleoid, ribosomes, mesosome, inclusions, flagellum. Source: Aliyev Sh.R. et al., 2013.
s0243 **Permanent structures** (present in almost all bacteria):
s0244 **Cell wall** — determines shape, provides resistance to osmotic pressure, and determines Gram staining and many antigenic properties. Its basis is **peptidoglycan (murein)**.
s0245 **Cytoplasmic membrane (CPM)** — a phospholipid bilayer; selective permeability, transport of nutrients, respiratory enzymes and ATP synthesis (bacteria have no mitochondria — energy is produced in the CPM). Its inward folding is the **mesosome**.
s0246 **Cytoplasm** — a colloidal medium; enzymes, RNA, reserve substances.
s0247 **Nucleoid** — a single circular double-stranded DNA (chromosome), without histones and without a nuclear membrane.
s0248 **Ribosomes** — of the **70S** type (30S + 50S subunits); the site of protein synthesis. Their difference from the eukaryotic ribosome (80S) is the basis for the selective action of many antibiotics.
s0249 **Plasmids** — small circular DNA outside the chromosome that replicates independently (antibiotic resistance, toxin genes — in detail in topic 11).
s0250 **Chemical composition:** water (≈ 80%), proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, lipids and minerals. The composition of the cell wall (peptidoglycan, teichoic acids or LPS) depends on the Gram group.
s0251 The cell wall: gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria
s0252 **Peptidoglycan (murein)** — alternately linked chains of N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid; they are cross-linked by short peptide bridges to form a net-like layer. This cross-linking is carried out by the enzyme **transpeptidase** (penicillin-binding protein) — the target of beta-lactam antibiotics.

s0280 **Special cases:**
s0281 **Acid-fast** bacteria (mycobacteria — tuberculosis, leprosy): their wall contains a lot of **mycolic acid** and wax; they stain poorly by Gram, so they are stained by the **Ziehl–Neelsen** method (red rods on a blue background).
s0282 **Mycoplasmas** — have no cell wall at all; variable shape, naturally resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics.
s0283 **L-forms** — temporary forms whose wall is partially lost (for example, under the action of penicillin).
s0284 Gram staining
s0285 The Gram method (1884, Christian Gram) is the most important differential stain in bacteriology. The difference in staining is based on the structure of the cell wall: thick peptidoglycan is dehydrated by alcohol, its pores narrow, and it retains the **gentian violet–iodine** complex (Gram+); Gram− cells, with thin peptidoglycan and an outer membrane that dissolves in alcohol, lose this complex and then turn red with the counterstain (fuchsin).
s0286 Figure 2.4
s0287 Steps of Gram staining (the algorithm from the laboratory manual). Source: Aliyev Sh.R. et al., 2013.
s0288 **Steps:** 1) gentian violet (1–2 min) — all cells turn purple; 2) Lugol’s solution (iodine — the fixative, 1 min) — the dye–iodine complex forms; 3) alcohol (decolorizer, 10–20 s) — Gram− cells are decolorized; 4) rinse with water, then fuchsin (counterstain, 1–2 min) — Gram− cells turn red. Result: **Gram+ — purple, Gram− — red (pink).**
s0289 Classification and nomenclature of bacteria and Bergey’s Manual
s0290 Bacteria are classified by **taxonomic units**: domain → phylum → class → order → **family** → **genus** → **species**.
s0291 **Nomenclature is binomial (two-name):** each species is named with two Latin words: the first is the genus name (capitalized), the second is the species name (lowercase), both in italics: for example, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Escherichia coli*, *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*.
s0292 **Bergey’s Manual** (*Bergey’s Manual of Determinative Bacteriology*) is the main reference that helps identify bacteria by their morphological, tinctorial (Gram), cultural and biochemical features. In practice, bacteria are identified by following this sequence of features.

s0307 Practical skill: the Gram staining algorithm
s0308 Place a drop of physiological saline on a clean slide, prepare a smear from the culture to be examined with a loop, and air-dry it.
s0309 **Fix** the smear by passing it 2–3 times over the flame (this fixes the cells to the glass and kills them).
s0310 Stain with gentian violet for 1–2 minutes.
s0311 Pour off the dye and apply Lugol’s solution for 1 minute.
s0312 Decolorize with alcohol for 10–20 seconds, until no more color comes off; rinse immediately with water.
s0313 Counterstain with fuchsin for 1–2 minutes, rinse and dry.
s0314 Examine with the immersion objective (100×): purple — Gram+, red — Gram−; record the shape and arrangement.

s0402 Which Gram group does the bacterium belong to and what is its wall structure?
s0403 A gram-negative (Gram−) rod — it has thin peptidoglycan, an outer membrane and LPS (endotoxin). Enteric rods (Enterobacteriaceae — for example, *E. coli*) are gram-negative.
s0404 While performing Gram staining, a student kept the alcohol decolorization step far too long (1 minute). As a result, a known gram-positive culture was also stained red.
s0405 What error occurred and how is the result assessed?
s0406 Over-decolorization with alcohol — the thick peptidoglycan also lost the gentian violet–iodine complex, and the gram-positive cells looked falsely gram-negative (red). The result is incorrect; the staining must be repeated.
s0407 A patient was prescribed an antibiotic of the penicillin group, but it had no effect on the causative agent (a mycoplasma).
s0408 Why was penicillin ineffective?
s0409 Mycoplasmas have no cell wall (peptidoglycan), while penicillin blocks peptidoglycan synthesis (transpeptidase). With no wall, the drug has no target — which is why mycoplasmas are naturally resistant to beta-lactams (macrolides and tetracyclines act on them).

s0432 A spore does not take up an ordinary dye: in an ordinary smear it appears as an **unstained white space**. With the Ozheshko method, however, the **spore is red and the vegetative cell is blue**.
s0433 Remember the location by the shape: **terminal — drumstick** (tetanus), **subterminal — racket** (botulism), **central — the shape does not change** (anthrax).
s0434 In a cerebrospinal fluid smear, encapsulated gram-positive diplococci raise suspicion of pneumococcal meningitis; in sputum, large encapsulated gram-negative rods (a slimy, dome-shaped colony) raise suspicion of Klebsiella pneumonia.
s0435 In a hanging drop, **true motility** must be distinguished from **Brownian motion**: in Brownian motion the cells merely vibrate in place, whereas in true motility they cross the field of view and change position.
s0436 General concept of nonpermanent structures
s0437 Permanent structures (cell wall, cytoplasmic membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid, ribosomes) are present in every bacterium. **Nonpermanent structures**, by contrast, occur only in some species or only under certain conditions. They are not essential for the bacterium’s survival, but they serve as an **important differential feature in identifying the species**, and for this reason they are studied separately in the practical class.

s0468 **Spore** — a resting form produced under unfavorable conditions by some gram-positive rods (*Bacillus* — aerobes, *Clostridium* — anaerobes). It is a form of **preservation, not reproduction**: one spore forms from one cell, and one vegetative cell grows back from the spore.
s0469 **Structure.** In the center are dehydrated cytoplasm and the nucleoid, surrounded by the cortex, a multilayered coat and sometimes an exosporium. There is almost no free water, and there is a lot of the **calcium salt of dipicolinic acid** — because of this, metabolism in the spore stops and it is much more resistant than the vegetative cell. That is why in medicine the reliability of sterilization is assessed precisely by the **destruction of spores** (see topic 9: autoclave and biological indicators).
s0470 **Spore location** — a morphological feature used in identifying the species:
s0471 **central** — in the middle of the cell, does not change the shape of the cell (anthrax bacillus);
s0472 **subterminal** — near the end, giving a “tennis racket” appearance (the causative agent of botulism);
s0473 **terminal** — at the very end, giving a **“drumstick bacillus”** appearance (the causative agent of tetanus).
s0474 If the spore diameter is greater than the width of the cell, the cell swells and takes the **clostridial (spindle-shaped)** form; if smaller, the **bacillus** form is retained.
s0475 The Ozheshko method (spore staining) — procedure
s0476 A smear is prepared and air-dried.
s0477 A 0.5% solution of hydrochloric acid is dropped onto the preparation and heated for 1–2 minutes — the spore coat becomes permeable to the dye.
