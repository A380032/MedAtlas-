s2400 6.1. Ways of transfer of genetic material (recombination)
s2401 **Transformation** — a bacterium takes up **free DNA** from the environment. For this the cell must be in a special state (competence). Historical significance: the experiments of Griffith (1928) and Avery proved that DNA is the hereditary material.
s2402 **Transduction** — the transfer of genes by a **bacteriophage**. In **general (generalized)** transduction any piece of the chromosome is transferred; in **specialized** transduction, particular genes next to the prophage are transferred.
s2403 **Conjugation** — two cells in direct contact transfer DNA (often a plasmid) through a **sex (F) pilus**. The donor is an F⁺ or Hfr cell, the recipient is F⁻.

s2422 **IS elements (insertion sequences)** are the simplest transposable elements, containing only the gene required for their own transposition. They move from place to place in the genome and can inactivate genes or, conversely, activate them.
s2423 **Transposons**, unlike IS elements, carry **additional genes** besides the transposition genes (often an antibiotic resistance gene or a toxin gene). They move between the chromosome and plasmids and speed up the spread of resistance genes. **Integrons** are systems that “collect” resistance gene cassettes and play an important role in the development of multidrug resistance.
s2424 **Temperate phages and lysogeny.** A temperate phage does not lyse the bacterium immediately but integrates its genome into the chromosome and is passed from generation to generation as a **prophage** (a **lysogenic culture**). If the prophage genes give the bacterium a new property, this is called **phage conversion**.
s2425 **The most important clinical examples:** toxin production by the diphtheria agent is controlled by a prophage gene — **only lysogenic strains are toxigenic**; the toxin genes of the cholera vibrio and of some *Shigella*/*E. coli* strains are also phage-associated.
s2426 The toxigenicity of the diphtheria bacillus is a classic example of **phage conversion**. That is why, in the diagnosis of diphtheria, it is essential to **determine the toxigenicity** of the isolated culture separately (topic 18).
s2427 Practical skill: choosing the method and material according to the stage of the disease
s2428 **Determine the stage of the disease:** incubation, prodromal, peak or recovery. At each stage the pathogen is in different places in the body.
s2429 **In the early stage**, look for the pathogen itself: take the material **before antibiotics** and choose a bacteriological or molecular method.
s2430 **From week 2**, assess the body’s response: for the serological method take **paired sera** (at the beginning of the illness and after 10–14 days).
s2431 **Choose the material:** according to where the pathogen is multiplying — blood (bacteremia), stool (intestine), sputum (respiratory tract), cerebrospinal fluid (CNS), urine, wound discharge.
s2432 **Collect and transport the sample correctly:** a sterile container, a transport medium if necessary; for fragile pathogens (meningococcus, gonococcus) — warm and fast.
s2433 **Fill in the referral:** clinical diagnosis, date of onset of the illness, whether antibiotics have been taken, vaccination history.
s2434 **Interpret the result:** not a single positive serological result but the **dynamics of the titer**; and with PCR, take into account that live and dead microbes are not distinguished.
