s4119 Airborne infections: the causative agents of tuberculosis and leprosy. Actinomycosis and atypical mycobacteria

s4125 **Ziehl–Neelsen result:** the acid-fast rods are **red (carbol fuchsin)**; the background and other cells are **blue (methylene blue)**.
s4126 In tuberculosis there is **no exotoxin**. The picture of the disease is determined mainly by the **delayed-type (type IV) hypersensitivity immune response** and the formation of granulomas. That is why tuberculosis runs as an “immunological disease”: tissue damage is largely the result of the body’s own response.
s4127 A positive Mantoux test does not mean **“tuberculosis disease”**: it shows that the body has met mycobacterial antigens. The diagnosis is made from a combination of clinical findings, X-ray and microbiological results.
s4128 The triad of actinomycosis: **chronic dense infiltrate + fistulas + yellow druses in the pus**.
s4129 General characteristics of mycobacteria
s4130 **Mycobacteria** (*Mycobacterium*) are slender, slightly curved rods; nonmotile, nonspore-forming, noncapsulated. Their main distinguishing feature is the special composition of the cell wall: it contains very large amounts of **mycolic acids**, waxes and lipids (40–60% of the wall is lipid).
s4131 **Acid-fastness (acid resistance)** — after staining they are not decolorized by acid and alcohol; for this reason the Gram method is unsuitable and the **Ziehl–Neelsen** method is used;
s4132 resistance to environmental factors, to many disinfectants and to drying;
s4133 **slow growth** (generation time 14–24 hours) — in culture the result is obtained in **3–8 weeks**;
s4134 the ability to survive inside the phagocyte (**incomplete phagocytosis**).
s4135 The causative agent of tuberculosis — *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*
s4136 **Historical note:** discovered in 1882 by **Robert Koch** (hence it is also called “Koch’s bacillus”).
s4137 **Morphology:** a slender, straight or slightly curved rod; in smears it lies singly, in pairs or in groups, sometimes **branching**. Under unfavorable conditions (antibacterial treatment, immune pressure) it can change into **L-forms**, filterable and granular forms — which matters in relapse of the disease.
s4138 Figure 19.1
s4139 *M. tuberculosis* in a sputum smear: red rods stained by the Ziehl–Neelsen method on a blue background. Source: Muhamedov I.M. et al., 2019.
s4140 **Cultural properties:** **obligate aerobe**; grown on **egg-based Löwenstein–Jensen** medium with added glycerin, or on Finn-2 medium. Growth takes **3–8 weeks**; the colonies are dry, rough, **“cauliflower”** or “bread-crumb” in appearance, cream-colored (**R-form — a sign of virulence**). In liquid medium, owing to **cord factor**, it grows in “rope”-like (ribbon-like) strands.
s4141 **Cord factor** (trehalose-6,6-dimycolate) — the main marker of virulence; it stops phagosome–lysosome fusion and damages macrophages;
s4142 **Sulfatides** — block the formation of the phagolysosome;
s4143 **Mycolic acids and lipids** — resistance and granuloma formation;
s4144 **Tuberculin (a protein antigen)** — causes the immune-allergic response.
s4145 **Pathogenesis:** mycobacteria that enter by the airborne route are engulfed by alveolar macrophages, but because of **incomplete phagocytosis** they survive and multiply in them. As a result a **specific granuloma** forms (caseous necrosis in the center, surrounded by epithelioid cells and Pirogov–Langhans giant cells, and lymphocytes). In most people the process is contained at this stage — **latent infection**; when immunity falls (HIV, diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, immunosuppressive therapy, old age) **reactivation** occurs.
s4146 **Clinical forms:** pulmonary tuberculosis (the most frequent) and extrapulmonary forms — lymph nodes, bone and joint, kidney, intestine, meningitis, disseminated (miliary) tuberculosis.
s4147 Laboratory diagnosis of tuberculosis
s4148 **Material:** sputum (morning, 2–3 times, with a deep cough), bronchial washings, gastric washings (in children), urine, cerebrospinal fluid, puncture fluid, tissue biopsy.
s4149 4.1. Bacterioscopic method
s4150 Staining by the **Ziehl–Neelsen** method — rapid and inexpensive; its limitation is low sensitivity (about 10⁴–10⁵ cells per 1 ml of material are needed);
s4151 **Fluorescence microscopy** (auramine–rhodamine) — more sensitive and convenient for viewing many samples;
s4152 **Enrichment methods** — **flotation** (shaking with xylene/benzine and making a smear from the layer that rises to the surface) and **homogenization/sedimentation** — raise the sensitivity.
s4153 Figure 19.2
s4154 Scheme of the bacterioscopic examination in tuberculosis: material, Ziehl–Neelsen staining, the fluorescence method, enrichment (flotation and homogenization). Source: Aliyev Sh.R. et al., 2013.
s4155 4.2. Bacteriological (cultural) method — the gold standard
s4156 The material is subjected to **decontamination** with alkali or acid (to remove the accompanying flora; mycobacteria survive because they are resistant), neutralized and centrifuged.
s4157 The sediment is inoculated onto **Löwenstein–Jensen** (or Finn-2) medium; modern laboratories use automated **liquid medium systems** (the result comes much faster).
s4158 At 37 °C for 3–8 weeks, observed weekly.
s4159 Identification: growth rate, colony appearance, **cord factor**, the **niacin test** (positive for *M. tuberculosis*), nitrate reductase, pigment formation; modern methods — molecular identification and MALDI-TOF.
s4160 **Antibiotic susceptibility** — by the cultural or molecular method (resistance genes).

s4162 Scheme of the bacteriological examination in tuberculosis: decontamination, inoculation onto Löwenstein–Jensen and Finn-2 media, cord factor, niacin test, antibiotic susceptibility. Source: Aliyev Sh.R. et al., 2013.
s4163 4.3. Molecular and immunological methods
s4164 **Rapid molecular tests (PCR-based)** — detect the pathogen and, at the same time, the **genes for resistance to the main drugs** within a few hours; at present one of the first-line methods;
s4165 **Tuberculin test (Mantoux)** — tuberculin is injected into the skin and the papule is measured after 72 hours; it shows **infection (sensitization)**, not disease. It can also be positive after BCG vaccination;
s4166 **Blood tests based on IFN-γ release (IGRA)** — independent of BCG, used to detect latent infection;
s4167 **Biological test** — now rarely used.
s4168 **Prophylaxis:** **BCG** — a live attenuated vaccine; it is important for protection against severe disseminated forms. Additional measures: early detection, isolation of the source, examination of contacts, social and hygienic measures.
s4169 The causative agent of leprosy — *Mycobacterium leprae*
s4170 **Features:** an acid-fast rod; **it does not grow on artificial nutrient media at all** — this is its main laboratory feature. In experiments it is propagated in armadillos and in the mouse footpad. It multiplies very slowly (generation time 12–14 days), and the incubation period is several years.
s4171 **Morphological feature:** in tissue and mucosal scrapings it lies **inside cells, in orderly bundles resembling “cigarettes in a pack”** (globi).
s4172 **Clinical forms:** **lepromatous** (weak immune response, many organisms — more contagious) and **tuberculoid** (strong cell-mediated immunity, few organisms), as well as intermediate forms. The skin, peripheral nerves and mucous membranes are mainly affected; nerve damage leads to loss of sensation and deformities.
s4173 **Diagnosis:** nasal mucosal scrapings, material from skin lesions, biopsy → **Ziehl–Neelsen** → seeing orderly intracellular bundles; the **lepromin test** (to assess the type of immune response, not for diagnosis); PCR.
s4174 Independent work: Actinomycosis and atypical mycobacteria
s4175 6.1. Actinomycosis
s4176 **Causative agent:** *Actinomyces israelii* and other actinomycetes — gram-positive, branching filamentous **bacteria** (topic 4), members of the normal flora of the oral cavity, intestine and urogenital tract.
s4177 **Pathogenesis:** an **endogenous** disease — when the mucosa is damaged (tooth extraction, jaw injury, appendicitis, an intrauterine device) the organism enters the tissue; under anaerobic conditions chronic **granulomatous** inflammation, a dense infiltrate and **fistulas** develop.
s4178 **Forms:** orofacial (the most frequent), thoracic, abdominal, urogenital, and central nervous system forms.
s4179 **Diagnosis:** fistula discharge or tissue → looking for visible yellow **druses** → microscopy (mycelium in the center of the druse, a radiating structure at the edge) → **anaerobic culture** (grows slowly, 7–14 days) → identification. Because actinomycetes are bacteria, they **respond to antibacterial treatment**.
s4180 6.2. Atypical (nontuberculous) mycobacteria
s4181 **General name:** NTM — mycobacteria that do not belong to the tuberculosis group. Their natural habitat is **water, soil, water pipes and moist environments**; they are hardly ever transmitted from person to person (the main epidemiological difference from tuberculosis).
s4182 **Classic groups by Runyon:** photochromogens (produce pigment in light — *M. kansasii*), scotochromogens (pigmented even in the dark — *M. scrofulaceum*), nonphotochromogens (*M. avium* complex), and **rapid growers** (*M. fortuitum*, *M. chelonae* — grow within up to 7 days).
s4183 **Clinical significance:** disseminated infection in the immunocompromised (especially in HIV infection — *M. avium* complex); pulmonary forms on the background of chronic lung disease; cervical lymphadenitis in children; skin and soft-tissue infections after surgical and cosmetic procedures (rapid-growing species).
s4184 **Diagnostic particularity:** finding an acid-fast rod does not in itself mean tuberculosis — **the species must be identified** (molecular methods, MALDI-TOF). Atypical mycobacteria are naturally resistant to most antituberculosis drugs, so the treatment regimen is entirely different.
s4185 When an acid-fast rod is found in sputum, **two questions** are asked: (1) is it *M. tuberculosis* or an atypical mycobacterium? (2) is it drug-resistant? Modern molecular tests answer both questions quickly.
s4186 Practical skill: collecting sputum for tuberculosis testing
s4187 The patient is told: the material needed is **not saliva but sputum brought up by a deep cough**.
s4188 The sample is collected **in the morning, on an empty stomach**, after rinsing the mouth with water.
s4189 The patient breathes in deeply, coughs forcefully and spits the sputum into a **wide-mouthed sterile container**; the volume is usually 3–5 ml.
s4190 The sample is delivered to the laboratory in a tightly closed container, **upright**, cooled (2–8 °C).
s4191 Usually **2–3** samples are collected (on different days or according to a set scheme) — this raises sensitivity.
s4192 In the laboratory: a smear (Ziehl–Neelsen or fluorescence), decontamination and culture, a rapid molecular test.
s4193 Safety: the material can generate aerosols — the work is done **in a special cabinet**, with protective equipment.
