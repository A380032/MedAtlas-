s4704 The Vi antigen is the **“virulence” antigen**: it protects the live bacterium from agglutination with O serum. Therefore, if there is no O agglutination in serological identification, the culture is heated (the Vi antigen is destroyed) and the reaction is repeated.
s4705 In typhoid fever the rule **“stage — material”** is basic: **week 1 — blood** (hemoculture), **from week 2 — stool and urine** (coproculture, urinoculture), **from week 2 — serum** (Widal/PHA), **after recovery — bile** (biliculture, detecting carriage).
s4706 A single positive Widal reaction is not enough for a final diagnosis: it can be positive in a vaccinated person or one who has had the disease before. The sign that has diagnostic value is **at least a 4-fold rise in titer in paired sera** (topic 11).
s4707 The causative agents of typhoid fever and paratyphoid
s4708 **Causative agents:** *Salmonella* Typhi (typhoid fever), *S.* Paratyphi A and *S.* Paratyphi B.
s4709 **Morphology and properties:** gram-negative, **peritrichous** (motile) rods; nonspore-forming, noncapsulated (the Vi antigen is present). **Lactose-negative**, they ferment glucose; *S.* Typhi **produces no gas**, whereas the paratyphoids **produce gas**; *S.* Typhi produces little H₂S, paratyphoid B a lot.
s4710 **Antigenic structure (based on the Kauffmann–White scheme):**
s4711 **O antigen** — somatic, heat-stable; determines the group (*S.* Typhi — group D, Paratyphi A — A, Paratyphi B — B);
s4712 **H antigen** — flagellar, heat-labile;
s4713 **Vi antigen** — a surface (capsule-type) antigen; associated with **virulence** and “masks” the O antigen; especially important **in detecting carriage**.
s4714 **Pathogenesis — the stages (this determines diagnostic tactics!):**
s4715 **Entry and multiplication in the intestine** (incubation period, 7–14 days): the bacteria enter the mucosa of the small intestine and multiply in Peyer’s patches and the mesenteric lymph nodes.
s4716 **Bacteremia** (week 1): the bacteria pass into the blood — the clinical picture begins (fever, headache, intoxication, relative bradycardia, a roseolar rash on the abdomen).
s4717 **Parenchymatous dissemination** (week 2): they settle in the liver, spleen, bone marrow and gallbladder.
s4718 **Excretion stage** (weeks 2–3): they are excreted from the gallbladder with bile into the intestine and through the kidney into the urine; **necrosis and ulcers** form in Peyer’s patches owing to sensitization — complications (intestinal bleeding, perforation) occur precisely in this period.
s4719 **Recovery or transition to carriage:** the pathogen can persist for a long time in the gallbladder (especially in gallstone disease), and **chronic carriage** may develop.

s4738 **Hemoculture technique:** 5–10 ml of blood is drawn from a vein and inoculated into a **10-fold larger** volume of liquid medium (Rappoport — a medium containing bile, glucose and an indicator). s4752 **Types of carriage:** acute (up to 3 months), **chronic** (over 3 months), transitory.

s4777 Independent work 2: Viral diarrheas
s4778 6.1. Rotavirus
s4779 **Features:** the family **Reoviridae**; double-stranded, **segmented RNA**; nonenveloped, resistant; on electron microscopy it has a **“wheel”** appearance (Latin *rota* — wheel).
s4780 **Epidemiology and clinical picture:** in children (especially 6 months to 2 years) **the most frequent cause of acute gastroenteritis**; fecal-oral route; seasonality — the cold period. Clinically: vomiting, **watery diarrhea**, fever; the main danger is **dehydration**.
s4781 **Diagnosis:** **antigen detection (ELISA, immunochromatographic rapid tests)** in stool — the main method in practice; PCR; electron microscopy (research).
s4782 **Prophylaxis:** **live oral rotavirus vaccine** — in infancy; it significantly reduces severe forms and hospitalizations.
s4783 6.2. Adenovirus (enteric types)
s4784 **Features:** **Adenoviridae**; double-stranded DNA, nonenveloped, icosahedral capsid, with projections (fibers).

s4787 **Clinical picture:** the enteric types (40, 41) cause longer-lasting diarrhea in children; other types — respiratory infection, conjunctivitis, pharyngoconjunctival fever.
s4788 **Diagnosis:** stool antigen test, PCR.

s4790 Practical skill: collecting a hemoculture
s4791 Blood is taken **during a rise in fever** and **before antibiotics**.
s4792 The skin is treated according to aseptic rules (wait until the antiseptic has dried) — this prevents **contamination**.
s4793 5–10 ml of blood (an age-appropriate volume in children) is drawn from a vein with a sterile syringe.
s4794 The blood is immediately inoculated into a **10-fold larger** volume of medium (Rappoport or bile broth; in modern settings, automated bottles).
s4795 The bottle is placed at 37 °C; it is observed for signs of growth (turbidity, color change, gas), or the automated system gives a signal.
s4796 From a positive bottle a smear (Gram) and a subculture onto solid medium are made; then identification and an antibiogram.
s4797 For reliability, usually **several samples from different sites** are taken.
s4798 Inoculating blood into a small volume of medium or failing to treat the skin are the two most frequent mistakes: the first leads to a false-negative, the second to a false-positive (skin flora) result.

s4866 What error might there be in the examination, and what is the correct tactic?
