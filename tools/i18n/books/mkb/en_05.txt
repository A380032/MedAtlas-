s0998 Independent work 1: Prions
s0999 A **prion** is an infectious particle that has no nucleic acid and consists of **protein** alone. In the brains of humans and animals the normal protein **PrP^C^** is normally present; the pathological form **PrP^Sc^** refolds the normal protein to resemble itself (changes its conformation). As a result, insoluble aggregates accumulate in the brain and the tissue becomes porous — **spongiform encephalopathy**.
s1000 **Features:** they do not provoke an immune response (no inflammation and no fever), are extremely resistant to ordinary sterilization regimens and to irradiation, the incubation period is measured in years, the disease develops gradually and has no cure.
s1001 **Prion diseases:** Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, kuru, Gerstmann–Sträussler–Scheinker syndrome, fatal familial insomnia; in animals — bovine spongiform encephalopathy, scrapie.
s1002 A prion is an infectious agent **without nucleic acid**. This is an exception to the classic “central dogma” in microbiology, and for this reason it is studied separately.
s1003 Independent work 2: Chromogenic nutrient media
s1004 **Chromogenic media** are modern differential media that contain a colorless **chromogenic substrate**. If a bacterium has a particular enzyme, it breaks down the substrate and a colored product is released — the colony is stained a **characteristic color**.
s1005 **Advantages:**
s1006 the species can be preliminarily identified **by colony color** — the answer is 18–24 hours faster;
s1007 it becomes easier to separate species in mixed growth (for example, in a urine culture);
s1008 the number of additional biochemical tests is reduced.
s1009 Examples used in practice: chromogenic agar for urine culture (E. coli — pink-red, enterococcus — blue-green colonies), chromogenic agar for distinguishing *Candida* species, and special chromogenic media for screening for MRSA and Salmonella.
s1010 A chromogenic medium gives a quick **preliminary** answer, but it does not replace final identification: a biochemical or molecular method (in modern laboratories, mass spectrometry or PCR) is still used for confirmation.
s1011 Practical skill: the algorithm for streaking by sectors (quadrant method)
s1012 Draw 4 sectors on the back of the plate and mark them; write the patient information and the date.
s1013 Flame the bacteriological loop in the alcohol burner **until red-hot** and cool it for 10–15 seconds.
s1014 Take the material with the loop and spread it in a dense zigzag in **sector 1**.
s1015 **Flame and cool the loop again**; draw it 3–4 times from the edge of sector 1 and spread into **sector 2**.
s1016 Continue in the same way into sectors 2→3 and 3→4. The loop is flamed before each transition — the number of cells decreases in each sector.
s1017 Place the plate **upside down** (so that condensation drops do not fall on the streak) in the incubator at 37 °C for 18–24 hours.
s1018 Touching the material with the loop **without cooling** it after flaming is the most common mistake: a hot loop kills the cells and the result becomes a false negative. The loop can be cooled by touching it to an empty area of the medium.
s1019 **Pasteur’s dilution method** and **Koch’s plate method** (serially diluting the material and pouring it mixed with molten agar) are other classic variants of isolation; in current practice, streaking by sectors is used more often.

s1096 Material taken from the CSF was inoculated onto blood agar, but after 24 hours there was no growth. In the smear, however, small gram-negative rods had been seen. Growth appeared when it was inoculated onto chocolate agar.
s1097 Why was there no growth on ordinary blood agar?
s1098 The causative agent needs growth factors (factors X and V) — probably *Haemophilus influenzae*. These factors are present only in blood agar in which the erythrocytes have been broken down, that is, heated blood (chocolate) agar.
s1099 To determine antibiotic susceptibility, a student used a culture that had stood in the incubator for three days. The results differed sharply from previous tests.

s1132 Catabolism and ways of obtaining energy
s1133 **Metabolism** consists of two directions: **catabolism** (breakdown, release of energy) and **anabolism/constructive metabolism** (synthesis, expenditure of energy — topic 7). In bacteria, energy is stored as **ATP** and is produced in the **cytoplasmic membrane** (there are no mitochondria).
s1134 The main pathways of carbohydrate breakdown: **glycolysis (Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas pathway)** — the most widespread; the **pentose phosphate pathway**; the **Entner–Doudoroff pathway** (for example, in *Pseudomonas*). The product of glycolysis is pyruvate, and its further fate depends on the type of respiration.

s1173 **Why is oxygen toxic to anaerobes?** In oxygen metabolism, toxic intermediate products form — the **superoxide anion (O₂⁻)** and **hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)**. Aerobes have enzymes that neutralize them — **superoxide dismutase** and **catalase**; obligate anaerobes lack these enzymes or have very little, so they die in the presence of oxygen.
s1174 **Methods of creating anaerobic conditions:**
s1175 **Physical:** anaerostat (replacing the air with a gas mixture), boiling the medium to drive off oxygen and sealing it with a layer of oil, deep inoculation (a tall column of medium).
s1176 **Chemical:** gas-generating packs, oxygen-absorbing substances (pyrogallol + alkali).
s1177 **Biological:** Fortner’s method — an aerobe and an anaerobe are inoculated side by side on one plate; the aerobe consumes the oxygen and the anaerobe begins to grow.
s1178 **Special media:** Kitt–Tarozzi medium (with liver pieces and a layer of oil), thioglycolate medium.
