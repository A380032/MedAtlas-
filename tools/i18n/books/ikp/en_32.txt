s6775 The term **“septic endocarditis”** in the course syllabus is outdated; the term **“infective endocarditis”** is now used (in the textbook, “infectious endocarditis”). The older literature distinguished, by course, **acute** (develops over a few days to weeks, most often staphylococcal) and **subacute** (endocarditis lenta, most often viridans streptococci) types.
s6776 The peripheral signs of IE — **“Osler — Ouch, Janeway — painless”**: Osler nodes are painful (immune vasculitis), Janeway lesions are painless (microembolism); Roth spots — on the fundus, Lukin spots — on the conjunctiva.
s6777 **Definite IE**: 2 major criteria, or 1 major + 3 minor criteria, or 5 minor criteria (or pathological confirmation). **Possible IE**: 1 major + 1 minor or 3 minor criteria. If the criteria are insufficient or another diagnosis is clear, **IE is rejected**.
s6778 The main signs of aortic regurgitation: **aortic configuration + weakening of the II sound over the aorta + a protodiastolic murmur at the Botkin–Erb point + a rapid and high pulse + a wide pulse pressure (low diastolic pressure) + peripheral vascular signs**.
s6779 The peripheral signs of aortic regurgitation — **“Everything pulsates”**: the head (de Musset), the carotid arteries (“carotid dance”), the nail-bed capillaries (Quincke), the pupils (Landolfi), the uvula (Müller); and the femoral artery “speaks” (Traube’s double sound, Duroziez’s double murmur).
s6780 The classic **symptom triad** of aortic stenosis: **angina, fainting (syncope) and dyspnea (heart failure)**. Once symptoms appear, the average survival without surgery shortens sharply (about 5 years with angina, 3 years with syncope and 2 years with heart failure) — that is why symptomatic severe aortic stenosis is an indication for valve replacement.
s6781 If a vegetation is **larger than 10 mm**, especially on the mitral valve and mobile, the risk of embolism increases sharply — this is one of the indications for early surgery. Other indications: severe heart failure, uncontrolled infection (abscess, persistent bacteremia), a fungal or multidrug-resistant pathogen.
s6782 Standard prophylaxis: **amoxicillin 2 g orally 30–60 minutes before** the procedure (children 50 mg/kg); in penicillin allergy — antibiotics of another group. But the most important prevention is **oral hygiene** and dental check-ups 1–2 times a year, as well as proper care of intravenous catheters.
s6783 Changes in the left ventricle in aortic defects: **“Stenosis — the wall thickens (pressure overload), regurgitation — the cavity dilates (volume overload)”**. That is why the heart is largest in aortic regurgitation (“ox heart”).
s6784 In IE, involvement of the aortic valve and heart failure are an indication for **urgent surgery**. A patient with endocarditis should have daily cardiac auscultation and ECG monitoring: the appearance of a new murmur or block indicates a complication.
s6785 To remember the surgical signal in aortic defects — **“Symptoms or Damage”**: the appearance of **S**ymptoms (angina, syncope, dyspnea) or **d**amage to the left ventricle (dilation, a fall in the ejection fraction) means that it is time to operate.
s6786 1. Infective (septic) endocarditis
s6787 **Infective endocarditis** (IE) is microbial inflammation of the heart valves or the parietal endocardium (and also of prosthetic valves, intracardiac devices and the endothelium of large vessels), accompanied by the formation of **vegetations**, destruction of the valves, emboli and immune complications.

s6802 1.2. Pathogenesis
s6803 Bacteremia (tooth extraction, catheter, injection) → microbes settle on the fibrin-platelet layer of the damaged endocardium → **vegetation** (microbes, fibrin, platelets) → destruction and perforation of the valve leaflets (**valvular regurgitation** — most often aortic and mitral), abscesses → detachment of fragments of the vegetation (**emboli**: brain, kidneys, spleen, coronary arteries, lungs — in tricuspid IE) → formation of immune complexes (**vasculitis, glomerulonephritis**, Osler nodes, Roth spots).

s6820 1.4. Laboratory and instrumental investigations
s6821 **Blood cultures** are the main investigation: they are taken before antibiotics are started, **at least 3 times**, from different veins, at 30-minute intervals (each time into aerobic and anaerobic bottles); there is no need to wait for a temperature spike.
s6822 Complete blood count: normochromic **anemia**, leukocytosis (or leukopenia), **markedly raised ESR** (50–70 mm/h); raised CRP and procalcitonin; rheumatoid factor may be positive.
s6823 Urine: proteinuria, microhematuria (glomerulonephritis, renal infarction).
s6824 **Echocardiography** — transthoracic and, if necessary, **transesophageal** (higher sensitivity): vegetations, valve perforation, abscess, new regurgitation.
s6825 Additional: cardiac CT, 18F-FDG PET/CT (especially with prosthetic valves), brain MRI (to detect emboli).

s6845 2. Aortic valve regurgitation
s6846 **Aortic regurgitation** is incomplete closure of the aortic valve leaflets during diastole with backflow of blood from the aorta into the left ventricle.
s6847 **Causes:** rheumatic heart disease, **infective endocarditis**, atherosclerosis and degenerative changes, a bicuspid aortic valve, dilation of the aortic root (Marfan syndrome, arterial hypertension, aortic dissection), syphilitic aortitis, ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, SLE.
s6848 **Hemodynamics:** in diastole part of the blood returns from the aorta to the left ventricle → the left ventricle receives blood both from the atrium and from the aorta → **volume overload of the left ventricle, eccentric hypertrophy and marked dilation** (the largest heart — “ox heart”) → in systole a large stroke volume is ejected into the aorta (**the systolic pressure rises**), while in diastole the pressure in the aorta falls sharply because of the backflow (**the diastolic pressure falls**) → **wide pulse pressure**. After a long period of compensation, left ventricular failure develops.

s6863 3. Narrowing of the aortic orifice (aortic stenosis)
s6864 **Aortic stenosis** is narrowing of the aortic orifice resulting from fusion, thickening and calcification of the aortic valve leaflets, which impedes the outflow of blood from the left ventricle in systole.
s6865 **Causes:** in the elderly, **degenerative (calcific) stenosis** — now the most common cause; a **congenital bicuspid aortic valve** (at the age of 40–60); rheumatic heart disease (often together with a mitral defect).
s6866 **Hemodynamics:** the normal aortic orifice area is **3–4 cm^2^**. When it narrows, a systolic **pressure gradient** appears between the left ventricle and the aorta → pressure overload of the left ventricle → **concentric hypertrophy** (the wall thickens without dilation of the cavity) → long compensation; then increased myocardial oxygen demand and reduced coronary perfusion (angina), limited stroke volume (syncope), and finally left ventricular dilation and failure.
