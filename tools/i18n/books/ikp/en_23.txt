s4857 **Beck’s triad** (cardiac tamponade): **arterial hypotension + distended neck veins + muffled heart sounds**. Suspicion of **aortic dissection** — a difference in systolic BP between the two arms of **more than 20 mm Hg** and sudden severe back pain.
s4858 If palpitations are accompanied by **syncope, chest pain, dyspnea** or structural heart disease, this may be a sign of a dangerous arrhythmia (ventricular tachycardia) and requires urgent investigation.
s4859 1. Brief anatomy and physiology of the heart
s4860 The heart is a four-chambered muscular organ located in the anterior inferior part of the mediastinum, two-thirds of it to the left of the midline. The base of the heart faces upward, to the right and backward, and the apex downward, to the left and forward. The anterior surface of the chest is touched mainly by the **right ventricle**; the left ventricle forms the left contour only as a narrow strip.

s4878 **The cardiac cycle** consists of systole and diastole. Ventricular systole: the periods of isovolumetric contraction (all valves closed) and ejection; diastole: the periods of isovolumetric relaxation, rapid and slow filling, and atrial systole. At a heart rate of 72 per minute, one cycle lasts about 0.8 seconds (systole 0.3, diastole 0.5 seconds).

s4920 2.2. Dyspnea and choking attacks (cardiac asthma)
s4921 In heart diseases, dyspnea most often develops as a result of **left ventricular failure** and congestion in the pulmonary circulation. It appears first on physical exertion, then on little exertion and at rest; it worsens on lying down and lessens on sitting up (**orthopnea**). A sudden choking attack at night is **cardiac asthma** (paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea); in severe cases, **pulmonary edema** with pink frothy sputum develops.

s4928 2.3. Palpitations and skipped beats
s4929 **Palpitations** are the sensation of the heartbeat. They are observed in tachycardia, extrasystole, atrial fibrillation, fever, anemia, thyrotoxicosis and neuroses. **Skipped beats** (the feeling that “the heart has stopped”) occur in extrasystole and AV blocks. Attacks of palpitations that begin and end suddenly are characteristic of paroxysmal tachycardia.
s4930 2.4. Edema
s4931 Cardiac edema is a sign of **right ventricular failure** (congestion in the systemic circulation): it first appears in the evening on the feet and ankles and lessens by the morning, then becomes permanent and spreads upward (thighs, lower back, abdominal wall); fluid accumulates in the body cavities (ascites, hydrothorax) — **anasarca**. In bedridden patients it is seen over the sacrum and lower back.

s4936 2.5. Other complaints
s4937 **Heaviness and pain in the right hypochondrium** — liver enlargement and stretching of its capsule (right ventricular failure).
s4938 **Cough** (dry, often on lying down) and **hemoptysis** — congestion in the pulmonary circulation (mitral stenosis).
s4939 **Headache, dizziness, tinnitus, “floaters” before the eyes** — arterial hypertension; **syncope** — aortic stenosis, severe bradyarrhythmias (Morgagni–Adams–Stokes attack).
s4940 **Leg pain on walking, limping** (“intermittent claudication”) — atherosclerosis of the leg arteries.
s4941 **Weakness, easy fatigability, fever** — rheumatism, infective endocarditis, myocarditis.
s4942 2.6. Features of the history
s4943 In the history of present illness, the following are established: when the pain began, how it has changed (increasing frequency of angina attacks — unstable angina), hospital admissions, ECG and echocardiography results, the medicines being taken. In the life history: **tonsillitis and joint pain in childhood**
s4944 (rheumatism), risk factors — smoking, arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, obesity, physical inactivity, psychological stress; **heredity** — premature ischemic heart disease in close relatives (men under 55, women under 65), sudden death.

s4972 4. Inspection of the heart region and the peripheral vessels
s4973 4.1. Inspection of the heart region
s4974 The patient is inspected lying down or standing, with the light falling from the side. The following are assessed:
s4975 **The cardiac hump** (gibbus cardiacus) — a bulging of the chest over the heart region; it develops as a result of cardiac enlargement from childhood (congenital and early acquired defects), while the chest is not yet ossified.
s4976 **The apex beat** — normally visible in more than half of healthy people, on the left in the V intercostal space.
s4977 **The cardiac impulse** — a diffuse pulsation at the left sternal border in the III–IV intercostal spaces and below the xiphoid process — right ventricular hypertrophy and dilation.
s4978 **A negative (retracting) apex beat** — indrawing of the area of the apex beat during systole — **adhesive pericarditis** (the heart is adherent to the pericardium and the chest wall).
s4979 **Pulsations in the region of the base of the heart:** in the II intercostal space to the right of the sternum — dilation or aneurysm of the aorta, aortic regurgitation; in the II intercostal space on the left — dilation of the pulmonary artery (pulmonary hypertension, mitral stenosis); behind the manubrium (in the jugular notch) — dilation of the aortic arch.
s4980 **Epigastric pulsation** — see section 6.
s4981 4.2. Inspection of the peripheral vessels
s4982 Arteries:
s4983 **The “carotid dance”** — strong pulsation of the carotid arteries; **de Musset’s sign** — nodding of the head in time with the pulse; **Quincke’s capillary pulse** — rhythmic alternation of redness and pallor under the nail; **pulsation of the pupils** (Landolfi’s sign). These are characteristic of **aortic regurgitation**.
s4984 Tortuous, thickened temporal arteries — atherosclerosis, giant cell arteritis.
s4985 Veins:
s4986 **Distension of the neck veins** — right ventricular failure, exudative and constrictive pericarditis, tricuspid valve defects, compression of the superior vena cava.
s4987 **Pulsation of the neck veins:** normally a visible negative venous pulse (corresponding to atrial systole); a **positive venous pulse** (the vein fills at the same time as ventricular systole) — **tricuspid regurgitation**.
s4988 **Kussmaul’s sign** — increased distension of the neck veins on inspiration (normally it decreases) — constrictive pericarditis, severe right ventricular failure.
s4989 **Hepatojugular reflux** — increased distension of the neck veins on pressing the right hypochondrium for 10–15 seconds — right ventricular failure.
s4990 Varicose veins of the legs; an infiltrate along a vessel with redness and pain — thrombophlebitis.
s4991 5. Palpation of the heart region
s4992 Palpation detects the apex beat, the cardiac impulse, chest thrills (the “cat’s purr”), other pulsations and the pericardial friction rub.
s4993 5.1. The apex beat
s4994 **The apex beat** is the striking of the apex of the left ventricle against the chest wall during systole (the contracting heart makes a rotary movement, and its apex rises forward).
s4995 **Method of palpation:** the patient lies on the back (with the head slightly raised) or stands. The physician places the palm of the right hand between the III and VI ribs, with the base of the palm toward the sternum and the fingers toward the axilla (orienting palpation). Once the impulse has been located, its exact site and features are assessed with the fingertips (2–3 fingers, bent). In women, the breast is moved upward and to the right.

s5012 5.2. The cardiac impulse
s5013 **The cardiac impulse** is a diffuse pulsation felt with the palm at the left sternal border in the III–IV intercostal spaces and in the epigastric region in hypertrophy and dilation of the right ventricle. It is not detected in a healthy person (except in children and thin people). It is observed in mitral stenosis, pulmonary hypertension, chronic cor pulmonale and congenital defects.

s5017 **The “cat’s purr”** (frémissement cataire) is a low-frequency vibration felt over the chest wall as a result of turbulence of the blood flow passing through a narrow opening; it resembles the purring of a stroked cat. s5045 7. A detailed analysis of the circulations and the cardiac cycle

s5050 **The systemic circulation** begins from the left ventricle with the aorta, delivers arterial blood to all the organs and ends via the venae cavae in the right atrium. **The pulmonary circulation** begins from the right ventricle with the pulmonary artery; in the pulmonary capillaries the blood is saturated with oxygen and drains through the four pulmonary veins into the left atrium. The pulmonary circulation is a low-pressure system: the systolic pressure in the pulmonary artery is normally **15–30 mm Hg** (100–140 in the aorta).
