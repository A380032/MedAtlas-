s3385 Which added sound is this? How is it produced, and in which conditions is it heard?
s3386 Crepitation. It is produced by the simultaneous separation, at the end of inspiration, of alveoli that stuck together during expiration because of exudate adhering to the alveolar walls. Its not changing after coughing distinguishes it from moist rales, and its not increasing under pressure of the stethoscope and its being heard only at the end of inspiration distinguish it from the pleural friction rub. s3416 The small bronchi **less than 2 mm** in diameter, which have no cartilaginous skeleton, are held open by the elastic recoil of the lung tissue. When elasticity is lost in emphysema, they collapse and close during expiration (**expiratory collapse**) — air is “trapped” in the alveoli.
s3417 In acute bronchitis the auscultatory findings are diffuse and symmetrical. If **consonating** fine-bubbling moist rales or crepitation appear over a limited area, this is a sign not of bronchitis but of pneumonia — radiography is mandatory.
s3418 By the WHO criterion, chronic bronchitis is a productive cough **for at least 3 months a year in at least 2 consecutive years**, with other causes of cough (tuberculosis, bronchiectasis, tumor and others) excluded.
s3419 According to GOLD 2026, spirometry is mandatory for the diagnosis of COPD: the **post-bronchodilator FEV~1~/FVC ratio must be less than 0.70**. The severity of obstruction is assessed by FEV1 as a percentage of the predicted value.
s3420 Older textbooks (including the textbook used here) grouped chronic bronchitis, bronchial asthma, bronchiectasis and emphysema together as “chronic obstructive lung diseases”. In modern classifications, **bronchial asthma and bronchiectasis are separate diseases**, and COPD is only a condition with persistent (not fully reversible) airflow limitation.
s3421 For asthmatic sputum, **“E + C + C”**: **E**osinophils, **C**urschmann spirals, **C**harcot–Leyden crystals — “Eosinophil, Curschmann, Charcot”.
s3422 GINA 2025 uses the concepts of a **severe and a life-threatening exacerbation** instead of the term “status asthmaticus”. Signs of a threat to life: impaired consciousness (drowsiness), a **“silent chest”**, cyanosis, bradycardia or hypotension, SpO2 below 90 %, PEF less than 25–50 % of the predicted value. Such a patient is transferred urgently to the intensive care unit.
s3423 The **“traffic light” system** of asthma control by PEF: **green zone** — PEF more than 80 % of the personal best (controlled); **yellow zone** — 50–80 % (caution, treatment needs to be stepped up); **red zone** — less than 50 % (a dangerous condition, urgent help).
s3424 Number of “yes” answers: **0 — well controlled**, **1–2 — partly controlled**, **3–4 — uncontrolled asthma**. Together with control, future risk factors (exacerbations, low FEV1, smoking, eosinophilia) are also assessed.
s3425 The **diurnal variability** of PEF = (highest PEF during the day − lowest PEF) / their mean × 100 %. If it averages **more than 10 %** over two weeks (13 % in children), it confirms the diagnosis of bronchial asthma.
s3426 The main principle of GINA 2025: in adults and adolescents, treating asthma **with short-acting beta~2~-agonists alone is not recommended**; every patient receives ICS-containing treatment (the preferred track is low-dose budesonide-formoterol “as needed” and as maintenance treatment). Treatment with salbutamol alone increases the risk of severe exacerbations and death.
