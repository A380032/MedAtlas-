s4468 2. Lung abscess
s4469 **A lung abscess** is a nonspecific purulent-necrotic destruction of the lung tissue with the formation of one or more cavities bounded by granulation tissue (a capsule). It occurs most often in men aged 30–50.
s4470 **Causes and predisposing factors:** aspiration (alcohol intoxication, epilepsy, anesthesia, impaired swallowing, dental caries and periodontal disease), pneumonia (Staphylococcus, Klebsiella), bronchial obstruction (tumor, foreign body), septic embolism, diabetes mellitus, reduced immunity. Pathogens: anaerobes, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

s4495 3. Bronchiectasis
s4496 **Bronchiectasis** is an irreversible dilation (bronchiectases) and deformity of the bronchi in which a chronic suppurative process takes place. The disease most often forms in childhood and adolescence (5–25 years) and is more common in males.
s4497 Figure 9.2
s4498 Diagram of bronchiectases: a healthy lung (left) and bronchiectasis (right) (Gadayev A. et al., 2012, Fig. 65)
s4499 **Causes:** severe pneumonias, measles and pertussis in childhood; congenital factors (developmental defects of the bronchial wall, cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia — Kartagener’s syndrome); bronchial obstruction (foreign body, tumor); immunodeficiency.
s4500 **Forms of bronchiectasis:** cylindrical, saccular, fusiform, mixed. **The lower lobes of the lungs** and the lingular segments of the left lung are most often affected (their drainage is poorer).
s4501 Clinical picture:
s4502 **Cough with a large amount of purulent sputum**, especially in the morning (“a mouthful”) and in the drainage position (lying on the healthy side with the head lower); the daily amount is 50–500 ml or more; when left to settle, the sputum separates into three layers and is often foul-smelling.
s4503 **Hemoptysis** (often recurrent), sometimes pulmonary hemorrhage.
s4504 Fever, weakness and sweating during exacerbations; exacerbations of the disease in spring and autumn are characteristic.
s4505 In a long course — dyspnea, weight loss, **clubbing** and watch-glass nails, cyanosis.
s4506 **Physical signs:** few changes during remission. During an exacerbation, over the affected area (most often the lower parts) percussion reveals an impaired note (surrounding pneumosclerosis and infiltration), and auscultation reveals harsh breathing and **medium- and coarse-bubbling moist rales**, which decrease or disappear after coughing and expectoration. When pneumosclerosis and emphysema develop, their signs appear.
s4507 **Diagnosis:** the “gold standard” is **high-resolution computed tomography** (a bronchial diameter larger than that of the adjacent artery, absence of tapering of the bronchus toward the periphery); bronchography was used previously. Bronchoscopy is used to find the source of pus and for sanitation.
s4508 **Complications:** pulmonary hemorrhage, respiratory failure, chronic cor pulmonale, amyloidosis (kidneys — nephrotic syndrome), lung abscess, brain abscess.

s4593 5.1. Lung gangrene
s4594 **Lung gangrene** is a poorly demarcated, rapidly spreading putrefactive necrosis of the lung tissue. It differs from an abscess in that the patient’s condition is very severe (sepsis, shock), the sputum is very abundant (up to 1 liter a day), **very foul-smelling** (noticed even by those nearby), brownish-gray and multilayered; there is unbounded destruction in the lung with multiple cavities. Mortality is high, and surgical treatment is often needed.
s4595 6. Pathogenesis and diagnosis of bronchiectasis
s4596 Bronchiectases develop according to a **“vicious circle”** (P. Cole’s hypothesis): impaired mucociliary clearance → accumulation of mucus → bacterial colonization → chronic neutrophilic inflammation → neutrophil elastase and other enzymes destroy the elastic and muscle fibers of the bronchial wall → the bronchus dilates and becomes deformed → clearance is further impaired.
