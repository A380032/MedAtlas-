s5881 The order of listening follows the frequency of valve involvement: **“Mitral — Aortic — Pulmonary — Tricuspid — Botkin”** — “**M**y **A**unt **P**olly **T**akes **B**uses”. By site: apex → right II → left II → xiphoid → Botkin–Erb.
s5882 In a healthy person **the I sound is louder than the II sound at the apex** (the I sound is about 1.5–2 times stronger), and **the II sound is louder than the I sound at the base of the heart**. In young people the II sound over the pulmonary artery is slightly louder than over the aorta; after the age of 40 it is the opposite — louder over the aorta.
s5883 **Physiological** splitting of the II sound appears on inspiration and disappears on expiration. **Pathological** splitting is constant and does not depend on the phases of respiration. To distinguish them, the patient is auscultated while holding the breath after inspiration and after expiration.
s5884 **“Quail rhythm” = “Snapping I sound + II sound + opening snap”** — only in mitral stenosis. Remember it by the rhythm “**SNAP — dub — click**”: “snap” is the snapping I sound, “dub” the II sound and “click” the opening snap.
s5885 To hear a diastolic murmur well: **mitral stenosis — “lie on the left side”** (bell, at the apex); **aortic regurgitation — “lean forward and breathe out”** (diaphragm, at the Botkin–Erb point).
s5886 Any **diastolic murmur** is almost always regarded as **organic** (the Austin Flint and Graham Steell murmurs are exceptions — but they too arise against the background of an organic defect). If a diastolic murmur is found, echocardiography is always performed.
s5887 In modern practice, in addition to the points, it is recommended to listen over the whole heart region by **“inching” auscultation** (moving the stethoscope from the apex upward along the left sternal edge, then to the right); otherwise murmurs between the points are missed.
s5888 The ability to auscultate requires regular practice. According to studies, students and young physicians correctly identify only about half of heart murmurs. It is therefore recommended to listen to every patient, compare the result with the echocardiogram and practice with recorded heart sounds.
s5889 A short formula for remembering a murmur — **“WHERE, WHEN, WHERE TO, HOW LOUD”**: where it is heard best (epicenter), when (systole or diastole), where it radiates, and how loud it is (Levine grade).
s5890 1. General rules of cardiac auscultation
s5891 The room should be quiet and warm.
s5892 The patient is auscultated standing, sitting and lying down, and if necessary on the left side (mitral defects) and leaning forward (aortic regurgitation).
s5893 Auscultation is performed first during quiet breathing, then while the breath is held after inspiration and after expiration (so that lung sounds do not interfere).
s5894 Sometimes auscultation is performed after physical exertion (sitting down and standing up 10–15 times) — the murmur of mitral stenosis becomes louder.
s5895 **The diaphragm** is used for high-frequency sounds (the I and II sounds, the murmur of aortic regurgitation), and **the bell** for low-frequency sounds (the III and IV sounds, the diastolic murmur of mitral stenosis).
s5896 To distinguish the sounds, the carotid pulse or the apex beat is palpated at the same time: they coincide with **the I sound**.

s5916 3. Heart sounds
s5917 3.1. Mechanism of formation of the sounds
s5918 **The I sound (systolic)** arises at the beginning of ventricular systole and consists of four components:
s5919 **The valvular component** (the main one) — closure and vibration of the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves;
s5920 **The muscular component** — vibration of the ventricular myocardium during isovolumetric contraction;
s5921 **The vascular component** — vibration of the initial segments of the aorta and pulmonary artery as they fill with blood;
s5922 **The atrial component** — atrial contraction (at the onset of the I sound).
s5923 **The II sound (diastolic)** arises at the beginning of ventricular diastole from **closure of the semilunar (aortic and pulmonary) valves** and vibration of the walls of the aorta and pulmonary artery. It consists of two components: **aortic (A~2~)** and **pulmonary (P~2~)**.
s5924 **The III sound** — vibration of the ventricular walls during rapid ventricular filling in early diastole (0.12–0.18 seconds after the II sound); it may be heard in healthy children and adolescents and in thin young people.
s5925 **The IV sound** — vibration of the ventricular walls in presystole (before the I sound), when the atria contract and drive blood into the ventricles; it is usually not heard in healthy adults.

s5944 4. Changes in the heart sounds
s5945 4.1. Simultaneous change of both sounds
s5946 **Accentuation of both sounds**: extracardiac causes — thinness, a thin chest wall, shrinkage of the lung edges; cardiac causes — thyrotoxicosis, physical exertion, excitement, fever, anemia, tachycardia.
s5947 **Weakening (muffling) of both sounds**: extracardiac causes — obesity, a thick chest wall, pulmonary emphysema, left-sided exudative pleurisy, pericardial fluid; cardiac causes — myocardial damage (reduced contractility in myocarditis, myocardial infarction, cardiosclerosis), collapse, shock.
