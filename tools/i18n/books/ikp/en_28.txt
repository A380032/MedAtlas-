s5977 4.4. Splitting and reduplication of the sounds
s5978 When the components of a sound do not arise at the same time but are heard at a certain interval, this is called **splitting** (an interval of 0.02–0.04 s — the sound is “cleft”) or **reduplication** (an interval of more than 0.05 s — two separate sounds).
s5979 **Reduplication of the I sound** — in bundle branch block (the ventricles do not contract at the same time) and in ventricular extrasystole.
s5980 **Physiological reduplication of the II sound** — on inspiration the negative pressure in the chest increases, the blood flow to the right ventricle rises, right ventricular systole lengthens and the pulmonary valve closes later. This reduplication is heard **only on inspiration** and disappears on expiration; it is found over the pulmonary artery in young people.

s6010 6. Heart murmurs
s6011 6.1. Mechanism of formation of murmurs
s6012 Normally the blood flow in the heart and vessels is **laminar** (in layers) and silent. A murmur appears when the blood flow becomes **turbulent** (eddying).
s6013 Figure 12.5
s6014 Mechanisms of murmur formation: A — normal laminar flow; B — turbulent flow through a narrowing; D — turbulent flow in a dilated vessel; E — irregularity of the wall (Gadayev A. et al., 2012, Fig. 43)
s6015 Causes of turbulent flow:
s6016 **Narrowing of an orifice** (stenosis) — the blood passes through the narrow site at high speed.
s6017 **Valvular regurgitation** — the blood flows back in the opposite direction through a narrow slit.
s6018 **Dilation of a vessel or cavity** — eddies form as blood passes from a narrow site into a wider one.
s6019 **Reduced blood viscosity** and **increased blood flow velocity** (anemia, fever, thyrotoxicosis) — functional murmurs.
s6020 Pathological shunts (ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus).

s6036 6.3. Plan for describing a murmur
s6037 **Phase** — systolic or diastolic (determined by palpating the apex beat or the carotid pulse at the same time).
s6038 **Relation to the sounds** — whether it begins after a sound or “swallows” the sound.
s6039 **Epicenter** — the point where it is heard best (shows which valve it belongs to).
s6040 **Radiation** — in the direction of the blood flow.
s6041 **Intensity (loudness)** — grades 1–6 on the Levine scale.
s6042 **Shape** — increasing (crescendo), decreasing (decrescendo), diamond-shaped, band-shaped.
s6043 **Timbre** — harsh, saw-like, blowing, soft, musical.
s6044 **Duration** — how much of the phase it occupies.
s6045 **Dependence on position, respiration and exertion**.

s6095 6.5. Extracardiac murmurs
s6096 **The pericardial friction rub** is heard in systole and diastole, best over the area of absolute cardiac dullness (at the left sternal edge); it does not radiate anywhere, becomes louder when pressed with the stethoscope and when leaning forward, and persists when the breath is held. **The pleuropericardial murmur**, by contrast, becomes louder on inspiration and decreases or disappears when the breath is held.

s6191 10.1. Types of functional murmurs
s6192 **The “innocent” (physiological) murmur of young people** — in children and adolescents and in thin young people; Still’s murmur (“musical”, midway between the apex and the left sternal edge).
s6193 **Flow murmurs** — anemia (blood viscosity is reduced), fever, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy, physical exertion.
s6194 **Murmurs of relative valvular regurgitation** — in left ventricular dilation the valve ring widens and the leaflets do not close completely (relative mitral and tricuspid regurgitation); they become softer when the dilation decreases with treatment.
s6195 **Venous hum** (a continuous murmur in the jugular veins) — in children and in anemia; it disappears when the head is turned or the vein is compressed.
