s0020 Caution
s0021 It is a mistake to base a diagnosis on a single sign. For example, ascites is not specific to liver cirrhosis: it also occurs in heart failure, nephrotic syndrome and tumors of the peritoneum. The combination of all the signs must always be analyzed.
s0022 The basic rules of communicating with the patient: introduce yourself, address the patient by name and obtain consent for the examination; sit face to face with the patient, with your eyes at the same level, in a relaxed position; listen to the patient without interrupting and show your attention by nodding; hide your negative emotions and stay calm even if you detect a serious sign; keep medical confidentiality: information about the patient is used only for treatment.
s0023 Leading (suggestive) questions push the patient toward a wrong answer and lead to diagnostic error. The conversation should begin with open questions and then move on to closed questions for clarification (the “funnel” technique).
s0024 The order of the examination is always the same: **history taking → general inspection → inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation of the organ systems → additional investigations**. The systems are usually examined in the following order: the respiratory, cardiovascular, digestive, urinary, endocrine, musculoskeletal and nervous systems.
s0025 Memory aid
s0026 The four methods of physical examination — **“IPPA”**: **I**nspection, **P**alpation, **P**ercussion, **A**uscultation. Never change the order: first we look, then we touch, then we tap, and last of all we listen.
s0027 The case history is a legal document. Every entry made in it is confirmed with the date, time and signature; corrections are made only in the established manner. Giving information about the patient to third parties without his or her consent is a breach of medical confidentiality.
s0028 In English-language textbooks (Macleod’s), the analysis of pain is remembered as **SOCRATES**: **S**ite, **O**nset, **C**haracter, **R**adiation, **A**ssociations (accompanying symptoms), **T**ime course, **E**xacerbating/relieving factors, **S**everity.
s0029 The smoking index (**“pack-years”**) = number of cigarettes smoked per day × years of smoking / 20. When the index exceeds 10 pack-years, the risk of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) rises considerably.
s0030 The review of systems is not a “formality”. For example, in a young woman who complains only of joint pain, questioning may reveal a facial rash and hair loss, pointing to systemic lupus erythematosus; in a patient admitted with a respiratory disease, the discovery of nocturia and edema may lead to heart or kidney disease.
s0031 When working with a patient, the student: introduces himself or herself as a student and obtains the patient’s consent; does not give the patient independent information about the diagnosis or prognosis — this is the task of the attending physician; does not share information about the patient (photos, case history) on social media; respects the patient’s decision if he or she refuses.
s0032 Protection against errors is the question **“What else could it be?”** After every diagnosis, list at least two alternative diseases and look for the signs that rule them out or confirm them.
s0033 Every additional investigation is ordered **to answer a clinical question**. Ordering many tests “just in case” exhausts the patient, increases costs and, because of incidental findings (“incidentalomas”), may lead to unnecessary invasive procedures. A correctly collected history and a meticulous physical examination reduce the number of investigations needed.
s0034 The **“3A” rule** of a good history: **A**ttend (do not interrupt the patient), **A**nalyze (each complaint according to the plan), **A**ffirm (repeat back what you heard and get the patient’s confirmation).
s0035 In the case history, write not the patient’s own expressions (“my insides burn”, “my heart is being squeezed”) but their medical interpretation; however, do not arbitrarily exaggerate or play down the patient’s words. For example, “my heart is being squeezed” may not be written down as “angina pectoris” without clarifying what it means.
s0036 1. The propaedeutics of internal medicine: the subject, its aim and tasks
s0037 **Propaedeutics** (Greek propaideuo — “to teach beforehand, to introduce into a science”) is the introductory stage of the clinical disciplines. The propaedeutics of internal medicine teaches the future physician the methods of examining the patient, how to detect and interpret the signs of disease and make a syndromic diagnosis, as well as medical ethics and deontology. This knowledge is needed not only in the senior years but throughout the physician’s whole career.
s0038 The discipline has three main parts:
s0039 **Diagnostic technique** — the methods of history taking, inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation of the patient, as well as laboratory and instrumental investigations.
s0040 **Semiotics (semiology)** — the study of the signs of disease (symptoms), their mechanism of origin and diagnostic significance.
s0041 **Diagnostic methodology** — clinical reasoning, combining the signs into syndromes and the rules for formulating the diagnosis.
s0042 The main tasks of propaedeutics:
s0043 to teach a systematic and complete examination of the patient;
s0044 to teach how to detect and interpret symptoms and syndromes;
s0045 to teach how to make a syndromic (provisional) diagnosis;
s0046 to teach how to keep the case history correctly;
s0047 to instill medical ethics and deontology.
s0048 The concepts of health and disease
s0049 According to the Constitution of the World Health Organization (WHO), **health** is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.
s0050 **Disease** is a complex general response of the organism to the action of harmful factors, in which structural, functional and metabolic changes and a breakdown of adaptive processes are observed and the capacity for work is limited. The causes of disease are mechanical, physical, chemical, biological, psychological and hereditary (genetic) factors.

s0072 A **syndrome** is a combination of symptoms that share a common pathogenesis. One syndrome may occur in several diseases (consolidation — pneumonia, pulmonary infarction, tumor).

s0083 Types of diagnosis
s0084 **Primary (provisional)** — made after the first examination of the patient.
s0085 **Clinical (main)** — made after clinical, laboratory and instrumental investigations, usually within three days.
s0086 **Final** — made before the patient is discharged from hospital (or after death).
s0087 The structure of the clinical diagnosis: **the main disease**, **complications of the main disease**, **a competing disease**, **a background disease** (for example, diabetes mellitus) and **concomitant diseases**. A complete diagnosis includes anatomical (morphological), functional, pathogenetic and etiological components.
s0088 An example of a complete clinical diagnosis: “Rheumatic heart disease: mitral stenosis (moderate). Complication: atrial fibrillation, permanent form. Chronic heart failure stage IIA, NYHA functional class III.” Here the morphology (the defect), function (the failure), complication (the arrhythmia) and etiology (rheumatic) are reflected in a single conclusion.
s0089 Methods of clinical reasoning
s0090 **Induction** — moving from particular signs to a general conclusion: the signs are collected and combined into syndromes, and then a conclusion about the disease is drawn.
s0091 **Deduction** — from general knowledge to the particular: the physician looks in the patient for the other signs characteristic of the disease he or she suspects and confirms or rejects the hypothesis.
s0092 **Diagnosis ex juvantibus** — if treatment directed against the suspected disease is effective, the diagnosis is considered confirmed.
s0093 3. Medical deontology and communication with the patient
s0094 **Medical deontology** (Greek deon — duty, logos — study) is the study of the physician’s professional duty and conduct. **Medical ethics** is a broader concept covering the relationships of the health worker with the patient, his or her relatives, colleagues and society.
s0095 Great contributions to the history of deontology were made by Hippocrates (“Do no harm” — primum non nocere), Avicenna (Ibn Sina) (“A physician should have the eyes of a falcon, the hands of a maiden, the wisdom of a serpent and the heart of a lion”) and, later, the Declaration of Geneva (1948), the International Code of Medical Ethics (1949) and the Declaration of Helsinki (1964).

s0186 6. History taking (interrogatio)
s0187 Although history taking seems simple, it is the most complex and the most valuable method of examination. It consists of the following parts: passport data → complaints → history of present illness → life history.
s0188 6.1. Passport data
s0189 The patient’s age, sex, occupation and place of residence are important for the diagnosis. For example, dyspeptic symptoms that appear for the first time in old age raise suspicion of a tumor, whereas in a young person they are most often associated with a functional disorder. The occupation helps to suspect occupational diseases (pneumoconiosis, intoxication).
s0190 6.2. The patient’s complaints (querelae)
s0191 Complaints are divided into two groups:
s0192 **Main (leading) complaints** — the signs that made the patient seek medical help and that are characteristic of the main affected organ (for example, cough and sputum, pain in the heart region).
