s4055 6. Pleurisy
s4056 **Pleurisy** is inflammation of the pleural layers. **Dry (fibrinous)** and **exudative (effusive)** pleurisy are distinguished. It is most often not an independent disease but a complication or manifestation of other diseases (pneumonia, tuberculosis, tumor, rheumatism, SLE, pulmonary infarction, uremia).
s4057 6.1. Dry (fibrinous) pleurisy
s4058 **Complaints:** **stabbing pain** on the affected side of the chest, worse on deep inspiration, coughing, sneezing and bending to the healthy side; dry cough, low-grade fever. The pain of diaphragmatic pleurisy may radiate to the abdomen and shoulder.
s4059 **Inspection:** the patient lies on the affected side or presses the chest with the hand (to restrict movement); the affected side lags behind in breathing; breathing is shallow and rapid.
s4060 **Palpation:** a tender area; sometimes the pleural friction is felt with the hand.
s4061 **Percussion:** the note is unchanged or slightly impaired; on the affected side the mobility of the lower edge is decreased (because of pain).
s4062 **Auscultation: the pleural friction rub** is the main sign; breath sounds are diminished.
s4063 6.2. Exudative pleurisy
s4064 It often begins as dry pleurisy: as fluid begins to accumulate, the pain lessens and the **dyspnea** increases. Fever, weakness, sweating. The physical signs correspond to the syndrome of fluid accumulation in the pleural cavity (see section 5). During the resorption of the fluid, the pleural friction rub reappears.

s4091 **Hydropneumothorax** is the presence of both air and fluid in the pleural cavity. s4092 8. Pleural puncture
s4093 **Indications:** diagnostic (to determine the nature of the fluid) and therapeutic (to remove fluid or air, to administer drugs).
s4094 **Site:** in the area where dullness on percussion is most pronounced, usually **in the VII–VIII intercostal space along the scapular or posterior axillary line**. The needle is inserted along the **upper edge** of the rib, because the intercostal vessels and nerves run along the lower edge of the rib. In pneumothorax, the puncture is performed in the II intercostal space along the midclavicular line.

s4205 12. Pathophysiology of pneumothorax and danger signs
s4206 Normally the pressure in the pleural cavity is below atmospheric (negative) and keeps the lung expanded. When air enters, the pressure equalizes and the lung collapses owing to its elastic recoil. In **valvular (tension) pneumothorax**, air enters with each inspiration but does not leave: the intrapleural pressure becomes positive, the mediastinum shifts to the healthy side, the venae cavae kink and the venous return to the heart decreases → **obstructive shock**.

s4268 15. Pleural empyema
s4269 **Pleural empyema** (pyothorax) is the accumulation of pus in the pleural cavity. It most often develops after pneumonia, rupture of a lung abscess into the pleura, chest injury and operations. Signs: hectic fever, chills, profuse sweating, intoxication, pain and heaviness in the chest, signs of pleural fluid; sometimes swelling and redness of the chest wall. In the aspirate — thick pus, pH below 7.0, markedly low glucose, bacteria on culture. **Treatment:** drainage of the pleural cavity and antibiotics; if delayed, the pleura thickens and the lung becomes “encased in armor”.
s4270 16. Pulmonary embolism and pulmonary infarction
s4271 In the differential diagnosis of conditions with consolidation and pleuritic pain, **pulmonary embolism** (PE) must always be remembered.
s4272 **Risk factors:** prolonged bed rest, operations (especially orthopedic), leg injuries, tumors, pregnancy and the postpartum period, hormonal contraceptives, previous venous thrombosis, obesity, long flights.
