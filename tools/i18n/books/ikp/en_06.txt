s1328 In central cyanosis the skin is **warm**, and the tongue and lips are also blue; in peripheral cyanosis the skin is **cold**, the tongue is usually pink, and the blueness is found in the parts of the body farthest from the heart (acral). Cyanosis becomes visible when the amount of reduced hemoglobin in the blood exceeds **50 g/l**; for this reason it appears late in severe anemia.
s1329 Distinguishing jaundice: in **true jaundice** the sclerae, the palate and the mucosa under the tongue also turn yellow; in **false jaundice** (from eating carrots, carotene, taking quinacrine) only the palms and soles turn yellow, while the sclerae remain white. Assess jaundice only in daylight.
s1330 The causes of clubbing — **“LHL”**: **L**ungs (suppurative diseases, cancer), **H**eart (“blue” congenital defects, infective endocarditis), **L**iver (cirrhosis) and inflammatory bowel diseases.
s1331 Normal temperature: in the axilla **36.4–36.8 °C** (36.3–36.9 °C in the textbook); in the mouth **0.3–0.5 °C** higher than in the axilla; in the rectum **0.5–1.0 °C** higher. In a healthy person the daily fluctuation does not exceed **1 °C**: the lowest temperature is between 3 and 6 in the morning, and the highest between 17 and 21 h in the evening.
s1332 **“Continuous — Lobar”**: in lobar pneumonia the fever is continuous (continua) and ends with a crisis. **“Hectic — Pus”**: a hectic fever always makes one think of a suppurative process (abscess, sepsis).
s1333 BMI misjudges fat tissue in athletes with strongly developed muscles and in patients with edema. That is why the waist circumference must always be measured together with the BMI: abdominal obesity may be present even with a normal BMI.
s1334 General rules of palpation: the physician’s hands should be **warm**, the nails short and the movements **gentle**; the examination begins in a painless area, and the painful area is palpated last; the patient should be in a comfortable position with the muscles relaxed; symmetrical areas are always compared.

s1337 If a lymph node is **larger than 2 cm**, **hard**, **fixed**, located **in the supraclavicular region**, or remains enlarged for **more than 4 weeks** and the patient is older than 40, there is a strong indication for biopsy. It is a mistake to keep observing such a node as “inflammation”.
s1338 A BMI below 18.5 kg/m^2^ and marked weight loss over a short period are signs of **protein-energy malnutrition**. Malnutrition occurs in 20–40 percent of hospital patients, and it increases the risk of infectious complications, slow wound healing and death.

s1432 3. Subcutaneous fat, muscles, bones and joints
s1433 The development of the **subcutaneous fat** is assessed by the thickness of a skinfold (next to the umbilicus, below the shoulder blade, on the back of the upper arm). s1443 4.1. Measuring body temperature
s1444 Body temperature is measured with mercury thermometers (used less often today for safety reasons), electronic (digital) and infrared (ear, forehead) thermometers. The main method is **measurement in the axilla**: the axilla is wiped dry, the tip of the thermometer is placed in the center of the axillary fossa and the arm is pressed against the chest; with a mercury thermometer the measurement takes **10 minutes**. The temperature is measured at least twice a day (in the morning at 7–8 a.m. and in the evening at 17–19 h) and recorded on the **temperature chart**.

s1448 **Fever** is a rise in body temperature resulting from an upward shift of the “set point” of the thermoregulatory center in the hypothalamus under the action of pyrogens. s1461 Stages of fever:
s1462 **Stage of rising temperature** (stadium incrementi) — heat production exceeds heat loss: chills, shivering, pale and cold skin (“goose flesh”).
s1463 **Stage of high temperature** (stadium fastigii) — heat production and heat loss are balanced at a high level: a feeling of heat, flushed and hot skin, headache, tachycardia.
s1464 **Stage of falling temperature** (stadium decrementi) — heat loss predominates, with profuse sweating. If the temperature falls sharply within a few hours, it is a **crisis** (risk of vascular insufficiency — collapse); if it falls gradually over several days, it is **lysis**.
s1465 For every 1 °C rise in temperature, the heart rate increases by about **8–10 beats** per minute. If the temperature is high but the pulse is relatively slow (**relative bradycardia**), typhoid fever, brucellosis and legionellosis should be considered. In lobar pneumonia, collapse may develop during the critical fall: the patient sweats, turns pale and the blood pressure falls.
