s6403 **Acute rheumatic fever** (ARF) is a systemic inflammatory disease that develops **2–3 weeks** after tonsillitis or pharyngitis caused by group A beta-hemolytic streptococcus in genetically predisposed individuals and mainly involves the cardiovascular system, the joints, the central nervous system and the skin. It most often occurs in children and adolescents aged **7–15 (20) years**, more often in girls.
s6404 **Rheumatic heart disease** is a chronic disease formed after ARF and characterized by permanent damage to the heart valves (a defect).
s6405 1.1. Etiology and pathogenesis
s6406 The main cause is **group A beta-hemolytic streptococcus** (GABHS). **Molecular mimicry** plays the main role in the pathogenesis: the streptococcal M protein and other antigens resemble antigens of the heart tissues (myosin, valves), the synovium of the joints and the basal ganglia of the brain.
s6407 The antibodies and T lymphocytes produced against the streptococcus damage the body’s own tissues (autoimmune inflammation). The morphological basis is disorganization of the connective tissue (mucoid swelling → fibrinoid changes → granulomatosis — **Aschoff–Talalayev granulomas** → sclerosis).
s6408 1.2. Clinical picture
s6409 The disease usually begins 2–3 weeks after tonsillitis or pharyngitis: fever (38–39 °C), weakness, sweating, joint pain.

s6447 2. General information on acquired heart defects
s6448 **A heart defect** is a permanent anatomical change of the valves or orifices that disturbs the blood flow. If a valve does not close completely, it is **regurgitation** (insufficiency); if an orifice is narrowed, it is **stenosis**. If both are present on one valve, it is a **combined (complex)** defect; if several valves are involved, it is a **multivalvular** defect.
s6449 **Causes:** rheumatic heart disease (the main cause in developing countries), infective endocarditis, atherosclerosis and degenerative calcification of the valves (in the elderly), connective tissue diseases, syphilis, mitral valve prolapse, cardiac injuries.
s6450 **Stages of the course of defects:** compensation (the circulation is maintained through hypertrophy and dilation of the cardiac chambers, no complaints) → subcompensation → decompensation (heart failure).
s6451 3. Mitral valve regurgitation
s6452 **Mitral regurgitation** is incomplete closure of the mitral valve leaflets during systole with backflow of blood from the left ventricle into the left atrium (regurgitation).
s6453 **Types: organic** (wrinkling and shortening of the leaflets and chordae: rheumatism, endocarditis, prolapse, chordal rupture) and **functional (relative)** — dilation of the valve ring and the left ventricle (dilated cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, aortic defects), papillary muscle dysfunction (myocardial infarction).
s6454 Figure 13.1
s6455 Hemodynamics in mitral valve regurgitation (Gadayev A. et al., 2012, Fig. 120)
s6456 **Hemodynamics:** in systole part of the blood returns to the left atrium → the left atrium is overfilled with blood and dilates → in diastole excess blood also enters the left ventricle → **dilation and hypertrophy of the left ventricle** (volume overload). Compensation is maintained for a long time; in decompensation the pressure in the left atrium rises, and congestion in the pulmonary circulation, pulmonary hypertension and later right ventricular failure develop.

s6468 4. Mitral stenosis (narrowing of the left atrioventricular orifice)
s6469 **Mitral stenosis** is narrowing of the left atrioventricular orifice resulting from fusion of the mitral valve leaflets along the commissures, their thickening and calcification, and shortening of the chordae. It is almost always of **rheumatic** origin and is more common in women.
s6470 Figure 13.2
s6471 Hemodynamics in mitral stenosis (Gadayev A. et al., 2012, Fig. 119)
s6472 **Hemodynamics:** the normal mitral orifice area is **4–6 cm^2^**. When the orifice narrows, blood cannot pass completely from the left atrium into the left ventricle in diastole → **the pressure in the left atrium rises**, and the atrium hypertrophies and dilates → the pressure is transmitted to the pulmonary veins and capillaries (**“passive” or venous pulmonary hypertension**) → reflex spasm of the pulmonary arterioles (**the Kitaev reflex** — “active”, arterial pulmonary hypertension, the “second barrier”) → **hypertrophy of the right ventricle**
s6473 **and its dilation** → right ventricular failure. The left ventricle, being underfilled, remains unchanged or becomes smaller.

s6491 5. Acute rheumatic fever: extended information
s6492 5.1. Epidemiology and risk factors
s6493 ARF and rheumatic heart disease remain the main cause of acquired heart defects among young people worldwide, especially in developing countries. According to the WHO, **more than 40 million** people in the world live with rheumatic heart disease. Risk factors: crowded and poor living conditions, delayed medical care, inadequate treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis, hereditary predisposition (familial cases, certain HLA antigens, the D8/17 marker of B lymphocytes).
s6494 5.2. Pathomorphology
s6495 Rheumatic inflammation of the connective tissue proceeds in four stages:
s6496 **Mucoid swelling** (1–2 weeks) — reversible changes;
s6497 **Fibrinoid changes** (2–4 weeks) — irreversible;
s6498 **Granulomatosis** — **Aschoff–Talalayev granulomas** (clusters of large basophilic cells in the heart tissue) — 1–3 months;
s6499 **Sclerosis** — scar formation; in the valves — thickening, fusion and wrinkling of the leaflets, shortening of the chordae → a defect.
s6500 **Rheumatic valvulitis** most often affects the mitral valve (70–75 %), then the aortic valve; the tricuspid and pulmonary valves are rarely involved (and usually together with others).

s6523 5.4. Rheumatic chorea
s6524 **Minor chorea** (Sydenham chorea) is autoimmune damage to the subcortical nuclei of the brain. It most often occurs in girls aged 5–15 years, **1–6 months** after the streptococcal infection (so other signs and laboratory activity may be absent). Signs: involuntary, irregular, purposeless movements (facial expressions, limbs), muscle hypotonia, impaired coordination, emotional lability. The movements become stronger with excitement and disappear during sleep. Clinical tests: **the “choreic hand”** (when the arms are stretched forward, the wrist flexes and the fingers extend), **the “milkmaid’s grip”** (rhythmic relaxation when squeezing the physician’s fingers), inability to keep the tongue protruded.
