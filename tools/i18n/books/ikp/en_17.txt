s3647 10. Pathogenesis of pulmonary emphysema
s3648 The main theory of the development of emphysema is **an imbalance between proteases and antiproteases**. Under the influence of smoking and other irritants, neutrophils and macrophages accumulate in the lung and release **elastase** and other proteases, which destroy the elastic fibers in the alveolar walls. Normally this process is stopped by **alpha~1~-antitrypsin**. In its congenital deficiency or in smoking (the smoke inactivates the antiproteases), the alveolar walls are destroyed, and the alveoli merge into large air spaces.
s3649 As a result: the elastic recoil of the lung decreases → the small bronchi collapse during expiration (**expiratory collapse**, “air trapping”) → the residual volume and total lung capacity increase (**hyperinflation**) → the diaphragm flattens and works inefficiently → dyspnea increases; the gas-exchange surface and the capillaries decrease → diffusion is impaired.
s3650 **Pursed-lip breathing** (“puffing”) is a useful technique that emphysema patients discover themselves: the resistance created at the mouth raises the pressure in the airways and prevents early collapse of the small bronchi. This technique is also taught in breathing exercises.

s3725 14. When bronchiectasis, bronchial asthma and COPD coexist
s3726 In practice, diseases often coexist:
s3727 **Asthma–COPD overlap** — in an asthma patient who smokes, or in a COPD patient showing eosinophilia and high reversibility; according to the GINA and GOLD recommendations, such patients must be given ICS.
s3728 **COPD and bronchiectasis** — detected on CT; runs a course with frequent bacterial exacerbations and much purulent sputum.
s3729 **COPD and heart failure** — both cause dyspnea; echocardiography, NT-proBNP and spirometry are used together for the diagnosis.
s3730 15. Complications of pulmonary emphysema and bullae
s3731 **Bullae** are thin-walled air spaces more than 1 cm in diameter. Large bullae (more than a third of the lung field) compress the healthy tissue (the “vanishing lung” syndrome). Complications: **spontaneous pneumothorax** (rupture of a bulla — sudden severe dyspnea in a COPD patient) and infection. On physical examination, a tympanic note and markedly diminished breath sounds over a large bulla resemble pneumothorax; they are distinguished by CT.
