s0506 14. Medical records and legal responsibility
s0507 The case history (the medical record of the inpatient) and the outpatient card are **legal documents** that confirm the patient’s rights, the physician’s work and the quality of medical care. The following requirements apply to them:
s0508 entries are made **on time**, accurately and legibly, with the date and time indicated and with a signature;
s0509 the results of investigations, the treatment prescribed and the patient’s consent (or refusal) are recorded;
s0510 corrections are made in the established manner (without erasing the old entry: it is crossed out, dated and signed);
s0511 information about the patient is **medical confidentiality** and is not given to third parties except in cases established by law.
s0512 The Law of the Republic of Uzbekistan “On the Protection of the Health of Citizens” establishes the patient’s rights — respectful treatment, receiving information about his or her health, giving consent to medical intervention or refusing it, and the preservation of medical confidentiality.

s0659 Case 1
s0660 A 58-year-old man presented saying “my heart is being squeezed”. The student immediately asked, “The pain spreads to your left arm, right?”, the patient answered “yes”, and the student wrote “angina pectoris” in the case history.
s0661 What mistakes did the student make? How should the complaint have been analyzed?
s0662 The student asked a leading question and wrote a diagnosis without clarifying the patient’s words. The correct way is to start with an open question (“Describe the squeezing”) and to analyze the pain according to the plan: site, onset (on exertion?), character, radiation, duration, the effect of nitroglycerin and rest, associated symptoms. The complaint is written in the case history in medical terms after the analysis, and the diagnosis is made only after all the information has been collected.
s0663 Case 2
s0664 A 35-year-old woman complains of palpitations and dyspnea. From the life history: in childhood she often had tonsillitis, and at the age of 14 she had “migrating” pain in the knee and ankle joints and was treated in hospital.
s0665 Which information in the life history is important? Which disease can be considered?
s0666 Recurrent tonsillitis in childhood and migrating polyarthritis are characteristic of acute rheumatic fever. The present palpitations and dyspnea give grounds to think of rheumatic heart disease (most often mitral stenosis) and its complications (atrial fibrillation, heart failure). This example shows how important it is to collect the life history in full.
s0667 Case 3
s0668 After a patient was admitted to hospital, penicillin injections were prescribed. After the first injection the patient developed dizziness, flushing of the skin and a sharp fall in blood pressure. It later turned out that the patient had wanted to say that he had previously “come out in a rash” from penicillin, but nobody had asked.
s0669 Which stage of history taking was missed? What are the consequences of such an error?
s0670 The allergy history was not collected. As a result, the patient developed drug anaphylaxis (anaphylactic shock), a life-threatening condition. Every patient must be asked about reactions to drugs, foods and other allergens, and the result is recorded separately on the first page of the case history.

s0701 Conditions of the inspection: the room should be warm (so that the patient does not get chilled) and quiet; the light should be **natural, diffuse daylight**: jaundice and pallor are poorly seen in artificial (electric) light; the light should fall from the side: protrusions and depressions on the body surface and pulsation are then well seen; the patient’s body is uncovered step by step, and the parts not being examined are kept covered; the symmetrical parts of the body are always compared.
s0702 The total GCS score ranges from 3 to 15: **15** — clear consciousness; **13–14** — obtundation; **9–12** — sopor (stupor); **8 or less** — coma (the airway needs protecting).
s0703 Coma by the odor of the breath: **“Acetone — Ammonia — Alcohol — Liver”**. Acetone — diabetic coma, ammonia (urine) — uremic coma, alcohol — alcoholic coma, sweetish-musty “liver odor” (fetor hepaticus) — hepatic coma.
s0704 In lung abscess and bronchiectasis, the patient lies on the affected side, because on turning onto the healthy side, pus flows through the draining bronchus into the trachea and increases the cough and sputum production. However, this very feature is used in treatment: for **postural drainage**, the patient is placed in the position in which the sputum drains out.
