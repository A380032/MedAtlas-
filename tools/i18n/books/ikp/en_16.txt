s3457 **The forced expiratory time test** is a simple bedside test: the patient takes a deep breath and breathes out as fast and as completely as possible with the mouth open, while the physician places the stethoscope over the trachea and measures the time. Normally expiration takes **less than 4 seconds**; more than 6 seconds indicates bronchial obstruction.
s3458 2. Acute bronchitis
s3459 **Acute bronchitis** is an acute diffuse inflammation of the bronchial mucosa. It most often develops as a manifestation of acute respiratory viral infections and accounts for 20–25 percent of acute nonspecific respiratory diseases.
s3460 **Causes:** viruses (respiratory syncytial virus, rhinoviruses, adenoviruses, influenza), bacteria (pneumococcus, Haemophilus influenzae), physical and chemical factors (cold air, dust, acid and alkali vapors). Predisposing factors: chilling, smoking, alcohol, foci of infection in the nasopharynx.
s3461 **Clinical picture:** the disease often begins with a runny nose, low-grade fever and weakness. A scratchy feeling behind the sternum and a dry or scantily productive cough appear; after 2–3 days the cough loosens and mucoid or mucopurulent sputum is produced. In the uncomplicated course there is no dyspnea; its appearance indicates involvement of the small bronchi and bronchioles (bronchiolitis).
s3462 **Physical signs:** a clear lung note on percussion; on auscultation, harsh breathing, dry (humming and whistling) rales and, when the sputum increases, nonconsonating moist rales.
s3463 **Laboratory and X-ray:** no changes in the blood, or mild leukocytosis and a raised ESR (15–20 mm/h); no changes on X-ray, or an accentuated lung pattern.
s3464 **Course:** usually 10–14 days, ending in complete recovery. The most common complication is focal pneumonia. Fever lasting more than 3–4 days and a sharp rise in leukocytosis indicate that pneumonia has developed.
s3465 3. Chronic bronchitis
s3466 **Chronic bronchitis** is a diffuse, long-standing inflammation of the bronchi accompanied by hypersecretion of mucus and impaired drainage function.
s3467 **Causes:** tobacco smoking (the main cause in about 80 percent of patients), occupational factors (cotton, coal and cement dust, chemical vapors), air pollution, recurrent respiratory infections, difficulty breathing through the nose.
s3468 **Classification:** by the nature of the exudate — catarrhal and purulent; by external respiratory function — obstructive and nonobstructive; by the course — phases of exacerbation and remission.
s3469 **Clinical picture:** the main sign is **cough**. At first it occurs in the morning after waking, with a small amount of sputum (the “smoker’s cough”); it gets worse in the cold, damp season. Later the sputum becomes mucopurulent and purulent. When obstruction and emphysema develop, **exertional dyspnea** appears and gradually increases.
s3470 **Physical signs:** in the uncomplicated course there are no changes on percussion; on auscultation, harsh breathing, dry rales and nonconsonating moist rales. In the obstructive form, prolonged expiration and wheezes; when emphysema develops, its signs appear.
s3471 **Complications:** pulmonary emphysema, respiratory failure, pulmonary hypertension and chronic cor pulmonale, pneumonia, bronchiectasis.

s3484 5. Bronchial asthma
s3485 **Bronchial asthma** is a heterogeneous disease with chronic inflammation of the airways. It is characterized by respiratory symptoms that vary over time and in intensity (wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness, cough) and by **variable expiratory airflow** (GINA 2025).
s3486 **Etiology:** exogenous allergens (plant pollen, house dust and mites, particles from pets’ fur, molds, foods), infections, drugs (aspirin and other NSAIDs, beta-blockers), occupational factors, physical exertion, cold air, psychological factors, hereditary predisposition.
s3487 **Pathogenesis:** it is based on sensitization of the organism and an IgE-dependent immediate (type I) allergic reaction. When the allergen binds to IgE fixed on the mast cells of the bronchial mucosa, histamine, leukotrienes and other mediators are released, causing **bronchospasm, mucosal edema and hypersecretion**. Eosinophilic inflammation and bronchial hyperreactivity form the basis of the disease.

s3523 6. The syndrome of increased air content of the lung tissue. Pulmonary emphysema
s3524 **Pulmonary emphysema** is a permanent enlargement of the air spaces distal to the terminal bronchioles with destruction of the alveolar walls. As a result, the lung tissue loses its elasticity, its air content increases and the gas-exchange surface decreases.
s3525 Causes and types:
s3526 **Primary (diffuse, panacinar)** — congenital alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency; in young people, in the lower parts of the lungs.
s3527 **Secondary (centriacinar)** — chronic obstructive bronchitis, long-standing bronchial asthma, smoking; more often in the upper lobes.
s3528 **Vicarious (compensatory)** — in the remaining part of the lung when part of it has been removed or is not functioning.
s3529 **Senile emphysema** — age-related decrease in elasticity.
s3530 **Bullous emphysema** — large air-filled blebs (bullae); if they rupture, pneumothorax.

s3541 Two classic phenotypes of patients with COPD are distinguished: the **“pink puffer”** (emphysematous type) — thin, very breathless, exhales through pursed lips, little cyanosis; the **“blue bloater”** (bronchitic type) — heavier, coughs a lot and produces sputum, with diffuse cyanosis and early right ventricular failure (edema). s3631 9. COPD: clinical forms, exacerbation and complications
s3632 **An exacerbation of COPD** is a worsening of dyspnea and/or cough with increased sputum within 14 days; its causes are most often viral and bacterial infections and air pollution. The **Anthonisen criteria** of exacerbation: increased dyspnea, increased sputum volume and purulent sputum; if all three signs are present, an antibiotic is indicated.
