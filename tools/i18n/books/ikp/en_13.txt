s2972 In vesicular breathing the ratio of the inspiratory to the expiratory phase is **3:1** (inspiration long and loud, expiration short and soft). It is best heard over the anterior lower parts of the chest, the axillary regions and the infrascapular regions (where the layer of lung tissue is thickest).
s2973 In a healthy person, bronchial breathing is heard only **over the larynx and trachea and in the region of the manubrium**, and behind in the interscapular region at the level of the **VII cervical to the III–IV thoracic vertebrae**. If bronchial breathing is heard over other parts of the chest, it is considered **pathological**.
s2974 In obstructive atelectasis (a bronchus blocked by a tumor), bronchial breathing is **not heard** even though the lung tissue is dense, because there is no airflow — breath sounds are markedly diminished or absent. In compression atelectasis (when fluid compresses the lung), bronchial patency is preserved, so soft bronchial breathing is heard over the upper border of the fluid.
s2975 Three tests distinguish the three sounds: **“Cough — Pressure — Sham breathing”**. If the sound changes after coughing, it is **rales**; if it increases when pressing with the stethoscope and is heard even during sham breathing, it is a **pleural friction rub**; if it occurs only at the end of inspiration and does not change after coughing, it is **crepitation**.
s2976 In modern English-language textbooks (ATS/ERS nomenclature, Bates, Macleod) the terms are different: **crackles** are divided into fine and coarse types and cover moist rales and crepitation together; **wheezes** are high-pitched dry rales; **rhonchi** are low-pitched dry rales; **stridor** is a loud inspiratory sound in narrowing of the upper airways. The “crepitation” of the Russian and Uzbek school roughly corresponds to the English fine end-inspiratory crackles. Keep this difference in mind when reading the literature.
s2977 “Artifact” sounds on auscultation: rubbing of the stethoscope head against clothing or hair (resembles crepitation), muscle tremor (in a cold room, with fear — a low rumble), the patient swallowing, the tubes touching each other, intestinal peristalsis (in the left lower region). If a doubtful sound is detected, press the stethoscope more firmly, ask the patient not to move and listen again.
s2978 The easiest way to distinguish bronchial from vesicular breathing is **the pause between the phases of respiration**: in bronchial breathing there is a short silence between inspiration and expiration, whereas in vesicular breathing there is no pause (inspiration passes without a break into expiration).
s2979 Vocal fremitus, bronchophony and egophony obey the same rule: **they are increased in consolidation and decreased in fluid, air, obstruction and emphysema**. All three confirm one another.
s2980 On auscultation, always keep to the order **“Type — Intensity — Phases — Added sounds — Bronchophony”**: first the type of breathing, then its intensity, the ratio of the respiratory phases, the added sounds and, last of all, bronchophony.
s2981 1. General information on auscultation
s2982 **Auscultation** (Latin auscultatio — listening) is a method of assessing the state of organs by listening to the sounds produced during their activity.
s2983 **Direct auscultation** (with the ear placed directly on the body) has been known since the time of Hippocrates. **Indirect auscultation** was discovered in 1816 by the French physician **René Laennec**: while examining a young woman, he rolled a sheet of paper into a tube, placed it on her chest and found that the heart sounds were clearly audible. He then made a wooden stethoscope and in 1819 published the book “On Mediate Auscultation”, in which many terms still in use today (rales, bronchial breathing, egophony) were described.

s3002 2. General rules of auscultation
s3003 The room should be warm and quiet.
s3004 The patient stands or sits, undressed to the waist; severely ill patients are examined lying down, being turned onto each side in turn.

s3020 3. The main breath sounds
s3021 3.1. Vesicular (alveolar) breathing
s3022 **Mechanism of production:** during inspiration, air enters the alveoli and rapidly tenses their walls — the vibrations of the walls of millions of alveoli add up to produce vesicular breathing. At the beginning of expiration it continues briefly owing to the relaxation of the alveolar walls.
s3023 **Features:** a soft, blowing sound, resembling the sound produced when saying the letter “f” or drawing air inward. It is heard **throughout** inspiration and only during the **first third** of expiration.
