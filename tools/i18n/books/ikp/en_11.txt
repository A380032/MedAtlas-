s2516 Percussion sets the tissues into vibration to a **depth of 5–7 cm** and **2–4 cm to the side** of the point struck. That is why deep-seated foci or foci smaller than 2–3 cm in diameter are not detected by percussion.
s2517 Common mistakes: striking with movement at the elbow or shoulder joint (the blow becomes heavy and imprecise), keeping the plexor finger on the pleximeter (the sound is damped), other fingers touching the body under the pleximeter, and long nails (the nail of the plexor finger must be short).
s2518 The rule of topographic percussion: **“From resonant to dull, parallel, gently, the border on the resonant side”**.
s2519 Even in a healthy person, the note may differ slightly at symmetrical points: over the right apex the note is somewhat duller than on the left (the right apex lies lower, and the muscles of the right shoulder girdle are better developed); on the left in the II–III intercostal spaces it is somewhat duller because of the proximity of the heart; on the right in the lower parts it is dulled because of the proximity of the liver; on the left below it has a tympanic tinge because of Traube’s space (the fundus of the stomach).
s2520 The normal width of Krönig’s isthmus is **5–6 cm** (individual variation 3.5–8 cm). The textbook gives the normal value as **3–8 cm**.
s2521 Remember the lower border of the right lung from front to back: **“5-6-7-8-9-10-11”** — the parasternal line in the V intercostal space, the midclavicular VI, the anterior axillary VII, the mid-axillary VIII, the posterior axillary IX, the scapular X, and the paravertebral line at the XI vertebra. On the left along the mid-axillary line, it is one rib lower — **IX**.
s2522 The total mobility of the lower edge of the lung: **4–6 cm along the midclavicular and scapular lines**, **6–8 cm along the mid-axillary line** (3–4 cm each on inspiration and on expiration).
s2523 Percussion is not performed over the scapula: the thick bone and muscles damp the sound, and the result is unreliable. In the interscapular region the pleximeter is placed **vertically**, and in the infrascapular regions it is again placed parallel to the ribs (horizontally).
s2524 Textbooks give different normal values for Krönig’s isthmus: Gadayev — **3–8 cm**, Grebenev and Mukhin — **5–6 cm** (individual variation 3.5–8 cm). In practice, **comparing the two sides** matters more than the absolute figure: unilateral narrowing (for example, 3 cm on the right and 6 cm on the left) raises suspicion of apical tuberculosis or pneumosclerosis.
s2525 The lower border: **“up — pushed from below, down — the lung is inflated”**: its rise is most often due to pressure from below (ascites, meteorism, an enlarged liver) or to shrinkage of the lung; its descent is a sign of increased lung volume (emphysema).
s2526 The absence of changes on percussion does not mean the absence of disease. If there is clinical suspicion, radiography, US or CT is performed. However, percussion and auscultation are the first step: they direct the investigation correctly and make it possible to follow the dynamics at the bedside.
s2527 The “triad” of physical signs: **Percussion — Fremitus — Breath sounds**. It is enough to remember each syndrome by these three parameters: consolidation — “Dull, Increased, Bronchial”; fluid — “Dull, Absent, Absent”; air — “Tympanic, Absent, Absent”; emphysema — “Hyperresonant, Decreased, Decreased”.
s2528 1. General information on percussion
s2529 **Percussion** (Latin percussio — tapping) is a method of determining the physical state, borders and size of the underlying organs by tapping the body surface and assessing the characteristics of the resulting sound.
s2530 The method was discovered in 1761 by the Austrian physician **Leopold Auenbrugger** (“Inventum novum”). He was inspired by his father, a wine producer, who determined the amount of wine in barrels by tapping them. In 1808 the French physician **J. Corvisart** introduced the method widely into practice. After percussion with a pleximeter (1828, P. Piorry) and a hammer, **finger-on-finger percussion** eventually prevailed; Russian clinicians (G. Sokolov, F. Yanovsky, V. Obraztsov) played a large role in its development.
s2531 1.1. Physical basis of percussion
s2532 When tapped, the body tissues vibrate and produce a sound. The character of the sound depends on the **density**, **air content** and **elasticity** of the tissue.

s2567 2.1. Technique of finger-on-finger percussion
s2568 The **pleximeter finger** (the middle finger of the left hand) is applied closely to the body surface, but without excessive pressure; the other fingers are slightly separated and do not touch the body.
s2569 The **plexor finger** (the middle finger of the right hand) is bent and strikes the **middle phalanx** of the pleximeter perpendicularly, using movement at the **wrist joint** only.
s2570 The blow is short and sharp, and the finger is withdrawn at once after striking (it “bounces back”).
s2571 **Two** blows of equal strength are delivered at each point.
s2572 The strength of the blows is kept the same throughout the examination.

s2575 2.2. Strength of percussion
s2576 **Loud (strong, deep) percussion** — for examining deep-seated organs (up to 7 cm); used in comparative percussion.
s2577 **Moderate percussion** — the strength most often used in comparative percussion.
s2578 **Quiet (weak) percussion** — for determining the borders of organs (topographic percussion).
s2579 **The quietest (threshold) percussion** — Goldscheider’s method; for determining the absolute cardiac dullness, with sound at the threshold of hearing.
s2580 3. General rules of performing percussion
s2581 The room should be warm and quiet.
s2582 The patient is in a comfortable position (standing, sitting or lying), with the muscles relaxed.
s2583 The physician’s hands are warm and the nails short.
s2584 In **comparative percussion**, symmetrical points are compared alternately.
s2585 In **topographic percussion**, percussion proceeds from the resonant note (clear lung note) toward the dull note; the pleximeter is placed **parallel** to the border being determined, and each step does not exceed **1–2 cm**.
s2586 The border is marked **on the side of the resonant note** (along the edge of the pleximeter facing the resonant side).

s2620 5. Comparative percussion of the lungs
s2621 **Purpose** — to detect a pathological focus by comparing the percussion note at symmetrical points of the chest. Percussion is performed with moderate or loud strength.
s2622 5.1. Order of comparative percussion
s2623 **In front** (the patient stands or sits with the arms lowered):
s2624 The supraclavicular fossae (pleximeter parallel to the clavicle).
s2625 The clavicles themselves (direct percussion — the finger strikes the middle part of the clavicle).
s2626 The I and II intercostal spaces along the midclavicular line.
s2627 Because the heart lies lower down on the left, comparative percussion is performed symmetrically only down to the III intercostal space.
s2628 **From the side** (the patient places the hands on the head): starting from the axillary fossa, the IV and V intercostal spaces along the mid-axillary line.
s2629 **From behind** (the patient crosses the arms over the chest and bends the head slightly — the shoulder blades move apart):
s2630 The suprascapular regions (pleximeter parallel to the spine of the scapula).
s2631 The interscapular region — the pleximeter is placed **vertically** (parallel to the spine), at three points from above downward.
s2632 The infrascapular regions — in the VII, VIII and IX intercostal spaces below the angle of the scapula, with the pleximeter parallel to the ribs.
