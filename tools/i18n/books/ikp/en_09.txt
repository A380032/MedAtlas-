s1974 In an elderly patient, a respiratory rate of **more than 25** per minute is often the earliest and most sensitive sign of pneumonia. That is why the respiratory rate must be counted accurately in every elderly patient.
s1975 1. Brief anatomy and physiology of the respiratory organs
s1976 The respiratory system consists of the upper airways (nose, pharynx, larynx), the trachea, the bronchi and the lungs. At the level of the IV–V thoracic vertebrae, the trachea divides into the right and left main bronchi (the bifurcation). The right lung has three lobes and the left lung two; each lung is divided into 10 segments. The main structure of gas exchange is the **acinus** (respiratory bronchiole, alveolar ducts and alveoli).
s1977 The three stages of external respiration are **ventilation** (the movement of air into and out of the alveoli), **diffusion** (the exchange of O~2~ and CO~2~ across the alveolar-capillary membrane) and **perfusion** (blood flow in the pulmonary capillaries). Impairment of any of them leads to respiratory failure.

s2048 2.4. Choking attacks (asthma)
s2049 A **choking attack** is a suddenly developing attack of severe dyspnea. Two types are distinguished:
s2050 **An attack of bronchial asthma** — expiratory dyspnea, wheezes audible at a distance, expectoration of glassy viscous sputum at the end of the attack;
s2051 **Cardiac asthma** — the result of acute left ventricular failure, usually at night; inspiratory or mixed dyspnea, the orthopnea position, moist rales in the lower parts of the lungs, and in severe cases pink frothy sputum (pulmonary edema).
s2052 2.5. Hemoptysis (haemoptysis)
s2053 Hemoptysis is the expectoration of blood with sputum on coughing. If more than 50 ml of blood is expectorated per day, it is called **pulmonary hemorrhage**. Causes: tuberculosis, lung cancer, bronchiectasis, lung abscess, pulmonary infarction, mitral stenosis, lobar pneumonia.

s2074 2.6. Chest pain
s2075 In respiratory diseases, pain arises mainly from irritation of the **parietal pleura** (the lung tissue has no pain receptors).
s2076 **Pleuritic pain** is stabbing, increases with deep inspiration and coughing, and lessens when the patient lies on the affected side or presses the chest with the hand (dry pleurisy, lobar pneumonia, pulmonary infarction, pneumothorax). In **intercostal neuralgia**, the pain spreads along the intercostal space and increases when the trunk bends to the healthy side. **The pain of angina pectoris**, by contrast, does not depend on breathing and appears on physical exertion.
s2077 2.7. Features of the history
s2078 In the history of present illness, the link of the disease with chilling or influenza and the periods of exacerbation and remission are established. In the life history, **smoking** (pack-years), **occupational hazards** (dust, silica, asbestos, chemicals), **allergic diseases** (pollinosis, eczema), past tuberculosis, contact with a tuberculosis patient and bronchial asthma in the family must always be asked about.
s2079 3. Inspection of the chest
s2080 The chest is inspected **statically** (shape, symmetry, supraclavicular and infraclavicular fossae, position of the shoulder blades, direction of the ribs, epigastric angle) and **dynamically** (type, rate, depth and rhythm of breathing, equal participation of both sides in breathing, use of the accessory muscles). The patient stands or sits, undressed to the waist.

s2111 3.2. Asymmetry of the chest
s2112 **Enlargement (bulging) of one side** — accumulation of a large amount of fluid (exudative pleurisy, hydrothorax) or air (pneumothorax) in the pleural cavity. On this side the intercostal spaces are smoothed out or bulge, and the side lags behind in breathing.
s2113 **Reduction (retraction) of one side** — obstructive atelectasis, removal of a lung (pneumonectomy), pleural adhesions (schwartes), pneumosclerosis. The shoulder is lowered, the shoulder blade protrudes backward, and the intercostal spaces are narrowed.
s2114 3.3. Type, rate, depth and rhythm of breathing
s2115 **Types of breathing:** the thoracic (costal) type — mainly in women; the abdominal (diaphragmatic) type — mainly in men; the mixed type — in the elderly. A change from the thoracic to the abdominal type is seen in pleurisy, rib fractures and intercostal neuralgia; a change from the abdominal to the thoracic type in diaphragmatic paralysis, peritonitis, ascites and meteorism.
s2116 **The respiratory rate** is counted for one minute without the patient noticing (for example, as if counting the pulse), with the hand placed on the chest or abdominal wall.

s2142 4. Palpation of the chest
s2143 Palpation determines the following: tender areas, the resistance (elasticity) of the chest, the equal participation of both sides in the respiratory movements and **vocal fremitus** (fremitus vocalis).
s2144 4.1. Detecting tender areas
s2145 The chest is palpated with the palms of both hands over symmetrical areas from above downward. Localized tenderness is found in rib fractures (a “grating” on pressure — crepitus), intercostal neuralgia (Valleix points: next to the spine, on the axillary line, next to the sternum) and muscle inflammation.

s2148 4.2. Determining the resistance (elasticity) of the chest
s2149 Standing beside the patient, the physician places one palm on the sternum and the other on the interscapular region and compresses the chest from front to back; then the two hands are placed on the sides and the chest is compressed from the sides. Normally the chest is elastic — it yields when compressed.

s2155 4.3. Determining vocal fremitus
s2156 **Vocal fremitus** is the transmission of the sound vibrations generated in the larynx during speech through the bronchi and lung tissue to the chest wall. The patient is asked to say, in a low (bass) voice, words containing the letter “r” (“thirty-three”, “ninety-nine”, “tractor”). The physician places the palms on symmetrical areas, compares the fremitus on the two sides, then swaps the hands and examines again.
s2157 **Areas examined:** in front — the supraclavicular and infraclavicular regions and the lateral surfaces of the chest; behind — the suprascapular, interscapular and infrascapular regions.

s2180 5. Anatomical and physiological basis of the respiratory system
s2181 Figure 4.16
s2182 General structure of the respiratory system (Gadayev A. et al., 2012, Fig. 1)
s2183 Figure 4.17
s2184 Gas exchange between the air in the alveoli and the red cells (same source, Fig. 2)
s2185 The airways are divided into the **conducting** part (from the nasal cavity to the terminal bronchioles — it does not take part in gas exchange; the “anatomical dead space”, about 150 ml) and the **respiratory** part (respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveoli). The two lungs contain about **300 million alveoli** with a total surface area of **70–100 m^2^**. The alveolar-capillary membrane is only **0.5 µm** thick, so gas diffusion is very rapid.
s2186 **The pleura** is a serous membrane consisting of two layers: the **visceral** layer (covers the lung and has no pain receptors) and the **parietal** layer (lines the inner surface of the chest wall, the diaphragm and the mediastinum and is rich in pain receptors). Between the layers there is 5–15 ml of fluid, and the pressure between them is negative — this keeps the lung in the expanded state.
