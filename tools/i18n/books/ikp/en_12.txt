s2660 6. Topographic percussion of the lungs
s2661 **Purpose:** to determine the height and width of the lung apices (Krönig’s isthmus), the lower borders of the lungs and the respiratory mobility of their lower edges. Percussion is performed with **quiet** strength.
s2662 6.1. Determining the height of the lung apices
s2663 **In front:** the pleximeter is placed in the supraclavicular fossa parallel to the clavicle, and percussion proceeds from the middle of the clavicle upward and medially (toward the sternocleidomastoid muscle) until a dull note appears. Normally the apex is **3–4 cm** above the clavicle.
s2664 **Behind:** the pleximeter is moved horizontally from the middle of the spine of the scapula toward the spinous process of the VII cervical vertebra. Normally the apex is at the level of the **spinous process of the VII cervical vertebra**.

s2669 6.2. Determining Krönig’s isthmus
s2670 **Krönig’s isthmus** is the width of the area of clear lung note over the lung apex along the anterior edge of the trapezius muscle. The pleximeter is placed perpendicular to the middle of the edge of the trapezius, and percussion proceeds first medially (toward the neck) and then laterally (toward the shoulder) until a dull note appears. The distance between the two points is measured.

s2674 Increased height of the apices and widening of Krönig’s isthmus — **pulmonary emphysema**.
s2675 Decreased height of the apices and narrowing of Krönig’s isthmus — **tuberculous infiltrate or pneumosclerosis** of the apex, shrinkage of the apex. A unilateral change is especially significant.
s2676 6.3. Determining the lower borders of the lungs
s2677 Percussion proceeds along the topographic lines from above downward, starting from the II–III intercostal space (behind, from the angle of the scapula), until the clear lung note changes to a dull note (on the right, liver dullness) or, on the left, to a tympanic note (Traube’s space). The pleximeter is placed in the intercostal space, parallel to the ribs. The rib or intercostal space to which the border corresponds is counted.

s2715 7. Physical basis of percussion: a deeper analysis
s2716 According to their acoustic properties, the body tissues fall into three groups:
s2717 **Airless and dense tissues** (muscles, liver, spleen, heart, fluid) — damp vibrations quickly and give a low-amplitude, short sound (dull, the thigh note).
s2718 **Air-containing tissue with elastic walls** (the lung) — the alveoli with their tense walls hold the air; the vibrations are irregular (of different frequencies), so the sound is loud and long but not tympanic (the clear lung note).
s2719 **Large air-filled cavities with smooth walls** (stomach, intestines, a large cavern, pneumothorax) — the vibrations are of a single frequency (harmonic), hence the tympanic note.
s2720 The character of the percussion note depends on the ratio of the amount of air to the tension and elasticity of the tissue. For example, **when the tension of the lung tissue decreases** (the onset of pulmonary edema, stage I of lobar pneumonia, compression atelectasis), the alveolar walls slacken even though air is preserved, and a **dull-tympanic** note appears. **When the tension increases** and the air increases (emphysema), a **hyperresonant (box-like)** note results.
s2721 **Škoda’s tympany** is the dull-tympanic note heard in exudative pleurisy above the fluid level, over the compressed lung (in Garland’s triangle and above it). When the lung tissue is compressed, its tension decreases, while air is partly preserved in the alveoli.

s2759 9.3. Traube’s space
s2760 **Traube’s space** (the semilunar tympanic area) is the area of tympanic note on the left in the lower part of the chest produced by the gas bubble of the fundus of the stomach. Its borders: above — the lower edge of the left lung; on the right — the left lobe of the liver; on the left — the spleen; below — the costal arch.

s2787 11. Comparing percussion findings with modern methods
s2788 The accuracy of percussion is limited: it detects superficial (5–7 cm) changes larger than 2–3 cm. Fluid in the pleural cavity is usually detected by percussion when it exceeds **300–500 ml**; on the radiograph, blunting of the costophrenic sinus appears at 200–300 ml, whereas **on US even 50 ml** of fluid is visible.

s2802 12. Features of percussion in children and the elderly
s2803 **In children**, because the chest wall is thin and elastic, percussion is performed **more quietly** (often directly or with a fingertip); a strong blow also sets neighboring areas into vibration and masks small foci. In children the lung borders are one rib higher than in adults, and the percussion note may have a slight tympanic tinge.
s2804 **In the elderly**, the chest is rigid and the air content of the lungs is increased (senile emphysema) — the note has a box-like tinge, the lower borders have descended, and mobility is decreased. A functional investigation is needed to distinguish these changes from pathological emphysema.
s2805 13. Additional methods of percussion
s2806 13.1. Auscultatory percussion
s2807 The stethoscope is placed over the organ, and a sound is produced around it by light flicking (or by rubbing with a finger). The sound changes sharply on crossing the border of the organ. The method is used to determine the borders of the liver, stomach and heart, as well as in bedridden patients and in situations where percussion is difficult.
s2808 13.2. Additional methods of detecting pleural fluid
s2809 **Positional percussion:** free fluid (transudate) shifts when the patient’s position changes — dullness appears at the bottom when sitting and on the dependent side when lying on the side.
s2810 **Independence of the fluid border from breathing:** the fluid level does not change with breathing, whereas the lung border shifts.
s2811 14. Drawing a conclusion from percussion
s2812 Percussion findings are never assessed in isolation. For example, a dull note is found in three different conditions — consolidation of the lung, pleural fluid and atelectasis — and to distinguish them, **inspection** (size of the hemithorax), **palpation** (vocal fremitus), **auscultation** (type of breath sounds) and **the position of the mediastinum** are analyzed together.

s2829 15. The influence of the diaphragm and abdominal organs on the lung borders
s2830 **Unilateral elevation of the diaphragm** — phrenic nerve paralysis (tumor, surgery), subphrenic abscess, marked enlargement of the liver or spleen, lobar atelectasis. On percussion the lower border of the lung is raised on the affected side, and mobility is absent or paradoxical (the diaphragm rises on inspiration).
s2831 **Subphrenic abscess** — the lower border of the right lung rises, and reactive pleural fluid sometimes appears; fever, pain in the right hypochondrium, pain radiating to the shoulder.
s2832 **Meteorism and ascites** — bilateral elevation, shallow breathing.
s2833 After abdominal operations, patients do not breathe deeply because of pain; diaphragmatic movement is restricted, and **atelectasis** and **hypostatic pneumonia** develop in the lower parts. Percussion reveals dulling and decreased mobility in the lower posterior regions. Prevention — early mobilization, breathing exercises, adequate pain relief.
