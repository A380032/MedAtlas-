s1099 In **tetanus** (the disease), tetanus toxin stops the release of glycine and GABA from inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord. Motor neurons send impulses continuously at a high frequency, and skeletal muscles contract tetanically and painfully (spasm of the masticatory muscles — “risus sardonicus”, opisthotonus). In **hypocalcemia**, on the other hand, the excitability of nerves and muscles increases and tetanic convulsions (tetany) appear.
s1100 5. The motor unit
s1101 A **motor unit** is a set consisting of one motor neuron, its axon and the muscle fibers innervated by that axon. It is the functional unit of muscle: when the motor neuron is excited, all its fibers contract simultaneously.

s1127 6. Muscle strength, work and fatigue
s1128 **Muscle strength** is the maximum load a muscle can lift or its maximum isometric tension. It depends on the **physiological cross-section** (the sum of the cross-sections of all fibers). **Absolute muscle strength** is the ratio of maximum strength to the physiological cross-section (5–10 kg/cm^2^ in humans). Pennate muscles are stronger because their physiological cross-section is large.
s1129 **Muscle work** is the product of the mass of the lifted load and the height to which it is lifted (A = P × h). If the load is zero or maximal, the work is zero; the greatest work is done at an **average load** — **the law of average loads**. Likewise, the most work is done when the rhythm of work is also average (**the law of average rhythm**).
s1130 **Fatigue** is a temporary decrease in working capacity as a result of prolonged or intense work; it is restored after rest. In an isolated preparation, fatigue occurs first at the **neuromuscular synapse**; in the whole organism, mainly in the **nerve centers** (I.M. Sechenov). Sechenov showed the effect of **active rest**: if, while the tired arm is resting, work is done with the other arm, working capacity is restored faster.
s1131 7. Smooth muscles
s1132 Smooth muscles are found in the walls of internal organs (stomach, intestine, urinary bladder, uterus), blood vessels and bronchi. Their contraction is slow but long-lasting and requires little energy (**tone**).
s1133 **Single-unit (visceral)** smooth muscles — the cells are connected by gap junctions and contract as a functional syncytium; most of them have **automaticity** (pacemaker cells): intestine, stomach, ureter.
s1134 **Multi-unit** smooth muscles — each cell is innervated separately: the muscles of the pupil, the ciliary muscle, large arteries. **Mechanism of contraction:** Ca^2+^ (entering mainly from outside the cell) binds to **calmodulin** → the complex activates **myosin light-chain kinase** → myosin is phosphorylated and its interaction with actin begins. Smooth muscles contract in response to stretch (the **myogenic response**, the Bayliss effect in vessels).
s1135 Disturbed contraction of smooth muscles underlies many diseases: spasm of bronchial smooth muscle in **bronchial asthma**, increased arteriolar tone in **arterial hypertension**, intestinal spasm in **intestinal colic**. That is why Ca^2+^ channel blockers (nifedipine), β~2~-adrenergic agonists (salbutamol (albuterol)) and antispasmodics (drotaverine) are widely used.
s1136 Experiment 1: recording a single twitch, incomplete and complete tetanus
s1137 Prepare a frog nerve-muscle preparation, attach the muscle tendon to the myograph lever and bring the tip of the lever into contact with the kymograph drum.
s1138 Stimulate the nerve with a single suprathreshold stimulus — record the curve of a single twitch. With the drum rotating fast, mark the latent period and the contraction and relaxation phases.
s1139 Set the stimulus frequency on the electrical stimulator to 10–15 Hz and stimulate for 2–3 s — record incomplete tetanus.
s1140 Increase the frequency to 30–50 Hz — record complete tetanus.
s1141 Increase the frequency further (100 Hz and higher) and observe the pessimum; show that contraction is restored when the frequency is reduced (optimum). **Expected results and analysis:** at low frequency each stimulus falls into the relaxation phase of the previous contraction and incomplete tetanus arises; at high frequency the stimuli fall into the contraction phase, the contractions fuse completely and complete tetanus appears. The amplitude of tetanus is larger than that of a single twitch. At a very high frequency the impulses fall into the refractory period and exceed the lability of the synapse — pessimum.
s1142 Experiment 2: hand dynamometry
s1143 The subject stands upright and extends the arm to the side; the dynamometer is held in the palm with the dial facing inward.
s1144 It is squeezed with maximum force 2–3 times (with 1 minute of rest in between); the best result is recorded.
s1145 Both hands are measured.
s1146 The **strength index** is calculated: hand strength (kg) / body mass (kg) × 100%. **Interpretation:** the normal strength index is 65–80% in men and 48–50% in women. The working hand is usually 5–10% stronger.
s1147 Experiment 3: ergography (the Mosso ergograph)
s1148 The subject's arm is fixed in the ergograph, and a cord with a 2–4 kg load is tied to the middle finger.
s1149 To the rhythm of a metronome (every 1–2 s) the load is lifted as high as possible; the lifting height is recorded on the kymograph tape.
s1150 The work continues until the load can no longer be lifted (until fatigue).
s1151 The experiment is repeated after 5 minutes of passive rest and 5 minutes of active rest (work with the other arm).
s1152 The work done is calculated: A = mass of the load × sum of the lifting heights. **Expected results and analysis:** the ergogram first shows warming up (the height increases slightly), then stable work and fatigue (the height decreases). After active rest working capacity recovers better than after passive rest — the Sechenov phenomenon.

s1257 An unvaccinated patient whose foot had been pierced by a rusty nail developed, a week later, spasm of the masticatory muscles (inability to open the mouth), “risus sardonicus” of the face and later strong spasms of the back muscles.
s1258 What disease is this? Why do the skeletal muscles contract strongly for a long time? Which mode of contraction does this resemble?
s1259 Tetanus. Tetanus toxin stops the release of glycine and GABA from inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord and brainstem. Motor neurons remain uninhibited and continuously send impulses at a high frequency. Each impulse then falls into the contraction phase of the previous contraction, and the muscles stay contracted for a long time in a state of complete tetanus. The muscles themselves and the neuromuscular synapse are not damaged — the disturbance lies in central inhibition.
s1260 A 20-year-old athlete performs well in the 100 m sprint but has poor endurance in a marathon. His friend, on the contrary, runs long distances well.
s1261 How can this difference be explained by the types of muscle fibers (motor units)?
s1262 The sprinter's muscles contain many fast fatigable (FF, IIb, “white”) motor units: they contract quickly and strongly and obtain energy from anaerobic glycolysis, but tire quickly. In the marathon runner, slow (S, type I, “red”) and fast fatigue-resistant (FR) units predominate: they have many mitochondria, much myoglobin and many capillaries and obtain energy by oxidation — so they can work for a long time. Although the fiber ratio is mainly inherited, training partly changes their properties.
s1263 A student stimulated a nerve-muscle preparation on a kymograph at a frequency of 20 Hz and obtained a wavy curve. The duration of a single twitch is 0.1 s, the contraction phase 0.04 s.
s1264 Which type of tetanus did the student obtain? What frequency is needed to obtain complete tetanus?
s1265 At 20 Hz the interval between stimuli is 0.05 s. This is shorter than the duration of a single twitch (0.1 s) but longer than the contraction phase (0.04 s) — each stimulus falls into the relaxation phase of the previous contraction, so incomplete tetanus arose. For complete tetanus the interval must be shorter than the contraction phase: less than 0.04 s, i.e., a frequency above 25 Hz (in practice 30–50 Hz).
