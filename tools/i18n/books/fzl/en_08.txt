s1594 The **erythron** (Castle) is the set of erythrocytes in the peripheral blood, their precursors in the bone marrow and the erythrocytes at the sites of their destruction. Normally the formation and destruction of erythrocytes are in balance.
s1595 **“CO — the Cherry Con”**: carbon monoxide “occupies” Hb and the skin becomes cherry-colored, yet the tissues suffocate. A pulse oximeter is “fooled” into reading HbCO as HbO~2~ — the saturation may look normal!
s1596 The color index is a traditional parameter widely used in the practice of our country and the CIS; in international practice it has been replaced by the **MCH**. The MCH also shows the amount of hemoglobin in one erythrocyte, but in absolute units (picograms). The **MCV** (microcytic, normocytic, macrocytic) is the most important index for determining the type of anemia.
s1597 The Sahli method is subjective and has a large error (± 10%). In modern laboratories the international standard is the **hemiglobincyanide method** (photometric) or automated analyzers. The Sahli method is used for teaching purposes.
s1598 1. Structure and properties of erythrocytes
s1599 **Erythrocytes** (red blood cells) in mammals and humans are anucleate cells shaped like biconcave discs. Their diameter is **7.2–7.5 µm**, their thickness 2.5 µm at the edge and 1 µm in the center, and their volume 80–94 fl.
s1600 Advantages of the erythrocyte shape:
s1601 **Large surface area** — a biconcave disc has a surface area 1.7 times greater than a sphere, which speeds up gas diffusion.
s1602 **Short diffusion distance** — the distance from any point to the surface is small.
s1603 **Deformability** — the erythrocyte folds to pass through capillaries 3 µm in diameter (thanks to the membrane skeleton formed by spectrin, actin and ankyrin).
s1604 **Absence of a nucleus and mitochondria** — there is much room for hemoglobin; the erythrocyte does not consume the oxygen it carries (it obtains energy from anaerobic glycolysis). **Hemoglobin** makes up ≈ 34% of the erythrocyte (95% of its dry mass). Blood group antigens (A, B, Rh) are located on its membrane.
s1605 Erythrocyte count
s1606 **4.0–5.0 × 10^12^/l** in men and **3.7–4.7 × 10^12^/l** in women. Up to 6–7 × 10^12^/l in newborns.
s1607 **Erythrocytosis** is an increase in the number of erythrocytes. It may be **relative** (hemoconcentration: dehydration, heavy sweating, vomiting) or **absolute** (increased erythropoiesis: high altitude, chronic lung and heart diseases, athletes; pathological — erythremia).
s1608 **Erythropenia** is a decrease: anemias, hydremia (large fluid intake, pregnancy).

s1621 2. Erythropoiesis and its regulation
s1622 **Erythropoiesis** is the formation of erythrocytes; in adults it takes place in the **red bone marrow** (flat bones, vertebrae, sternum, pelvic bones). In the embryo erythropoiesis occurs first in the yolk sac, then in the liver and spleen. About 200 billion erythrocytes (2–3 million per second) are produced per day.
s1623 Figure 6.1
s1624 Stages of erythropoiesis
s1625 **Reticulocytes** are young erythrocytes; residual ribosomes (a reticular substance) become visible in them with special staining. Normally they make up **0.5–1.5%** (5–15‰) of the peripheral blood. The reticulocyte count reflects the erythropoietic activity of the bone marrow: it increases after blood loss or treatment with iron preparations (**reticulocyte crisis**).
s1626 Regulation of erythropoiesis
s1627 **Erythropoietin (EPO)** is the main humoral regulator of erythropoiesis, a glycoprotein hormone. It is produced mainly in the **kidneys** (90%) and in small amounts in the liver. The main factor that increases its synthesis is **tissue hypoxia**.

s1650 3. Life cycle and destruction of erythrocytes
s1651 Erythrocytes live in the blood for an average of **120 days**. The membrane of aging erythrocytes becomes rigid, and they are phagocytosed by macrophages mainly in the **spleen** (and in the liver and bone marrow) (**intracellular hemolysis**). A small proportion is destroyed inside the blood vessels (**intravascular hemolysis**).
s1652 Figure 6.3
s1653 The erythron: formation, circulation and destruction of erythrocytes (Alaviya O.T., 2018)
s1654 Breakdown of hemoglobin: **globin** is split into amino acids; **iron** returns to the bone marrow with transferrin or is stored in the liver as ferritin and hemosiderin; **heme** → biliverdin → **bilirubin** (unconjugated, carried by albumin) → conjugated with glucuronic acid in the liver (conjugated bilirubin) → into the intestine with bile → stercobilin (color of stool) and urobilin (color of urine).
s1655 4. Hemoglobin
s1656 **Hemoglobin (Hb)** is a complex protein (chromoprotein) with a molecular mass of 64,500 Da. It consists of **4 hemes** and **globin** (4 polypeptide chains). **Heme** is a compound of protoporphyrin IX with **ferrous iron (Fe^2+^)**; oxygen is bound by the iron in heme. One Hb molecule can bind **4 molecules of O~2~**.
s1657 Figure 6.4
s1658 The hemoglobin molecule: four subunits and the structure of heme (Alaviya O.T., 2018)
s1659 Hemoglobin content
s1660 **130–160 g/l** in men and **120–140 g/l** in women. 1 g of Hb binds **1.34 ml of O~2~** (Hüfner's number). Therefore, with Hb of 150 g/l the **oxygen capacity** of blood is ≈ 200 ml O~2~/l (20 vol%).

s1701 5. Color index and erythrocyte indices
s1702 The **color index (CI)** is the relative saturation of one erythrocyte with hemoglobin:
s1703 CI = (Hb, g/l × 3) / (the first three digits of the erythrocyte count)
s1704 For example: Hb 150 g/l, erythrocytes 5.0 × 10^12^/l → CI = 150 × 3 / 500 = 0.9.

s1730 Experiment 1: counting erythrocytes in a Goryaev chamber
s1731 Put 4 ml of 3% NaCl solution (or Hayem's fluid) into a test tube.
s1732 Take 0.02 ml of blood from a finger, add it to the tube (dilution 1 : 200) and mix.
s1733 Cover the Goryaev chamber with a coverslip (rub it on until “Newton's rings” appear); put a drop of the diluted blood under the coverslip.
s1734 After 1 minute, count the erythrocytes under the microscope (×8 objective) in 5 large squares along the diagonal (each consisting of 16 small squares — 80 small squares in total). Count cells on the borders by the rule “those on the left and upper lines are counted”.
s1735 Calculation: X = (A × 4000 × 200) / 80, where A is the number of erythrocytes in 80 small squares; the count per 1 µl; simplified: X = A × 10,000 (per 1 µl) or A / 100 × 10^12^/l. **Interpretation:** compare the result with the normal range for the person's sex.
s1736 Experiment 2: determining hemoglobin by the Sahli method
s1737 Pour 0.1 N HCl up to the mark into the middle tube of the Sahli hemometer.
s1738 Take 20 µl (0.02 ml) of blood, add it to the HCl and mix; wait 5 minutes — hemoglobin forms **hematin hydrochloride** (brown) with hydrochloric acid.
s1739 Add distilled water drop by drop until the color matches the standard tubes.
s1740 Read the hemoglobin content (g/dl or g/l) from the tube scale.
s1741 Experiment 3: calculating the color index
s1742 Take the results of the erythrocyte count and hemoglobin content.
s1743 Calculate by the formula CI = Hb (g/l) × 3 / the first three digits of the erythrocyte count.
s1744 Evaluate the result: normo-, hypo- or hyperchromia.
s1745 Skill: suggesting the type of anemia from a blood test
s1746 Compare Hb and the erythrocyte count with the normal values (whether anemia is present).
s1747 Calculate the color index (or MCH).
s1748 Evaluate erythrocyte size from the MCV.
s1749 Evaluate the reticulocyte count: if increased, the bone marrow is active (blood loss, hemolysis); if decreased, erythropoiesis is suppressed.
s1750 Hypochromic microcytic — think of iron deficiency; hyperchromic macrocytic — B~12~/folate deficiency; normochromic — acute blood loss or hemolysis.
s1751 Skill: calculating the oxygen capacity from the hemoglobin content
s1752 Take the Hb content in g/l.
s1753 Multiply by Hüfner's number (1.34 ml O~2~/g).
s1754 Express the result in ml O~2~/l (normally ≈ 190–210 ml/l).
