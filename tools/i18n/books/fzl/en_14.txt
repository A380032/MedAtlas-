s2911 3. The cardiac cycle
s2912 The **cardiac cycle** is the period consisting of one contraction (systole) and relaxation (diastole). At a heart rate of 75 /min the cycle lasts **0.8 s**: atrial systole 0.1 s, atrial diastole 0.7 s; ventricular systole 0.33 s, ventricular diastole 0.47 s. The period when the atria and ventricles are relaxed at the same time is the **general pause** (≈ 0.4 s).

s3023 Exercise 1: Stannius ligatures (frog heart)
s3024 Immobilize the frog, open the chest and expose the heart. Count the heart rate for 1 minute.
s3025 **Ligature I** — tie a thread at the boundary between the sinus venosus and the right atrium. The sinus venosus continues to contract at its previous rhythm; the atria and ventricle stop temporarily and then begin to contract at a slower rhythm.
s3026 **Ligature II** — tie it at the boundary between the atria and the ventricle (the AV node region). The ventricle contracts at an even slower rhythm.
s3027 **Ligature III** — tie it at the lower third of the ventricle. The apex of the heart does not contract (it has no center of automaticity). **Result and analysis:** the experiment proves the **gradient of automaticity**: the main center of automaticity is in the sinus venosus (in humans, in the sinoatrial node), the lower centers have slower rhythms, and the apex of the heart has no automaticity.
s3028 Exercise 2: extrasystole and compensatory pause (frog heart)
s3029 Connect the apex of the heart to a kymograph lever with a serrefine and record the contractions.
s3030 Stimulate the ventricle with a single electrical stimulus in different phases of systole and then in diastole.
s3031 Compare the results on the kymogram.
s3032 **Result and analysis:** a stimulus given in systole causes no response (absolute refractoriness). A stimulus given in diastole causes a premature contraction — an **extrasystole**; it is followed by a prolonged **compensatory pause**, because the next impulse from the sinus venosus falls into the refractory period of the ventricle after the extrasystole.
s3033 Exercise 3: recording an ECG in a human
s3034 Lay the subject on a couch; wipe the electrode sites with alcohol and apply contact gel.
s3035 Place the limb electrodes: red — right arm, yellow — left arm, green — left leg, black (ground) — right leg.
s3036 Place the chest electrodes at points V~1~–V~6~.

s3038 In standard lead II measure the amplitude of the waves and the duration of the intervals.
s3039 Determine the rhythm (sinus rhythm — a P wave before every QRS, equal RR intervals) and the HR (60 / RR).
s3040 Exercise 4: auscultation of the heart sounds
s3041 Listen to the subject in a sitting or lying position.
s3042 Place the stethoscope successively at the auscultation points.
s3043 At the same time palpate the carotid pulse and identify the first sound (simultaneous with the pulse).
s3044 Compare the first and second sounds by pitch, duration and pauses: the first sound is louder at the apex, the second at the base of the heart.
s3045 Skill: determining the HR and main intervals on the ECG
s3046 Determine the paper speed (at 50 mm/s 1 mm = 0.02 s; at 25 mm/s 1 mm = 0.04 s).
s3047 Measure the distance between two adjacent R waves and calculate RR (s).
s3048 HR = 60 / RR.
s3049 Measure PQ, QRS and QT and compare them with the norm.
s3050 Check the criteria of sinus rhythm: the P wave is positive (in lead II) and precedes each QRS, PQ is constant, RR intervals are equal.
s3051 Skill: calculating the minute volume and ejection fraction
s3052 Stroke volume (SV) = end-diastolic volume − end-systolic volume.
s3053 Ejection fraction = SV / end-diastolic volume × 100%.
s3054 Minute volume = SV × HR.

s3144 A 70-year-old patient has attacks of dizziness and fainting. On the ECG the P waves are regular (70 /min), while the QRS complexes are independent of them, widened, at a rate of 35 /min.
s3145 What is the condition? Under the influence of which pacemaker are the ventricles contracting? Explain from the standpoint of the gradient of automaticity.
s3146 Complete (third-degree) atrioventricular block. The sinus node excites the atria at a normal rhythm (70 /min), but the impulses do not pass through the AV node to the ventricles. The ventricles contract under the influence of lower pacemakers — the bundle of His or Purkinje fibers; according to the gradient of automaticity their rate is low (30–40 /min or less), and because excitation does not spread through the conduction system in the usual way, the QRS is widened. The slow rhythm reduces the minute volume — the brain receives too little blood and fainting occurs (Morgagni–Adams–Stokes attacks). Treatment — an artificial pacemaker (electrocardiostimulator).
s3147 An athlete at rest has a HR of 50 /min and a stroke volume of 100 ml. His non-athlete peer has a HR of 75 /min and a stroke volume of 66 ml.
s3148 Calculate the minute volume of both. Explain the difference.
s3149 Athlete: 100 ml × 50 = 5000 ml/min (5 l/min). Non-athlete: 66 ml × 75 ≈ 4950 ml/min (≈ 5 l/min). At rest the minute volume is almost the same, because the body's needs are the same. In the athlete regular training has enlarged the left ventricular cavity and strengthened the myocardium (physiological hypertrophy) — the stroke volume is large; therefore the same minute volume is achieved with fewer contractions (bradycardia; vagal tone is also high). This makes the work of the heart economical and provides a large reserve during physical load.
s3150 A student stimulated a frog heart with an electric current during the diastole phase. A premature contraction followed by a long pause appeared on the kymogram. A stimulus given during systole caused no response.
s3151 Explain the results.
s3152 During systole cardiac muscle is in the absolute refractory period (long because of the plateau phase), so the stimulus caused no response. In diastole excitability had been restored, and the stimulus caused a premature excitation and contraction — an extrasystole. After it, the next impulse from the sinus venosus coincided with the refractory period of the ventricle following the extrasystole and got no response; the ventricle waited for the next sinus impulse — a compensatory pause arose.
