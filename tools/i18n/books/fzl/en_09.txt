s1839 Calculate the color index. What anemia can be suspected? Explain its cause.
s1840 CI = 85 × 3 / 360 = 0.71 — hypochromia. The MCV is reduced (72 fl) — microcytosis. Hypochromic microcytic anemia — iron-deficiency anemia. Chronic blood loss (heavy menstruation) has depleted the iron stores; iron is needed for heme synthesis, so hemoglobin synthesis decreases and the erythrocytes are small and pale. The weakness and dizziness are due to the reduced oxygen capacity of the blood and tissue hypoxia.
s1841 In winter, family members who slept in a closed room with a burning coal stove were found in the morning with headache, nausea and weakness. One of them had cherry-red skin and mucous membranes. A pulse oximeter showed a saturation of 98%.
s1842 What kind of poisoning is this? Why is the pulse oximeter reading normal? What is the principle of first aid?
s1843 Carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning. CO binds to hemoglobin 200–300 times more strongly than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin; it does not carry oxygen and also hinders the release of oxygen to the tissues by the remaining oxyhemoglobin — severe tissue hypoxia. Because carboxyhemoglobin has a color similar to oxyhemoglobin, an ordinary pulse oximeter cannot distinguish them and shows a falsely normal saturation. First aid: move the patient to fresh air and give 100% oxygen (which speeds up the elimination of CO); in severe cases, hyperbaric oxygenation.
s1844 A mountaineer who moved from Tashkent to the Pamir mountains (3500 m) was found a month later to have erythrocytes of 5.8 × 10^12^/l, hemoglobin of 175 g/l and reticulocytes of 2.5%.
s1845 Explain the mechanism of these changes. Is this a relative or an absolute erythrocytosis?
s1846 This is an absolute (true) compensatory erythrocytosis. At high altitude the partial pressure of oxygen in the atmospheric air is low, so arterial pO~2~ decreases and tissue hypoxia develops. Hypoxia enhances erythropoietin synthesis in the kidneys; erythropoietin accelerates the proliferation of erythroid progenitors in the red bone marrow. The increase in reticulocytes reflects the activity of the bone marrow. The increase in erythrocytes and hemoglobin raises the oxygen capacity of the blood and compensates for the hypoxia.

s1881 1. General characteristics of leukocytes
s1882 **Leukocytes** (white blood cells) are nucleated, colorless cells 6–20 µm in size. They are only **transported** in the blood and perform their main function **in the tissues**: they pass through the capillary wall (**diapedesis**) and move toward the focus of inflammation.
s1883 **General properties:** amoeboid movement, diapedesis, **chemotaxis** (movement toward chemical substances), phagocytosis and secretion of biologically active substances.
s1884 **Number:** **4–9 × 10^9^/l** in adults (4–10 × 10^9^/l in some sources). Up to 10–30 × 10^9^/l in newborns.
s1885 **Leukocytosis** is an increase above 9 (10) × 10^9^/l.
s1886 **Leukopenia** is a decrease below 4 × 10^9^/l.

s1932 Shifts of the leukocyte formula
s1933 By degree of maturity, neutrophils pass through: myelocyte → metamyelocyte (juvenile) → band → segmented. Normally only band and segmented neutrophils are present in the peripheral blood.
s1934 A **shift to the left** is an increase in young neutrophils (band forms, metamyelocytes and even myelocytes). In acute bacterial infection and inflammation it shows that the bone marrow is working intensively. Nuclear shift index = (myelocytes + metamyelocytes + band forms) / segmented forms; normally 0.05–0.08.
s1935 A **shift to the right** is an increase in segmented neutrophils with 5 or more segments (hypersegmentation); seen in B~12~-deficiency anemia and radiation sickness.
s1936 The leukogram aids diagnosis: **neutrophilia** with a shift to the left — bacterial infection (pneumonia, appendicitis); **lymphocytosis** — viral infections (pertussis, mononucleosis), tuberculosis; **eosinophilia** — helminth infestation, allergic diseases (bronchial asthma); **monocytosis** — tuberculosis, infectious mononucleosis, recovery from sepsis; **agranulocytosis** — a sharp decrease in granulocytes, with a risk of severe infections.
s1937 3. Functions of the types of leukocytes
s1938 Neutrophils
s1939 The most numerous (50–70% of leukocytes) and mobile cells; they are the **first** to reach the focus of infection. They circulate in the blood for 6–8 hours and live in the tissues for 1–2 days. Functions: **phagocytosis** of bacteria (“microphages” — I.I. Mechnikov); killing bacteria with lysozyme, lactoferrin, cationic proteins, oxygen radicals and myeloperoxidase; release of pyrogens (fever). Pus consists mainly of dead neutrophils.
s1940 Their granules stain deep pink with eosin. Functions: **defense against helminths** (they damage the parasite with major basic protein and peroxidase); **limiting allergic reactions** — they release histaminase (which breaks down histamine) and arylsulfatase; phagocytosis of antigen–antibody complexes. There are fewer of them in the blood by day and more at night (the effect of cortisol).
s1941 The least numerous. Their granules contain **histamine** (dilates vessels and increases their permeability) and **heparin** (an anticoagulant). IgE receptors are located on their membrane; when an allergen binds, degranulation occurs — **immediate-type allergy** (anaphylaxis). Similar cells in the tissues are mast cells.
s1942 The largest leukocytes (15–20 µm), with a bean-shaped nucleus. After circulating in the blood for 2–3 days they pass into the tissues and become **macrophages** (“macrophages” — Mechnikov): Kupffer cells in the liver, alveolar macrophages in the lungs, osteoclasts in bone, microglia in the CNS. Functions: phagocytosis of large particles and dead cells (“scavengers”), **antigen presentation** (to T lymphocytes) and secretion of cytokines (IL-1, TNF-α).

s1959 4. Leukopoiesis and its regulation
s1960 Granulocytes and monocytes are formed in the **red bone marrow**, and lymphocytes in the bone marrow and lymphoid organs (thymus, lymph nodes, spleen, Peyer's patches).

s1963 Leukopoiesis is regulated by **colony-stimulating factors (CSF)**: **G-CSF** (granulocytes), **M-CSF** (monocytes), **GM-CSF**; and also by **interleukins** (IL-3 — general, IL-5 — eosinophils, IL-7 — lymphocytes). s1980 Experiment 1: counting leukocytes in a Goryaev chamber
s1981 Put 0.4 ml of 3–5% acetic acid solution (tinted with methylene blue) into a test tube — it hemolyzes the erythrocytes and stains the leukocyte nuclei.
s1982 Take 0.02 ml of blood from a finger, add it to the tube (dilution 1 : 20) and mix.
s1983 Fill the Goryaev chamber and wait 1 minute.
s1984 At low magnification (×8) count the leukocytes in 100 large squares (1600 small squares).
s1985 Calculation: X = A × 50 (per 1 µl), where A is the number of leukocytes in 100 large squares; or A / 20 × 10^9^/l. **Interpretation:** normal 4–9 × 10^9^/l.
s1986 Experiment 2: preparing a blood smear and determining the leukocyte formula
s1987 Place a small drop of blood near the edge of a clean, degreased glass slide.
s1988 Touch the drop with a ground-edge spreader slide at an angle of 45°; once the blood spreads along its edge, draw the smear with one even movement (the smear should be thin, with a “broom-shaped” end).
s1989 Air-dry the smear and fix it in methyl alcohol for 3–5 minutes.
s1990 Stain with Romanowsky–Giemsa stain for 20–30 minutes, rinse with water and dry.
s1991 Using the immersion objective (×90), move along the edges of the smear in a “meander” pattern and count 100 (or 200) leukocytes, classifying them by type.
s1992 Calculate the percentage of each type and its absolute count: absolute count = total leukocyte count × percentage / 100. **Interpretation:** compare the result with the normal leukogram and determine whether there is a shift to the left.
