s7970 Axons of the spiral ganglion neurons (the cochlear part of nerve VIII) → **cochlear nuclei** in the medulla oblongata → **superior olivary complex** (pons; information from the two ears is compared for the first time) → lateral lemniscus → **inferior colliculi** (midbrain; auditory orienting reflexes) → **medial geniculate body** (thalamus) → **primary auditory cortex** (temporal lobe, Heschl's gyri, **areas 41–42**). The pathways partly cross, so each hemisphere receives information **from both ears** — unilateral cortical damage does not cause deafness.
s7971 **Binaural hearing and sound localization** — determining the direction of a sound source. Mechanisms: the **difference in arrival time** of the sound at the two ears (for low frequencies; differences down to 10 µs are detected) and the **difference in intensity** (for high frequencies — the head creates a “shadow”); these differences are analyzed by the superior olivary complex.
s7972 4. The vestibular apparatus
s7973 The **vestibular apparatus** is the organ that senses the position of the head in space and its movement (acceleration). It consists of the **vestibule ** (otolith organs — the **utricle** and **saccule**) and three **semicircular canals** located in mutually perpendicular planes; all are filled with endolymph.

s7987 **How the semicircular canals work:** when rotation starts, the endolymph lags behind the canal wall because of inertia and bends the cupula in the direction **opposite** to the rotation; during uniform rotation the endolymph moves together with the canal — the cupula returns to its initial position; on a sudden stop the endolymph continues to move and bends the cupula in the **opposite** direction — **post-rotational sensations** (a feeling of rotating the other way) and **nystagmus** appear.

s8001 Exercise 1: determining hearing acuity with speech
s8002 The subject stands sideways to the examiner, closes the ear not being tested with a finger and closes the eyes (so as not to lip-read).
s8003 After expiration, using the residual air, the examiner **whispers** two-digit numbers (for example, 21, 47); starting from 6 m, he comes closer if the subject cannot repeat them.
s8004 Write down the greatest distance at which the subject repeated correctly; the other ear is also tested. **Interpretation:** normally whispered speech is heard from **6 m** or more, and ordinary conversational speech up to 20 m.
s8005 Exercise 2: the Rinne test
s8006 Place the stem of a vibrating tuning fork (128 or 512 Hz) on the mastoid process (bone conduction).
s8007 As soon as the subject stops hearing the sound, bring the tuning fork close to the ear canal (1–2 cm away) (air conduction).
s8008 Ask whether the sound is heard again. **Interpretation:** normally air conduction lasts longer than bone conduction (≈ 2 times) — **Rinne  positive**. When the **sound-conducting** apparatus (outer and middle ear) is damaged, bone conduction is longer — **Rinne negative**. When the **sound-perceiving** apparatus (inner ear, nerve) is damaged, both are shortened but the ratio is preserved — Rinne positive.
s8009 Exercise 3: the Weber test (lateralization)
s8010 Place a vibrating tuning fork on the top of the head (in the midline).
s8011 Ask in which ear the sound is heard louder.
s8012 Close one ear with a finger and repeat the test. **Result and analysis:** normally the sound is heard equally in both ears (in the middle of the head). When the ear is closed or **conduction** is impaired (otitis, earwax plug), the sound lateralizes **toward the affected (closed)  ear** — external noise does not act there and the sound coming through the bone is heard better. When **perception** is impaired, the sound lateralizes **toward the healthy ear**.
s8013 Exercise 4: testing the excitability of the vestibular apparatus
s8014 **Bárány rotation test** — as in topic 24: after 10 rotations in 20 s, the duration of nystagmus (normal 25–35 s), autonomic reactions and loss of balance are evaluated.
s8015 **Yashchenko (Voyachek) test** — after rotation, standing with the head bent forward and then walking along a straight line with the eyes open; the degree of deviation is evaluated.
s8016 Stability **in the Romberg position** is evaluated. **Interpretation:** prolonged nystagmus, severe nausea, pallor and sweating — high vestibular excitability (predisposition to motion sickness); important in occupational selection (pilots, cosmonauts, sailors).
s8017 Skill: determining the type of hearing loss from tuning fork tests
s8018 Perform the Rinne test on both ears.
s8019 Perform the Weber test.
s8020 Rinne negative + Weber toward the affected ear — **conductive** hearing loss (outer or middle ear).
s8021 Rinne positive (both shortened) + Weber toward the healthy ear — **sensorineural** hearing loss (inner ear or auditory nerve).
s8022 State that the result needs to be confirmed by audiometry.
s8023 Skill: reading an audiogram
s8024 Note that the horizontal axis shows frequency (125–8000 Hz) and the vertical axis the hearing threshold (dB, increasing from top to bottom).
s8025 Find the air conduction (o — right, x — left) and bone conduction lines.
s8026 Thresholds of 0–20 dB — normal; 20–40 — mild, 40–70 — moderate, 70–90 — severe, > 90 dB — profound hearing loss.
s8027 If there is a gap between the air and bone lines — conductive; if both are lowered together — a sensorineural disorder.

s8118 After a cold a child's right ear began to hurt, his hearing decreased and his temperature rose. The Rinne test is negative on the right and positive on the left; in the Weber test the sound is heard louder in the right ear.

s8120 Acute right-sided otitis media — conductive hearing loss. In children the Eustachian tube is short and wide, so infection easily passes from the nasopharynx to the middle ear; fluid accumulates in the middle ear and restricts the movement of the tympanic membrane and ossicles — air conduction of sound is impaired. s8121 A patient treated with gentamicin for a long time developed ringing in the ears, bilateral hearing loss and unsteadiness when walking. Rinne is positive on both sides, and there is no Weber lateralization.
s8122 What disorder has developed? Explain its mechanism.
s8123 The ototoxic (and vestibulotoxic) effect of aminoglycosides — bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. Gentamicin accumulates in the endolymph and damages the hair cells (first the outer hair cells at the base of the cochlea, which receive high frequencies) and the hair cells of the vestibular apparatus. Because the sound-conducting apparatus is healthy, Rinne is positive (air and bone conduction are shortened equally); because the damage is symmetrical, there is no lateralization in the Weber test. Damage to the vestibular hair cells leads to loss of balance. That is why doses and blood concentrations are monitored during treatment with aminoglycosides.
s8124 A passenger traveling by ship for the first time developed nausea, vomiting, pallor and cold sweat while sitting in the cabin. When he went out on deck and looked at the horizon, he felt better.
s8125 What is the condition? Explain its mechanism and why looking at the horizon helps.
s8126 Motion sickness (seasickness). When the ship rocks, the vestibular apparatus (otolith organs and semicircular canals) continuously registers changing accelerations, while in the cabin the eye sees motionless surroundings — a mismatch arises between vestibular, visual and proprioceptive information. Through the connections of the vestibular nuclei with the autonomic centers (the vomiting center, vagal nuclei), vestibulo-autonomic reactions develop: nausea, vomiting, pallor, sweating. When looking at the horizon, visual information matches vestibular information (the eye also sees the rocking) — the mismatch decreases and the symptoms ease.
