s2726 **Hemotransfusion shock** — when incompatible blood is transfused, acute intravascular hemolysis develops: pain in the lower back, chills, a fall in blood pressure, hemoglobinuria (“black urine”), acute kidney failure and DIC syndrome. s2847 An Rh^-^ woman is in her third pregnancy. Her first child was born healthy; the second pregnancy ended in abortion, and no prophylaxis was given. Her husband is Rh^+^. At 30 weeks of pregnancy a high titer of anti-D antibodies was found in her blood.
s2848 What condition is developing? Explain its mechanism and the danger to the fetus. What prophylaxis should have been given?
s2849 Rh conflict (Rh sensitization). During the first birth and the abortion, Rh^+^ fetal erythrocytes entered the mother's blood; the mother has no natural anti-D antibodies, but after sensitization IgG-class anti-D antibodies were formed. IgG can cross the placenta and hemolyzes the Rh^+^ fetal erythrocytes — hemolytic disease of the newborn: anemia, jaundice, toxic effect of bilirubin on the brain, and in severe cases hydrops fetalis and fetal death. Prophylaxis: anti-D immunoglobulin should have been given within 72 hours after each birth (of an Rh^+^ child), abortion or miscarriage, and at 28 weeks of pregnancy.
s2850 After an accident, a patient who has lost a lot of blood needs emergency surgery. The patient's blood group is A (II) Rh^+^. The blood bank has run out of group A (II) blood, but 0 (I) Rh^-^ packed red cells are available.
s2851 Can group 0 (I) packed red cells be transfused in this situation? Under what conditions? Why are packed red cells chosen rather than whole blood?
s2852 In an emergency, when group-specific blood is unavailable, up to 500 ml of 0 (I) Rh^-^ packed red cells may be transfused. Group 0 (I) erythrocytes have no A or B agglutinogens, so the recipient's β agglutinins do not agglutinate them. Packed red cells rather than whole blood are chosen because 0 (I) plasma contains high-titer α and β agglutinins — in large volumes they can hemolyze the recipient's A erythrocytes. In any case, individual compatibility and biological tests are performed; group-specific blood is used as soon as possible.
s2853 A student determined a blood group with standard sera: agglutination appeared in all three sera — 0 (I), A (II), B (III).

s2855 If agglutination occurs in all three sera, the erythrocytes carry A and B agglutinogens — group AB (IV). Saline is also added to the agglutinated drops, and the result is confirmed with tsoliclones.

s2897 1. The hemodynamic function of the heart
s2898 The heart is the central organ of the circulatory system, a rhythmically working **pump**. By ejecting blood into the vessels it creates a pressure gradient in them and ensures the continuous movement of blood through the systemic and pulmonary circulations. The heart consists of two pumps: the **left heart** (systemic circulation, high pressure) and the **right heart** (pulmonary circulation, low pressure). Both ventricles contract simultaneously and eject the same volume of blood.
s2899 The heart **valves** ensure one-way movement of blood: the **atrioventricular (cuspid) valves** (bicuspid — mitral — on the left, tricuspid on the right) between the atria and ventricles; the **semilunar valves** (aortic and pulmonary) between the ventricles and arteries. The valves open and close passively under the action of the pressure difference.
s2900 2. Physiological properties of cardiac muscle
s2901 Cardiac muscle consists of two kinds of cells: **working (typical) cardiomyocytes**, which provide contraction, and **atypical cells of the conduction system**, which generate and conduct impulses.
s2902 Automaticity
s2903 **Automaticity** is the ability of the heart to be rhythmically excited and to contract under the influence of impulses generated within the heart itself. Automaticity is **myogenic** in nature: an isolated heart works for hours in a nutrient solution (the Langendorff method). The source of automaticity is the **conduction system**.

s2906 **Gradient of automaticity** (W. Gaskell): the level of automaticity is high in the upper parts of the conduction system and decreases further down. Normally the rhythm is set by the fastest impulse generator, the **sinoatrial node** (**sinus rhythm**); the other centers are subordinate to its rhythm (latent pacemakers).
s2907 **Action potential in pacemaker cells:** the resting potential is not stable (maximum diastolic potential −60 mV). In phase 4 **slow diastolic depolarization** occurs, due to the “funny” **I~f~ current ** (Na^+^ entry), T-type Ca^2+^ channels and a decrease in K^+^ efflux. On reaching threshold (−40 mV), depolarization proceeds slowly through **L-type Ca^2+^ channels**; then repolarization occurs with K^+^ efflux.

s2918 An **extrasystole** is a premature contraction. If an extra stimulus arrives in **diastole** (after relative refractoriness has ended), the heart contracts prematurely. After a ventricular extrasystole a **compensatory pause** is observed: the next impulse from the sinus node falls into the refractory period of the ventricle and gets no response, and the ventricle waits for the following impulse.
s2919 Excitation spreads in the heart through **gap junctions between cardiomyocytes** (nexuses, intercalated discs) — the heart works as a **functional syncytium** and obeys the “all or nothing” law. Conduction velocity: in the atria ≈ 1 m/s, in the AV node 0.02–0.05 m/s, in the bundle of His and
s2920 Purkinje fibers 2–4 m/s, in the ventricular myocardium 0.3–1 m/s. The **AV delay** (≈ 0.1 s) ensures that the atria finish contracting and the ventricles fill with blood.
s2921 For contraction of cardiac muscle part of the Ca^2+^ enters **from outside the cell** (in the plateau phase) and triggers the release of additional Ca^2+^ from the SR (**calcium-induced calcium release**). Therefore the force of contraction depends on extracellular Ca^2+^. The force of contraction is regulated by the **Frank–Starling law ** (the more the myocardium is stretched, the stronger it contracts) and by neurohumoral factors (topic 12).
