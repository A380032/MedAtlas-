s5560 Advantages of membrane digestion: 1) **high speed** — the enzymes are arranged in order over a large surface and the substrate is close to them; 2) **coupling of digestion and absorption** — the monomers formed are passed directly to the transport systems; 3) **sterility** — microorganisms cannot use the monomers.
s5561 When the spinal cord is damaged above the sacral segments, the defecation reflex is preserved but voluntary control is lost (reflex automatic emptying). When the sacral segments themselves are damaged, the reflex is lost — fecal retention and incontinence are observed.

s5576 2. Intestinal juice
s5577 Intestinal juice is produced by the **Brunner glands** (in the duodenum — rich in mucus and bicarbonate) and the **crypts of Lieberkühn** (throughout the intestine). **1.5–2.0 l** is secreted per day; pH **7.2–8.6**.
s5578 The juice consists of two parts: a **liquid part** (water, electrolytes, bicarbonates, mucus) and a **dense part** — shed enterocytes (they disintegrate and release their enzymes into the intestinal lumen). The intestinal epithelium is completely renewed in 3–5 days.
s5579 **Enzymes of intestinal juice** (more than 20): **enterokinase** (enteropeptidase — activates trypsinogen), peptidases (aminopeptidases, dipeptidases), disaccharidases (**maltase, saccharase (sucrase), lactase**, isomaltase), intestinal lipase, phospholipases, alkaline phosphatase, nucleases.
s5580 **Regulation of secretion** is mainly **local**: mechanical and chemical stimulation of the mucosa by chyme (enteric nervous system reflexes); humoral factors — VIP, gastrin, secretin, CCK and motilin enhance it; somatostatin inhibits it.
s5581 3. Cavity and membrane digestion (A.M. Ugolev's doctrine)
s5582 A.M. Ugolev (1958) identified two main types of digestion in the small intestine:
s5583 **Cavity (distant) digestion** — in the intestinal lumen, mainly under the action of pancreatic enzymes; large molecules (polymers) undergo initial breakdown (oligomers are formed).
s5584 **Membrane (parietal) digestion** — on the surface of the microvilli, under the action of enzymes located in the glycocalyx and membrane; the intermediate products are broken down to the final stage (monomers). Membrane digestion is **the final stage of digestion** and is directly coupled with **absorption**.

s5609 **Lactase deficiency** is the most common disorder of membrane digestion (widespread in adults). s5610 4. Absorption
s5611 **Absorption** is the passage of substances from the lumen of the digestive system into the blood and lymph. The main site is the **small intestine** (most substances in its proximal part, B~12~ and bile acids in the ileum). Little is absorbed in the oral cavity (nitroglycerin); water, alcohol and some drugs are absorbed in the stomach, and water and electrolytes in the large intestine.
s5612 **Mechanisms of absorption:** simple and facilitated diffusion, osmosis, filtration, primary and **secondary active transport** (driven by the Na^+^ gradient), endocytosis (pinocytosis — antibodies from breast milk in infants).

s5630 Absorption of water, electrolytes, vitamins and iron
s5631 **Water** — 8–9 l of fluid enters the intestine per day (2 l with food + 7 l of digestive juices); ≈ 7–8 l is absorbed in the small intestine and 1–1.5 l in the large intestine; 100–150 ml leaves with the feces. s5632 **Na^+^** — actively (Na^+^/K^+^-ATPase); together with glucose and amino acids; aldosterone enhances it in the large intestine.
s5633 **Ca^2+^** — absorbed actively in the duodenum under the action of calcitriol (vitamin D).
s5634 **Iron** — in the duodenum; it is well absorbed as Fe^2+^ (ascorbic acid and HCl reduce Fe^3+^

s5636 **Oral rehydration** (in dehydration, for example in cholera) is based on the co-transport of glucose and sodium (SGLT1): when a glucose-salt solution is drunk, Na^+^ is absorbed together with glucose and water follows — even in severe secretory diarrhea. This discovery has saved the lives of millions of children.
s5637 5. Digestion in the large intestine
s5638 0.5–1.5 l of chyme enters the large intestine per day. Its main functions are **absorption of water and electrolytes** (formation of feces), microbial digestion, and storage and elimination of feces.
s5639 **Juice of the large intestine** — small in amount (50–60 ml per day), rich in mucus, alkaline (pH 8.5–9.0), with few enzymes. The mucus protects the intestinal wall and binds the fecal masses together.

s5656 Motility of the large intestine and defecation
s5657 Movements in the large intestine are slow: **pendular**, **haustral** contractions (mixing, aiding water absorption), **peristaltic**, and 3–4 times a day, often after meals (**gastrocolic **
s5658 **reflex**) — **strong propulsive contractions (mass movements)** that push the contents into the rectum. Food residues remain in the large intestine for 12–24 hours.
s5659 **Defecation** is a complex reflex act. When feces enter the rectum and stretch its wall (pressure 40–50 cm H₂O), the mechanoreceptors are excited. **The center of the defecation reflex** is in the **sacral** (S~2~–S~4~) segments of the spinal cord; through the pelvic (parasympathetic) nerves it ensures contraction of the rectum and relaxation of the **internal anal sphincter** (smooth muscle, involuntary). The **external anal sphincter** is striated muscle (pudendal nerve) and is controlled **voluntarily**: the cerebral cortex can delay or enhance defecation (this control develops by the age of two). Defecation is aided by contraction of the abdominal muscles and descent of the diaphragm (straining).
s5660 Exercise 1: Ugolev's experiment — proving membrane digestion
s5661 **Aim:** to show the efficiency of digestion associated with the intestinal mucosa (membrane digestion) compared with cavity digestion.
s5662 Take the small intestine of a freshly killed rat (or another animal), wash it with Ringer's solution and divide it into three pieces of equal length.
s5663 **Sample 1** (membrane + cavity digestion): a piece of intestine is cut lengthwise and placed with the mucosa intact in a substrate solution (starch or maltose, 37 °C).
s5664 **Sample 2** (cavity digestion only): a piece of intestine is shaken several times in the same volume of solution and the “washings” are collected (non-adsorbed enzymes) — the substrate is added to these washings.
s5665 **Sample 3** (control): substrate and solution only.
s5666 Incubate all tubes at 37 °C for 20–30 minutes.
s5667 Determine the amount of glucose formed by the glucose oxidase method (or test strips, the Trommer test) and compare the results. **Expected result and analysis:** the most glucose is formed in the sample with the mucosa (1); considerably less in the washings (2); and none in the control (3). Therefore the main part of the enzymes is not free in the intestinal lumen but **bound to the membrane and glycocalyx of the  enterocytes**, and the final stage of digestion proceeds efficiently precisely there. This experiment proves Ugolev's doctrine of membrane digestion.
s5668 Exercise 2: effect of intestinal juice on disaccharides
s5669 Pour 2 ml of 1% maltose, sucrose and lactose solution into three test tubes respectively.
s5670 Add intestinal mucosa homogenate (or intestinal juice) to each.
s5671 Keep at 37 °C for 20 minutes and determine glucose. **Result and analysis:** glucose is formed in all tubes — intestinal juice contains maltase, sucrase and lactase, which break down disaccharides to monosaccharides. Lactase activity is often lower in adults.
