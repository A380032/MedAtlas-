s3189 The Frank–Starling law **equalizes the work of the two ventricles**: if the right ventricle ejects more blood, more blood comes from the lungs to the left ventricle, and it too contracts more strongly. As a result the blood volumes in the systemic and pulmonary circulations remain balanced. **“The more the heart receives, the more it ejects.”**
s3190 Five effects — **“CIDBT”**: **C**hronotropic (rate), **I**notropic (force), **D**romotropic (conduction), **B**athmotropic (excitability), **T**onotropic (tone). Sympathetic — all “+”, vagus — all “−”.
s3191 **Potassium** stops the heart in **diastole**, and **calcium** in **systole**. That is why cardioplegic solutions (used to stop the heart temporarily during heart surgery) are high in potassium.
s3192 1. Levels of regulation of cardiac activity
s3193 Cardiac activity constantly changes according to the body's needs: if the minute volume is 5 l at rest, during heavy physical work it reaches 25–30 l/min. This is achieved by changing the **heart rate** and the **stroke volume**.

s3208 2. Intracardiac regulatory mechanisms
s3209 Heterometric regulation (Frank–Starling law)
s3210 **Frank–Starling law:** the more the myocardium is stretched in diastole (the more blood enters the ventricle), the more strongly it contracts in the next systole. Mechanism — the sarcomere approaches its optimal length (2.2 µm), the area of interaction between actin and myosin filaments increases, and the Ca^2+^ sensitivity of troponin C rises.

s3215 **Anrep phenomenon** — when the pressure (resistance) in the aorta rises, the force of ventricular contraction increases gradually and the stroke volume is maintained.
s3216 **Bowditch “staircase”** (rate dependence) — when the contraction frequency increases, the force of each contraction increases step by step (accumulation of Ca^2+^ inside the cell).
s3217 Peripheral intracardiac reflexes
s3218 Inside the heart there is an **intracardiac (metasympathetic) nervous system** consisting of afferent, intercalary and efferent neurons; it carries out reflexes even without the central nervous system. For example, when the right atrium is stretched, contraction of the left ventricle increases (when the atrium is underfilled — the reverse). These reflexes are preserved even after heart transplantation.

s3221 3. Nervous regulation of the heart
s3222 The heart has **dual autonomic innervation**.
s3223 Parasympathetic (vagus nerve) influence
s3224 The preganglionic neurons are located in the **medulla oblongata** (the dorsal nucleus of the vagus and the nucleus ambiguus); the postganglionic neurons are inside the heart. The mediator is **acetylcholine**, the receptors are **M~2~-cholinergic receptors**. The right vagus acts mainly on the sinus node, the left on the AV node.
s3225 **Experiment of I. and E. Cyon (1866)**: stimulation of the vagus nerve in the frog slows and stops the heart. With prolonged stimulation the heart begins to contract again — **“escape” from the vagal effect** (lower pacemakers become active).
s3226 **Mechanism:** acetylcholine activates a G protein via M~2~ receptors and **opens K^+^ channels (I~K-ACh~)** — the membrane hyperpolarizes; it inhibits adenylate cyclase and lowers cAMP — the I~f~ and Ca^2+^ currents decrease; slow diastolic depolarization slows down.
s3227 Sympathetic influence
s3228 The preganglionic neurons are in the lateral horns of the upper thoracic segments of the spinal cord (Th~1~–Th~5~); the postganglionic neurons are in the cervical and upper thoracic sympathetic ganglia (stellate ganglion). The mediator is **noradrenaline**, the receptors are **β~1~-adrenergic receptors**. Sympathetic nerves go to all parts of the heart, including the ventricles.
s3229 **Mechanism:** β~1~ receptors → G~s~ protein → adenylate cyclase → **cAMP** ↑ → protein kinase A → L-type Ca^2+^ channels and I~f~ channels are phosphorylated, and the return of Ca^2+^ to the SR is accelerated (phospholamban).

s3250 Tone of the cardiac centers
s3251 At rest the vagal centers have constant (tonic) activity — **vagal tone**. Therefore the intrinsic rate of the sinus node (≈ 100 /min — its “own rhythm”) is normally slowed to 60–80 /min. When the vagus is cut or atropine (an M-cholinergic blocker) is given, the heart rate increases. Athletes have high vagal tone — bradycardia at rest. In newborns vagal tone is low — HR 120–140 /min.
s3252 Respiratory sinus arrhythmia
s3253 HR increases on inspiration and decreases on expiration. The reason is that during inspiration the lung stretch receptors and the activity of the respiratory center reduce the tone of the vagal center. This is a **physiological** condition and is well expressed in young people.

s3283 An attack of **paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia** can sometimes be stopped by **vagal maneuvers**: the Valsalva maneuver (straining against a closed glottis), immersing the face in cold water, carotid sinus massage. s3284 Influence of the cerebral cortex
s3285 Cardiac activity changes under the influence of emotions (fear, anger, excitement) and conditioned reflexes (an increase in HR in an athlete before the start). The hypothalamus and limbic system control the sympathetic and parasympathetic centers.

s3309 Exercise 1: effect of vagus nerve stimulation on the frog heart
s3310 Immobilize the frog, expose the heart and isolate the vagosympathetic trunk in the neck.
s3311 Record the heart contractions with a kymograph.
s3312 Stimulate the vagosympathetic trunk with a rhythmic electric current (weak and strong) for 10–20 s.
s3313 Continue the stimulation and observe the “escape from the vagal effect”. **Result and analysis:** with weak stimulation the heart rate slows and the force of contraction decreases (negative chrono- and inotropic effects); with strong stimulation the heart stops in diastole. With prolonged stimulation the heart begins to contract again (escape). After stimulation is stopped, the rhythm is restored.
s3314 Exercise 2: the Goltz reflex (in the frog)
s3315 Open the frog's abdominal cavity and count the heart contractions.
s3316 Tap the abdominal organs (stomach, intestine) lightly several times with the handle of forceps.
s3317 Record the change in HR. **Result and analysis:** the heart slows or stops temporarily. Afferent impulses from the abdominal organs (celiac plexus) go to the medulla oblongata and excite the vagal center. After the vagus nerve is cut or atropine is given, the reflex disappears.
s3318 Exercise 3: the Dagnini–Aschner oculocardiac reflex in humans
s3319 The subject rests lying down for 5 minutes; the pulse is counted for 15 s and converted to 1 minute.
s3320 With the eyes closed, light pressure is applied to the eyeballs from both sides for 20–30 s (the pressure should not cause pain).
s3321 The pulse is counted again during the pressure (the last 15 s). **Interpretation:** a decrease in pulse of 4–12 /min — normal; more than 12 — high parasympathetic tone (vagotonia); no decrease or an increase — predominance of sympathetic tone (sympathicotonia).
s3322 Exercise 4: the orthostatic test
s3323 The subject's pulse is counted while lying for 5 minutes.
s3324 The subject stands up quickly; the pulse is counted after 1 minute.
s3325 **Interpretation:** an increase in pulse of 10–16 /min — normal (in the upright position venous return decreases, impulses from the baroreceptors decrease and the sympathetic influence increases). An increase of more than 20 /min — high sympathetic tone or low orthostatic stability.

s3427 After heart transplantation, a patient's resting HR is 95–100 /min. During physical load the HR increases slowly, but the stroke volume increases well.
s3428 Why is the HR high at rest? By what mechanisms does the heart increase its work during exercise?
s3429 The transplanted heart is denervated — the vagus and sympathetic nerves do not reach it. Normally at rest vagal tone acts on the heart and slows the intrinsic rhythm of the sinus node (≈ 100 /min) to 60–80 /min; after denervation this influence is absent, so the HR is close to the intrinsic rhythm. During exercise the increase in cardiac work is provided by intracardiac mechanisms (the Frank–Starling law: venous return increases, the ventricles are stretched more and contract more strongly; intracardiac reflexes) and by humoral factors (adrenaline released from the adrenal glands, which acts more slowly).
