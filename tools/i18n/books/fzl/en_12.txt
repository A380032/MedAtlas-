s2487 6. Regulation of hemostasis
s2488 The **sympathetic nervous system** and **adrenaline** (stress, pain, fear) accelerate blood coagulation (**stress enhancement of hemocoagulation**) — an evolutionary defense that reduces blood loss from wounds. At the same time fibrinolysis is also enhanced.
s2489 The **parasympathetic nervous system** slightly slows coagulation.
s2490 **Cortisol and vasopressin** accelerate coagulation; at the end of pregnancy the clotting factors increase (preparation for blood loss in childbirth).
s2491 The cerebral cortex influences the rate of coagulation through conditioned reflexes.

s2616 After a fall, a 6-year-old boy has a swollen, painful knee joint (bleeding into the joint). Earlier he had prolonged bleeding when his teeth erupted. His mother's brother has the same disease. Platelets 250 × 10^9^/l, bleeding time 3 minutes, prothrombin time 13 s, APTT 78 s.
s2617 What disease can be suspected? Explain the laboratory results. How is the disease inherited?
s2618 Hemophilia (probably A — factor VIII deficiency). The platelet count and bleeding time are normal — vascular-platelet hemostasis is not disturbed. The prothrombin time is normal — the extrinsic pathway is preserved. The APTT is sharply prolonged — deficiency of intrinsic pathway factors (VIII or IX). Bleeding into joints and muscles (hematoma type) is typical of a coagulation hemostasis disorder. The disease is inherited in an X-linked recessive manner: the mother is a carrier and boys are affected (the mother's brother is also ill). The exact type is determined by measuring factor VIII and IX activity.
s2619 A patient with a mechanical heart valve prosthesis takes warfarin. After starting an antibiotic his gums began to bleed and the INR rose to 5.8.
s2620 Explain the mechanism of action of warfarin. Why did the INR rise, and what help is given?
s2621 Warfarin is a vitamin K antagonist: in the liver it stops the γ-carboxylation of the vitamin K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X); they cannot bind Ca^2+^ and become inactive — blood coagulation slows down. The intestinal microflora synthesizes part of vitamin K~2~; because the antibiotic suppressed the microflora (and affected warfarin metabolism), vitamin K decreased and the effect of warfarin increased — the INR exceeded the therapeutic range (2.5–3.5) and bleeding appeared. Help: stop or reduce the warfarin dose, give vitamin K, and in severe bleeding — fresh frozen plasma or prothrombin complex concentrate.
s2622 After a long bus journey a 45-year-old patient's left calf became swollen and painful. Then sudden shortness of breath and chest pain appeared. Blood D-dimer 3.2 µg/ml.
s2623 What complication can be suspected? Explain the mechanism of the rise in D-dimer. What are the principles of treatment?
s2624 Deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. During prolonged immobile sitting the blood flow in the leg veins slows (part of Virchow's triad) and a thrombus forms; a fragment of it breaks off and lodges in the pulmonary artery. The body's fibrinolytic system tries to break down the fibrin of the formed thrombus: plasmin splits covalently cross-linked fibrin, forming D-dimer — its rise shows that fibrin is being formed and broken down in the body. Treatment: anticoagulants (heparin, then oral anticoagulants) — to stop new thrombus formation; in massive embolism, thrombolytics (t-PA) — to dissolve the thrombus.

s2659 The ABO rule: **“Whatever is on the erythrocyte, its enemy is absent from the plasma”**. Group A — A antigen and anti-B; group B — B antigen and anti-A; group 0 — no antigen, both antibodies; group AB — both antigens, no antibodies.
s2660 Today **only group-specific blood (ABO- and Rh-compatible) is transfused**. The concept of the “universal donor” is outdated: group 0 (I) blood contains high-titer α and β agglutinins and, when transfused in large amounts, can hemolyze the recipient's erythrocytes. Only in emergencies, when group-specific blood is unavailable, may up to 500 ml of 0 (I) Rh^-^ **packed red cells** (without plasma) be transfused. In addition, whole blood is rarely transfused: the patient receives only the needed **component** (packed red cells, platelet concentrate, fresh frozen plasma).
s2661 **Tsoliclones** (monoclonal antibodies) are the main reagents in current practice: compared with standard sera they are more accurate, have a high titer and do not depend on donor blood. Tsoliclones directly detect the **agglutinogen** (the antigen on the erythrocyte).
s2662 1. The doctrine of blood groups
s2663 A **blood group** is the set of hereditarily determined antigens (**agglutinogens**) on the erythrocyte membrane. Blood groups were discovered in 1901 by the Austrian scientist **K. Landsteiner** (three groups; Nobel Prize in 1930); the fourth group was described in 1907 by the Czech scientist **J. Jansky**. Today more than 400 antigens and more than 40 systems are known on erythrocytes (ABO, Rh, Kell, Duffy, Kidd, MNSs and others), but in clinical practice the most important are the **ABO** and **Rhesus (Rh)** systems.
s2664 2. The ABO system
s2665 In the ABO system erythrocytes may carry two kinds of agglutinogen — **A** and **B**; the plasma contains natural antibodies against them — **agglutinins α (anti-A)** and **β (anti-B)**. When an agglutinogen and agglutinin of the same name (A and α, B and β) meet, the erythrocytes stick together — the **agglutination** reaction occurs, followed by hemolysis. Therefore one person's blood never contains an agglutinogen and agglutinin of the same name.

s2691 3. The Rh system
s2692 The **Rh factor (Rh)** was identified in 1940 by K. Landsteiner and A. Wiener on the erythrocytes of rhesus monkeys. The Rh system has several antigens (D, C, c, E, e); the most immunogenic is the **D antigen**. People who carry it are **Rh-positive (Rh^+^)** — ≈ 85% of Europeans; the rest are **Rh-negative (Rh^-^)** — ≈ 15% (Rh^-^ is less common among Asian peoples).
s2693 **Difference from the ABO system:** the Rh system has **no natural antibodies**. Anti-D antibodies (IgG class) in an Rh^-^ person form only after **sensitization** by Rh^+^ erythrocytes: when Rh^+^ blood is transfused or when an Rh^-^ woman is pregnant with an Rh^+^ fetus.

s2696 Rh conflict and the development of hemolytic disease of the newborn
s2697 **Hemolytic disease of the newborn** — anti-D IgG antibodies formed in an Rh^-^ mother cross the placenta and destroy the erythrocytes of the Rh^+^ fetus: anemia, jaundice (bilirubin is toxic to the brain — “kernicterus”), and in severe cases hydrops fetalis. Usually the first pregnancy passes without conflict (antibodies form during childbirth); the risk increases in subsequent pregnancies. **Prevention:** an Rh^-^ woman is given **anti-D immunoglobulin** at 28 weeks of pregnancy and within 72 hours after the birth of an Rh^+^ child — it removes the fetal erythrocytes that entered the mother's blood before the immune system “notices” them. Conflict by the ABO system is also possible (most often the mother is group 0 and the child A or B), but it is usually milder.
s2698 4. Principles of blood transfusion
s2699 Formerly **Ottenberg's rule** was applied in blood transfusion: “the donor's erythrocytes must not be agglutinated by the recipient's plasma” (the donor plasma was assumed to be diluted in the recipient's blood). Accordingly, group 0 (I) was called the “universal donor” and AB (IV) the “universal recipient”.
