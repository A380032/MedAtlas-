s8165 evaluate pain intensity with scales.
s8166 The taste threshold is lowest for **bitter** substances (quinine ≈ 0.000008 M) and higher for salty and sweet ones (table salt ≈ 0.01 M, sucrose ≈ 0.01 M). This is an evolutionary defense: most poisons taste bitter.
s8167 The **“taste map”** of older textbooks (sweet at the tip of the tongue, sour and salty at the edges, bitter at the root) is, according to modern data, a **simplified** idea: all taste qualities are sensed by the buds in all regions of the tongue, with only slight differences in sensitivity (for example, the root of the tongue is slightly more sensitive to bitter). Testing sensitivity by region in laboratory work shows this.
s8168 Smell is the only sense that reaches the cortex **bypassing the thalamus**, and it is directly connected with the **limbic system ** (amygdala, hippocampus). That is why smells evoke strong emotions and memories (the “Proust effect”).
s8169 Reduced perception of pain during stress, heavy physical exertion or injury (for example, in battle or in a sports competition) is **stress analgesia**: endorphins and the descending inhibitory system are activated. The **placebo ** effect is also partly due to the release of endogenous opioids (naloxone, an opioid antagonist, reduces it).
s8170 1. The taste system
s8171 Taste receptors
s8172 **Taste buds** are onion-shaped structures located in the papillae of the tongue (fungiform, circumvallate, foliate) and in the mucosa of the soft palate, pharynx and epiglottis. Adults have ≈ 5000–10,000 buds. Each bud consists of **50–150 taste receptor cells** (renewed every 10–14 days), supporting cells and basal cells; at its tip there is a **taste pore** into which the microvilli of the receptor cells project. Taste receptors are **secondary sensory ** receptors.
s8173 For taste to be sensed, substances must **dissolve in saliva**.

s8199 Taste pathways and centers
s8200 Impulses from the anterior 2/3 of the tongue travel via the **facial nerve** (VII, chorda tympani), from the posterior 1/3 via the **glossopharyngeal nerve** (IX), and from the pharynx and epiglottis via the **vagus nerve** (X) to the **nucleus of the solitary tract** in the medulla oblongata → the ventral posteromedial nucleus of the thalamus → the taste area of the cortex (**insula and parietal operculum**, area 43). The nucleus of the solitary tract also has connections with the hypothalamus and limbic system (emotional evaluation of taste, appetite) and with the centers of salivation and digestive reflexes.
s8201 **Features of taste: adaptation** (especially rapid to sweet and salty); **contrast** (sour is felt more strongly after sweet); **mixed taste** (taste together with smell, temperature, texture and pungency — capsaicin sensations — forms “flavor”); sensitivity decreases with age.
s8202 **Disorders of taste**: ageusia (loss), hypogeusia (reduction), dysgeusia (alteration — a metallic taste). Causes: facial nerve palsy (taste is lost in the anterior 2/3 of the tongue), dry mouth, some drugs (metronidazole, ACE inhibitors), zinc deficiency, radiation and chemotherapy, smoking, viral infections (loss of smell and taste in COVID-19).
s8203 2. The olfactory system
s8204 The **olfactory epithelium** lies in the upper part of the nasal cavity (the superior nasal concha and the upper part of the septum), over an area of ≈ 5 cm^2^. It contains ≈ 10–20 million **olfactory receptor cells** — these are **primary sensory** receptors (bipolar neurons whose axons form the olfactory nerve) and the only neurons that are renewed, every 30–60 days. The **cilia ** at the tip of their dendrite lie in the mucus.
s8205 **Transduction:** the odorant dissolves in the mucus and binds to **olfactory receptor proteins** on the cilia (≈ 350–400 functional genes in humans) → G protein (G~olf~) → adenylate cyclase → **cAMP** ↑ → cation channels open (Na^+^, Ca^2+^ enter) → Ca^2+^-dependent Cl^-^ channels open and Cl^-^ leaves → depolarization → action potentials. Each receptor cell expresses **one** type of receptor protein; but one odor excites many receptors to different degrees — odors are distinguished by a **combinatorial code** (R. Axel and L. Buck, Nobel Prize 2004). Humans can distinguish several thousand (according to some data more than a million) odors.
s8206 **Olfactory pathways:** the fibers of the olfactory nerve (I) pass through the openings of the ethmoid bone to the **olfactory bulb**; there, in the **glomeruli**, they synapse with mitral cells (all cells with the same receptor converge on one glomerulus) → **olfactory tract** → **primary olfactory cortex** (piriform cortex, amygdala, entorhinal cortex) — **bypassing the thalamus ** (the only sense to do so). Then to the orbitofrontal cortex (also via the thalamus) — conscious discrimination of odors.
s8207 **Features of smell:** very high sensitivity (mercaptans are detected at 10-11 g per 1 l of air); **rapid adaptation** (ceasing to notice a constant smell within a few minutes); sensitivity is slightly higher in women; it decreases with age. A large part of food “flavor” is actually **retronasal smell** — that is why food seems “tasteless” during a cold.
s8208 **Anosmia** (loss of smell) — in colds, polyps, head injury (the olfactory nerve fibers are torn at the ethmoid bone) and viral infections; it is also observed as an early sign **in Parkinson's and Alzheimer's diseases**. Anosmia is dangerous: the person does not smell a gas leak, smoke or spoiled food. For safety, a strong-smelling substance (mercaptan) is added to household gas.
s8209 3. The pain sensory system
s8210 **Pain** is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage (International Association for the Study of Pain, IASP). Pain is a **protective signal**: it forces the body to escape the harmful factor and encourages it to protect the damaged organ. However, chronic pain may lose its protective significance and become a disease in its own right.
s8211 **Components of pain:** sensory-discriminative (location, intensity, character), emotional-affective (unpleasantness, fear), autonomic (tachycardia, rise in pressure, sweating, pupil dilation), motor (protective reflexes) and cognitive-evaluative components.

s8219 Nociceptors and pain pathways
s8220 **Nociceptors** are **free nerve endings** (primary sensory receptors); they are present in almost all tissues (except brain tissue). Types: **mechanical**, **thermal** (> 45 °C or < 15 °C; TRPV1 — the capsaicin receptor) and the most widespread, **polymodal** (responding to mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli). Nociceptors **adapt slowly ** or not at all — pain persists as long as the stimulus lasts.

s8223 **Pain mediators (algogens)** are formed in damaged tissue and excite or sensitize nociceptors: **bradykinin**, **prostaglandins** (E~2~ — sensitizes nociceptors), **histamine**, **serotonin**, **K^+^ and H^+^ ions**, ATP, **substance P** and calcitonin gene-related peptide (released from nerve endings, causing neurogenic inflammation).
s8224 **Hyperalgesia** is increased pain sensitivity: **primary** (in the damaged area — sensitization of nociceptors by mediators) and **secondary** (in surrounding areas — **central sensitization** of spinal neurons). **Allodynia** — pain evoked by a normally painless stimulus (touch) (skin after a burn).
s8225 **Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs** (NSAIDs — ibuprofen, diclofenac) inhibit cyclooxygenase and reduce the synthesis of **prostaglandins** — the sensitization of nociceptors decreases (a peripheral effect). **Local anesthetics** block Na^+^ channels in nerve fibers and stop the conduction of impulses.
s8226 4. The antinociceptive system and pain control
s8227 The gate theory
s8228 **The “gate theory”** (R. Melzack and P. Wall, 1965): in the dorsal horns of the spinal cord there is a “gate” for the transmission of pain signals; it is controlled by **inhibitory interneurons**. When the thick tactile **Aβ fibers** are activated, the inhibitory neurons are excited and the transmission of pain signals to the brain decreases (“the gate closes”). That is why rubbing a bruised spot reduces pain. This theory is the basis of **transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS)**, massage and acupuncture.

s8231 The endogenous antinociceptive system
s8232 The **antinociceptive system** is the set of structures that limit and regulate pain sensation:
s8233 **Descending inhibitory pathways** from the **periaqueductal gray matter** (midbrain), the **raphe nuclei** (serotonin) and the **locus coeruleus ** (noradrenaline) to the dorsal horns of the spinal cord; electrical stimulation of these areas causes strong analgesia (stimulation-produced analgesia).
s8234 **Endogenous opioid peptides** — **endorphins** (β-endorphin), **enkephalins** and **dynorphins**: they bind to opioid receptors (μ, δ, κ) and inhibit the transmission of pain signals presynaptically and postsynaptically.
s8235 **Non-opioid mechanisms** — serotonin, noradrenaline, GABA, cannabinoids.
s8236 **Morphine** and other opioid analgesics bind to the μ receptors on which endogenous opioids act and produce strong analgesia; their side effects are depression of the respiratory center, pupil constriction, constipation and dependence. The antagonist in opioid poisoning is **naloxone**.
s8237 Referred and phantom pain
s8238 **Referred pain** — when an internal organ is diseased, pain is felt in the skin area innervated from the same segment (**Zakharyin–Head zones**): heart — left arm and scapula, diaphragm — shoulder, duodenum — epigastrium (topic 26). The cause is the **convergence** of visceral and somatic afferents onto the same neurons of the spinal cord.
s8239 **Phantom pain** is the sensation of pain in an amputated limb: it is associated with the damaged nerve endings and with reorganization of the somatotopic “map” in the spinal cord and cortex.
s8240 Exercise 1: determining the taste threshold
s8241 Prepare solutions of sucrose (sweet), table salt (salty), citric acid (sour) and quinine (bitter) in decreasing concentrations.
s8242 The subject rinses the mouth with water; starting with the lowest concentration, 1–2 drops are applied to the tongue with a pipette.
s8243 Write down the lowest concentration at which the subject identifies the taste — this is the **taste  threshold**.
s8244 After each solution the mouth is rinsed with water and there is a 1–2-minute break. **Interpretation:** compare the thresholds of the different substances — the threshold for bitter is the lowest.
s8245 Exercise 2: testing taste sensitivity in different regions of the tongue (gustometry)
s8246 The subject keeps the tongue protruded.
s8247 One of the taste solutions is applied with a brush to the tip, lateral edges and root of the tongue in turn.
s8248 Without drawing the tongue into the mouth, the subject indicates the taste with a hand signal or a card.
s8249 Mark the results on a diagram. **Result and analysis:** all taste qualities are perceived in most regions of the tongue, with slight differences in sensitivity; in the central back part of the tongue there are few buds — sensitivity is low.
s8250 Exercise 3: determining olfactory acuity and adaptation
s8251 Vials of different odorous substances (vinegar, mint, vanillin, coffee) are brought in turn to one nostril (the other is closed), and the subject identifies the smell with the eyes closed.
s8252 Adaptation: the subject smells one odor continuously and the time until it is no longer perceived is recorded; then another odor is presented. **Result and analysis:** adaptation to a constant odor occurs in 1–3 minutes, but another odor is perceived immediately — adaptation is **selective** (only to that odor).
s8253 Exercise 4: determining the pain threshold — algometry
s8254 **Mechanical algometry** (tensoalgometer): the tip of the instrument is placed perpendicular to the skin (for example, the inner surface of the forearm or the palm) and the pressure is increased gradually.
s8255 The subject says when the pressure turns into a **sensation of pain** — this is the **pain threshold ** (kg/cm^2^ or N).
s8256 Increasing the pressure further, the subject indicates the unbearable level — the **pain tolerance  threshold** (under the instructor's supervision, with caution).
s8257 Repeat the measurement in different areas of the body (fingertip, palm, forearm, shoulder).
s8258 Measure the threshold again after local rubbing or applying cold. **Interpretation:** the pain threshold differs in different areas of the body (depending on nociceptor density); individual differences are large. An increase in the threshold after rubbing confirms the “gate theory”. Other types of algometry: **thermal** (a heat beam) and **electrical** algometry.

s8260 **Visual analogue scale (VAS)**: the subject marks his pain on a 10 cm line (0 — no pain, 10 — the worst pain imaginable).
s8261 **Numerical rating scale (0–10)**: rating pain with a number.
s8262 In children the **faces scale** is used.
