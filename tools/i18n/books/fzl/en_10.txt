s2078 A 19-year-old man came to the hospital with pain in the right lower abdomen, nausea and a body temperature of 38 °C. Blood test: leukocytes 15.8 × 10^9^/l; band neutrophils 14%, segmented 70%, lymphocytes 12%, monocytes 4%.
s2079 Analyze the leukogram. What do these changes indicate?
s2080 Leukocytosis (15.8 × 10^9^/l), neutrophilia (84%) and an increase in band neutrophils (14%, normal 1–6%) — a shift of the leukocyte formula to the left. Lymphocytes are relatively decreased. Such a change is typical of acute bacterial inflammation (the clinical picture matches acute appendicitis). Cytokines formed in the focus of inflammation (IL-1, TNF-α) and G-CSF release neutrophils, including young forms, from the bone marrow into the blood and enhance granulopoiesis.
s2081 A student gave blood after breakfast. The leukocyte count was 10.5 × 10^9^/l. When retested on an empty stomach the next day, it was 6.2 × 10^9^/l.
s2082 What caused the first result? Why is a blood test taken on an empty stomach?
s2083 This is alimentary (postprandial) physiological leukocytosis. After food intake leukocytes accumulate in the intestinal submucosa and partly enter the circulating blood; this is redistributive in nature and passes within a few hours. To avoid misinterpreting the result (mistaking it for inflammation), the blood test is taken in the morning on an empty stomach, without physical exertion or emotional stress.
s2084 A patient seen in spring with nasal congestion, sneezing and itchy eyes had a blood test showing leukocytes of 7.0 × 10^9^/l and eosinophils of 12%.
s2085 What change has been found? Explain its mechanism and the role of eosinophils in this condition.
s2086 Eosinophilia (12%, normal 0.5–5%). The clinical picture matches allergic rhinitis (pollinosis). The allergen degranulates mast cells and basophils that carry bound IgE — histamine and other mediators are released. Mast cells release a chemotactic factor for eosinophils, and Th2 lymphocytes enhance eosinophil production through IL-5. Eosinophils release histaminase and arylsulfatase, breaking down histamine and leukotrienes, and so limit the allergic reaction. That is why the eosinophil count rises in allergy.

s2121 **Clotting time** and **bleeding time** are two different parameters. s2123 1. Structure and number of platelets
s2124 **Platelets** (thrombocytes) are anucleate, disc-shaped cell fragments **2–4 µm** in diameter. They are shed from the cytoplasm of the giant bone marrow cells — **megakaryocytes**.
s2125 **Number:** **180–320 × 10^9^/l** in adults (150–400 × 10^9^/l in international practice). Their life span is **7– 10 days**; aged platelets are destroyed in the spleen and liver. About 1/3 of platelets are stored **in the spleen**.
s2126 **Thrombocytopenia** is a decrease below 150 (180) × 10^9^/l. Below 50 × 10^9^/l bleeding after injury increases, and below 20 × 10^9^/l spontaneous hemorrhages appear (petechiae, nosebleeds).
s2127 **Thrombocytosis** is an increase above 400 × 10^9^/l: physiological (physical work, after meals, pregnancy — redistributive), reactive (after blood loss, inflammation, after splenectomy) or neoplastic (essential thrombocythemia). The risk of thrombosis increases.
s2128 A platelet has a peripheral **hyalomere** (a light part containing microtubules and the contractile protein **thrombosthenin** — actomyosin) and a central **granulomere** (granules). The membrane has an **open canalicular system**, through which the contents of the granules are released.

s2155 2. Thrombocytopoiesis and its regulation
s2156 Figure 8.1
s2157 Scheme of thrombocytopoiesis
s2158 The main regulator of thrombocytopoiesis is **thrombopoietin**; it is synthesized mainly in the **liver** at a constant rate. Thrombopoietin binds to receptors on the membranes of platelets and megakaryocytes and is “captured” and destroyed by them. Therefore, when platelets decrease, free thrombopoietin increases and thrombocytopoiesis intensifies; when they increase, it weakens (feedback). IL-3, IL-6 and IL-11 also stimulate thrombocytopoiesis.
s2159 **In liver cirrhosis** thrombopoietin synthesis decreases and the enlarged spleen (hypersplenism) “traps” many platelets — thrombocytopenia develops and a bleeding tendency appears. Thrombopoietin receptor agonists (eltrombopag, romiplostim) are used to treat immune thrombocytopenia.

s2179 4. Vascular-platelet (primary) hemostasis
s2180 **Vascular-platelet hemostasis** stops bleeding from microcirculatory vessels (diameter up to 100 µm, low pressure) within 1–3 minutes. It consists of the following stages:
s2181 **Reflex spasm** — contraction of the damaged vessel (a pain reflex, then the action of serotonin, thromboxane A~2~ and epinephrine released from platelets and endothelin from the endothelium).
s2182 **Adhesion** — subendothelial collagen is exposed; von Willebrand factor binds to collagen and links to the **GPIb** receptor of platelets.
s2183 **Activation and secretion** — platelets change shape (put out processes), release ADP, serotonin and Ca^2+^ and synthesize **thromboxane A~2~** (TxA~2~) from arachidonic acid.
s2184 **Reversible aggregation** — under the action of ADP and TxA~2~ the **GPIIb-IIIa** receptors are activated, and platelets stick to one another through fibrinogen “bridges”.
s2185 **Irreversible aggregation** — under the action of thrombin the platelet membranes break down and the platelets turn into a solid mass; a white (platelet) thrombus is formed.
s2186 **Retraction** — thrombosthenin contracts and compacts the thrombus.

s2191 **Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid)** in a low dose (75–100 mg) irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase in platelets and stops the synthesis of thromboxane A~2~. Because platelets have no nucleus, the enzyme is not resynthesized — the effect lasts for the rest of the platelet's life (7–10 days). That is why aspirin is used to prevent myocardial infarction and stroke and is stopped 5–7 days before elective surgery. **Clopidogrel** blocks the P2Y~12~ (ADP) receptors.

s2230 Experiment 1: counting platelets by the Fonio method
s2231 Wipe the skin of a finger with alcohol and place a drop of 14% magnesium sulfate solution on it (to prevent platelet aggregation).
s2232 Puncture the finger through this drop; the blood that emerges mixes with the magnesium sulfate.
s2233 Make a smear from the mixture, fix it and stain with Romanowsky–Giemsa stain.
s2234 Using the immersion objective, count 1000 erythrocytes and the platelets in the same fields of view.
s2235 Calculation: platelet count (× 10^9^/l) = erythrocyte count (× 10^12^/l) × the number of platelets per 1000 erythrocytes. **Example:** erythrocytes 4.5 × 10^12^/l, 55 platelets per 1000 erythrocytes → 4.5 × 55 = 247.5 × 10^9^/l.
s2236 Experiment 2: determining the bleeding time by the Duke method
s2237 Wipe the earlobe with alcohol and let it dry.
s2238 Puncture it with a scarifier to a depth of 3–4 mm and start a stopwatch.
s2239 Every 30 seconds blot the emerging drop of blood with filter paper (without touching the skin).
s2240 Note the time when no new spot is left on the paper — the time when bleeding stopped. **Interpretation:** normally 2–4 minutes. Prolongation may be due to thrombocytopenia, thrombocytopathy, von Willebrand disease or aspirin intake.
s2241 Experiment 3: testing capillary resistance (tourniquet test)
s2242 Put a blood pressure cuff on the upper arm and keep the pressure midway between systolic and diastolic pressure (≈ 90–100 mm Hg) for 5 minutes.
s2243 Remove the cuff and, after 5 minutes, draw a circle 2.5 cm in diameter below the elbow crease.
s2244 Count the petechiae inside the circle. **Interpretation:** up to 10 petechiae — normal; 10–20 — doubtful; more than 20 — capillary fragility (thrombocytopenia, vasculitis, vitamin C deficiency).
s2245 Experiment 4: evaluating clot retraction
s2246 Take 5 ml of venous blood into a graduated test tube and place it in a thermostat at 37 °C.
