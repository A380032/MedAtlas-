s4348 A 45-year-old woman complains of weakness, weight loss, dizziness and darkening of the skin. Arterial pressure 85/55 mm Hg, blood Na^+^ 128, K^+^ 5.8 mmol/l, glucose 3.4 mmol/l. Blood cortisol is low, ACTH high.
s4349 What is the condition? Explain each sign by linking it to the hormone deficiency.
s4350 Primary adrenal cortical insufficiency (Addison's disease). Because of aldosterone deficiency, Na^+^ and water are lost in the kidney and K^+^ is retained — hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, decreased blood volume and arterial hypotension. Because of cortisol deficiency, gluconeogenesis weakens — hypoglycemia, weakness; the effect of catecholamines on the vessels (permissive effect) decreases and the hypotension worsens. When cortisol falls, the pituitary releases a lot of ACTH by negative feedback; ACTH and MSH are formed from a common precursor (proopiomelanocortin) and stimulate melanocytes — the skin darkens.
s4351 On the second day after thyroid resection, the patient developed numbness of the lips and fingertips, followed by painful cramps of the hand muscles. The Chvostek and Trousseau signs are positive.
s4352 What complication has developed? Explain its mechanism.
s4353 During the operation the parathyroid glands were removed or damaged — hypoparathyroidism. Because of parathyroid hormone deficiency, the release of calcium from bone and calcium reabsorption in the kidney decrease, calcitriol synthesis falls and intestinal calcium absorption decreases — hypocalcemia develops. When extracellular Ca^2+^ falls, the opening threshold of Na^+^ channels decreases (membrane stability decreases) — the excitability of nerves and muscles increases and spontaneous impulses arise: paresthesias, tetany. Treatment — intravenous calcium preparations, then calcium and active vitamin D.
s4354 A patient treated with prednisolone (a glucocorticoid) for a long time has a rounded face, fat accumulation in the upper body, purple stripes on the abdomen, arterial pressure 160/100 mm Hg and blood glucose 8.2 mmol/l.
s4355 What is the condition? Explain the mechanism of the signs. Why is it dangerous to stop the treatment abruptly?
s4356 Drug-induced (iatrogenic) Itsenko–Cushing syndrome. Glucocorticoids enhance gluconeogenesis and reduce glucose uptake by tissues — hyperglycemia; protein catabolism thins the skin and collagen — striae, muscle weakness, osteoporosis; they redistribute fat — “moon face”, obesity of the upper body; through mineralocorticoid action and increased vascular sensitivity to catecholamines — hypertension. During long-term treatment, exogenous glucocorticoids suppress CRH and ACTH by negative feedback, and the patient's own adrenal glands atrophy. If treatment is stopped abruptly, acute adrenal insufficiency may develop; therefore the dose is reduced gradually.

s4402 2. Mechanism of inspiration and expiration
s4403 Because the lung has no muscle tissue of its own, it cannot expand actively; it passively follows the movements of the chest. Between the lung and the chest wall there is a sealed **pleural cavity** (a thin layer of fluid).
s4404 Inspiration — an active process
s4405 **Main inspiratory muscles: the diaphragm** (70–80% of the volume change in quiet breathing; its dome descends 1.5–2 cm, up to 10 cm in deep breathing) and the **external intercostal muscles ** (they raise the ribs and expand the chest anteroposteriorly and laterally). **Accessory muscles** (in deep breathing): sternocleidomastoid, scalene, pectoralis major and minor.
s4406 Sequence: impulse from the respiratory center → the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles contract → chest volume increases → intrapleural pressure becomes more negative → the lungs expand → alveolar pressure falls 1–2 cm H₂O below atmospheric → air enters the lungs.
s4407 Expiration
s4408 In quiet breathing it is **passive**: the respiratory muscles relax, and the **elastic recoil** of the lungs and the weight of the chest return it to the initial position; alveolar pressure exceeds atmospheric and air leaves. In forced expiration (physical work, coughing, bronchial asthma) it is **active**: the **internal intercostal muscles** and the **abdominal muscles** contract.

s4410 Donders model: A — resting state; B — when the “diaphragm” (rubber) is pulled down, the lungs expand (Alavia O.T., 2018)
s4411 Intrapleural pressure
s4412 **Intrapleural pressure** is **lower** than atmospheric (“negative”): −3…−5 cm H₂O (−2…−4 mm Hg) at the end of quiet expiration, −6…−8 during quiet inspiration, and down to −20 cm H₂O during deep inspiration.
s4413 **Origin:** the **elastic recoil** of the lungs (which tends to shrink the lung toward its root) and the outward pull of the chest wall act in opposite directions; the pleural layers cannot separate from each other and a negative pressure arises in the cavity. In the postnatal period the faster growth of the chest relative to the lungs also contributes.

s4416 **Pneumothorax** is the entry of air into the pleural cavity (chest injury, rupture of a bulla). The negative pressure disappears and the lung collapses due to its elastic recoil — it no longer takes part in breathing.
s4417 **Valvular (tension) pneumothorax** is life-threatening: with each inspiration air enters but does not leave, pressure rises and the mediastinum shifts. Emergency help — puncture and drainage of the pleural cavity.
s4418 Elastic recoil of the lungs and surfactant
s4419 The elastic recoil of the lungs is due to two factors: the **elastic fibers** of lung tissue (≈ 1/3) and the **surface tension** of the fluid layer on the inner surface of the alveoli (≈ 2/3).
s4420 **Surfactant** is a complex of phospholipids (mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) and proteins produced by type II alveolocytes. Its functions: it **reduces surface tension** and increases lung compliance (reducing the work of breathing); it **prevents the collapse of small alveoli (atelectasis)** — according to Laplace's law the pressure in a small alveolus should be greater, but surfactant is denser in it and reduces the tension more; it keeps the alveoli dry (prevents pulmonary edema); and it has a protective function.
s4421 Surfactant begins to be synthesized in the fetus at 24–28 weeks and becomes sufficient by 34–35 weeks. In premature infants surfactant deficiency leads to **respiratory distress syndrome** (hyaline membrane disease): the alveoli collapse and breathing becomes difficult. Prevention — glucocorticoids for the mother when preterm birth is threatened (they accelerate surfactant synthesis); treatment — endotracheal administration of surfactant.
s4422 **Lung compliance** is the change in lung volume per 1 cm H₂O change in pressure; normally ≈ 200 ml/cm H₂O (lungs and chest together — 100). It decreases in fibrosis and increases in emphysema.
s4423 **Airway resistance** is mainly in the medium-sized bronchi; it rises sharply when the bronchi narrow (asthma, bronchitis) (Poiseuille's law: the fourth power of the radius).

s4445 Gases pass from the alveoli into the blood and from the blood into the tissues only by **diffusion**, down the partial pressure gradient. In the lungs the gradient for O~2~ is 100 − 40 = **60 mm Hg**, and for CO~2~ 46 − 40 = **6 mm Hg**. Nevertheless CO~2~ diffuses well: its solubility is 20–25 times higher than that of O~2~.
s4446 The alveolar-capillary membrane
s4447 The air–blood barrier is only **0.3–0.5 µm** thick: the surfactant layer, the alveolar epithelium (type I alveolocytes), the common basement membrane and the capillary endothelium; plus a layer of plasma and the erythrocyte membrane. The total diffusion surface is **70–100 m^2^**. An erythrocyte passes through a capillary in ≈ 0.75 s, while gas equilibrium is reached in 0.25 s — there is a large reserve.

s4450 According to **Fick's law**, the rate of diffusion is directly proportional to the partial pressure difference, the membrane area and the solubility of the gas, and inversely proportional to membrane thickness. **The diffusing capacity of the lung ** is the volume of gas that diffuses in 1 minute at a gradient of 1 mm Hg; for O~2~ at rest it is ≈ 25 ml/min/mm Hg.
s4451 The ventilation-perfusion ratio
s4452 For effective gas exchange, alveolar ventilation (≈ 4.2 l/min) and pulmonary blood flow (≈ 5 l/min) must be matched: **V/Q ≈ 0.8–1.0**. At the lung apices V/Q is high (ventilation predominates), at the base it is low (blood flow predominates). **Hypoxic vasoconstriction** — when O~2~ falls in a poorly ventilated area, the vessels there constrict and blood is directed to well-ventilated areas.
