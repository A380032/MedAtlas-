s7134 Exercise 1: examining cerebellar function in humans
s7135 **Romberg test**: the subject stands with the feet together, the arms stretched forward and the eyes closed (30–60 s). Observe swaying or a tendency to fall. A more difficult variant — standing with the feet in one line (heel to toe).
s7136 **Finger-to-nose test**: stretch the arm out to the side, then, with the eyes closed, touch the tip of the nose with the index finger (with each hand). Evaluate accuracy and tremor.
s7137 **Heel-to-knee test**: lying down, place the heel of one leg on the knee of the other and slide it down along the shin.
s7138 **Adiadochokinesis test**: rapidly turning the palms up and down alternately (supination–pronation).
s7139 **Gait**: walking along a straight line, heel-to-toe walking. **Interpretation:** normally stability is maintained in the Romberg position and the tests are performed accurately, without tremor and smoothly. In cerebellar damage — swaying, tremor on approaching the target (intention tremor), “overshooting” (dysmetria), slow and incorrect alternation of movements.

s7244 A 45-year-old patient sways when walking, walks with the legs wide apart, and his speech is broken into syllables and slow. In the finger-to-nose test the tremor increases as the target is approached and the finger “overshoots” the nose. In the Romberg position he sways even with the eyes open.
s7245 Which structure is damaged? Explain each sign.
s7246 The cerebellum is damaged (cerebellar ataxia). The cerebellum ensures the accuracy, smoothness and coordination of movements: it compares the planned movement with information about the ongoing movement (from proprioceptors) and corrects errors. When it is damaged: dynamic ataxia — a swaying, wide-based gait; intention tremor — on approaching the target the corrective movements overshoot and oscillation appears; dysmetria — misjudging the amplitude of a movement (“overshooting”); scanning speech — incoordination of the movements of the speech muscles; static ataxia persists regardless of visual control (the difference from sensory ataxia).
s7247 For two years a 68-year-old man's movements have been slowing; he has a “pill-rolling” tremor of the hands at rest, a mask-like face, walks stooped with small steps, and uneven resistance is felt on passive flexion of his arms.
s7248 What is the disease? Explain its pathophysiological basis through the connections of the basal ganglia.
s7249 Parkinson's disease (akinetic-rigid syndrome). The dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra die and the supply of dopamine to the striatum decreases. Normally dopamine activates the direct pathway (D~1~) and inhibits the indirect pathway (D~2~), facilitating the passage of movement-initiating signals through the thalamus to the motor cortex. In dopamine deficiency the indirect (inhibitory) pathway predominates — the activation of the cortex by the thalamus decreases: initiating movements becomes difficult (akinesia, bradykinesia), automatic movements disappear (mask-like face, no arm swing), muscle tone increases (“cogwheel” rigidity) and a resting tremor appears. Treatment — levodopa, dopamine agonists.
s7250 After a left-sided stroke the patient understands what is said and follows instructions, but speaking is very difficult for him: few words, short, broken phrases; there is weakness in the right arm.
s7251 Which area of the cortex is damaged? Why is there weakness in the right arm?
s7252 Broca's area (areas 44–45) in the inferior frontal gyrus of the left hemisphere is damaged — motor (expressive) aphasia: comprehension of speech (Wernicke's area) is preserved, but the construction of the motor program of speech is impaired. Broca's area lies close to the primary motor cortex (the lower part of the precentral gyrus — the projection of the face and arm); because this too is damaged and the pyramidal tracts cross in the medulla oblongata, weakness (central paresis) has developed in the opposite (right) arm.

s7291 **The sympathetic division** — the “fight or flight” reaction: mobilization of energy, increased blood supply to the heart and skeletal muscles, inhibition of digestion. **The parasympathetic division** — “rest and digest”: saving and restoring energy, digestion, excretion.
s7292 1. General description of the autonomic nervous system
s7293 The **autonomic nervous system (ANS)** is the part of the nervous system that regulates the activity of the internal organs, blood vessels, glands, smooth muscles and heart, and metabolism. It maintains homeostasis and ensures the body's adaptation to different conditions of activity (the **adaptive-trophic** function — L.A. Orbeli). Its activity is usually not subject to consciousness (“autonomic”).

s7316 2. Divisions of the autonomic nervous system
s7317 The sympathetic division
s7318 Its centers are in the lateral horns of the **thoracic and lumbar segments (Th~1~–L~2~ (L~3~))** of the spinal cord (the **thoracolumbar division**). The preganglionic fibers are **short**, and the ganglia lie close to the center: in the **paravertebral ** (sympathetic trunk) and **prevertebral** (celiac, superior and inferior mesenteric plexus) ganglia. The postganglionic fibers are **long**. One preganglionic fiber connects with many (≈ 1 : 10–30) postganglionic neurons — a **widespread (generalized)** effect.
s7319 The parasympathetic division
s7320 Its centers are in the **brainstem** (nuclei of cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X) and in the **sacral segments (S~2~–S~4~)** of the spinal cord (the **craniosacral division**). The preganglionic fibers are **long**, and the ganglia lie next to or inside the organ (intramural); the postganglionic fibers are **short**. The effect is **local**. ≈ 75% of the parasympathetic fibers run in the **vagus nerve**.
