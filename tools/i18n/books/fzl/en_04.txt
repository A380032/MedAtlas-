s0665 A student stimulated the nerve of a nerve-muscle preparation with a current of 2 V — the muscle contracted. He then raised the voltage from zero to 4 V very slowly (over several seconds) — the muscle did not contract.
s0666 Why did the stronger current not evoke a response? Which law does this illustrate?
s0667 This is the gradient law and the phenomenon of accommodation. When the current rises slowly, the membrane depolarizes gradually; meanwhile the Na^+^ channels are inactivated, K^+^ permeability increases and the critical level of depolarization rises. As a result, even when the current exceeds the initial threshold, no regenerative Na^+^ current arises and no action potential appears. For excitation, the stimulus strength must rise quickly enough.
s0668 A physiotherapist placed a direct current electrode over the painful area of a patient. A student asked: “Which pole should be placed over the painful area to reduce pain?”
s0669 Which pole should be placed there, and why? Which law is this based on?
s0670 The anode is placed over the painful area. According to Pflüger's polar law, the membrane under the anode is hyperpolarized (anelectrotonus), its potential moves away from the critical level and excitability decreases — pain receptors and fibers are excited less. Under the cathode, by contrast, excitability increases because of depolarization (catelectrotonus), so the cathode is used to stimulate muscles and nerves.

s0709 Conduction velocity depends on the **diameter** of the fiber and its **myelination**: the thicker the fiber and the thicker its myelin, the higher the velocity. The fastest are Aα fibers (up to 120 m/s), the slowest are C fibers (0.5–2 m/s).
s0710 Vvedensky regarded parabiosis as **a peculiar localized, persistent (stationary) state of excitation**. This doctrine shows **the unity of the processes of excitation and inhibition**: inhibition can be a special form of excitation.
s0711 The neuromuscular synapse has a high **safety factor**: the EPP produced by one impulse is 3–4 times larger than needed for an action potential. Therefore, normally every motor impulse is sure to excite the muscle fiber.
s0712 Steps of the synapse — **“Arrives–Enters–Releases–Binds–Opens–Breaks down”**: the impulse **arrives**, Ca^2+^ **enters**, the neurotransmitter is **released**, it **binds** to the receptor, the channel **opens**, the neurotransmitter is **broken down**.
s0713 1. Structure of the neuron and nerve fibers
s0714 The **neuron** is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. It consists of a body (soma), short branching processes — **dendrites** (which receive information) — and one long process — the **axon** (which transmits information). The site where the axon leaves the body — the **axon hillock** (initial segment) — has the lowest threshold: this is where the action potential is generated (the trigger zone).
s0715 A **nerve fiber** is an axon surrounded by glial cells (**Schwann cells** in the periphery, **oligodendrocytes** in the CNS). In myelinated fibers the Schwann cell wraps the axon in many layers, forming a lipid-rich **myelin sheath**. The myelin is interrupted every 1–2 mm; these sites are called **nodes of Ranvier**, and voltage-gated Na^+^ channels are packed very densely in them.

s0737 2. Conduction of excitation in a nerve fiber
s0738 The inner surface of an excited region is positive, while that of the neighboring unexcited region is negative. **Local (circular) currents** arise between them, depolarize the neighboring region to the critical level and evoke a new action potential in it. In this way excitation spreads **without decrement**.
s0739 **In an unmyelinated fiber** conduction is **continuous**: each neighboring segment of the membrane is excited in turn; the velocity is low.
s0740 **In a myelinated fiber** conduction is **saltatory (jumping)**: myelin acts as an electrical insulator, so the action potential is generated only at the nodes of Ranvier and “jumps” from one
s0741 node to the next (even skipping 2–3 nodes). This increases the velocity 50-fold and saves energy (the Na^+^/K^+^ pump works only at the nodes).
s0742 Figure 3.2
s0743 Conduction of excitation: I — continuous in an unmyelinated fiber; II — saltatory in a myelinated fiber (Alaviya O.T., 2018)
s0744 **Multiple sclerosis** and **Guillain–Barré syndrome** are demyelinating diseases. When myelin is destroyed, saltatory conduction is disturbed: impulses slow down or stop. The result is paralysis, sensory disturbances and impaired vision. Electroneuromyography reveals a decrease in conduction velocity.

s0765 3. Lability of the nerve and parabiosis
s0766 N.E. Vvedensky (1901) established that when various damaging agents (KCl, procaine (novocaine), ether, cold) act on part of a nerve, its lability decreases and it responds to excitation in a peculiar way. This state
s0767 was called **parabiosis** (“beside life”). Parabiosis is a reversible process: when the damaging agent is removed, nerve function is restored.

s0800 5. Transmission of excitation in a chemical synapse
s0801 Most synapses in the human body are **chemical** synapses. In them the synaptic cleft is 20–50 nm wide; the presynaptic terminal contains **synaptic vesicles** that store the neurotransmitter, and the postsynaptic membrane carries **receptors** sensitive to it.
s0802 Figure 3.4
s0803 Stages of transmission of excitation in a chemical synapse
s0804 The amount of neurotransmitter in one vesicle is called a **quantum** (one quantum of acetylcholine ≈ 5–10 thousand molecules). Even at rest some vesicles are released spontaneously, producing small **miniature potentials**.
s0805 The terminal of a motor neuron axon forms a **neuromuscular synapse (end plate)** with a skeletal muscle fiber. The neurotransmitter is **acetylcholine (ACh)**; the postsynaptic membrane carries **N-cholinergic receptors** (ligand-gated Na^+^/K^+^ channels).
s0806 The action potential arrives at the presynaptic terminal, and Ca^2+^ channels open.
s0807 Under the action of Ca^2+^, vesicles containing ACh undergo exocytosis (100–300 quanta per impulse).
s0808 ACh binds to N-cholinergic receptors, cation channels open and Na^+^ enters.
s0809 An **end-plate potential (EPP)** arises on the postsynaptic membrane — like the local response, it is graded and summates.
s0810 The EPP depolarizes the adjacent muscle membrane to the critical level and an **action potential** arises — the muscle contracts.
s0811 ACh is broken down within 1–2 ms by the enzyme **acetylcholinesterase (AChE)** into choline and acetic acid; choline returns to the presynaptic terminal and is used to synthesize new ACh.

s0814 Properties of chemical synapses
s0815 **One-way conduction** — the neurotransmitter is released only from the presynaptic terminal, and the receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane.
s0816 **Synaptic delay** — 0.2–0.5 ms at each synapse (for release of the neurotransmitter, its diffusion and binding to the receptor).
s0817 **Low lability** — 100–150 impulses/s.
s0818 **Rapid fatigue** — depletion of the neurotransmitter store, desensitization of receptors.
s0819 **High sensitivity to chemicals** — drugs and poisons act selectively on synapses.
s0820 **Summation and facilitation** — with successive impulses, transmitter release increases because Ca^2+^ accumulates.

s0841 6. Laboratory work
s0842 Experiment 1: demonstrating isolated conduction in a nerve
s0843 Isolate the frog's sciatic nerve together with its branches to both legs (or, in one leg, observe the branches to the gastrocnemius and other muscles).
s0844 Stimulate part of the nerve trunk and observe which muscles contract.
s0845 Change the site of stimulation. **Expected results and analysis:** each time only the muscles innervated by the stimulated fibers contract; excitation does not pass to neighboring fibers. This proves the law of isolated conduction.
s0846 Experiment 2: Vvedensky's parabiosis experiment
s0847 Prepare a nerve-muscle preparation. Place a piece of cotton wool soaked in KCl or procaine solution on the middle part of the nerve (the parabiotic region).
s0848 Place the stimulating electrodes above the parabiotic region (closer to the center).
s0849 Every 1–2 minutes stimulate alternately with weak and strong stimuli and record the muscle contractions.
s0850 Note the sequence of phases.
s0851 Remove the cotton wool, wash the nerve with Ringer's solution and observe the recovery of function.
s0852 **Expected results and analysis:** first the responses to strong and weak stimuli become equal (equalizing phase), then the response to a weak stimulus becomes larger than to a strong one (paradoxical phase), and finally there is no response at all (inhibitory phase). After washing, the phases pass in reverse order and conduction is restored — parabiosis is a reversible process.
s0853 Experiment 3: relative indefatigability of the nerve and rapid fatigue of the synapse
s0854 Stimulate the nerve of a nerve-muscle preparation for a long time at a frequency of 30–50 Hz.
s0855 Wait until the muscle stops contracting (becomes fatigued).
s0856 Immediately place the electrodes directly on the muscle and stimulate it. **Expected results and analysis:** the muscle that does not contract when stimulated through the nerve contracts again when stimulated directly. Hence neither the muscle nor the nerve is fatigued — the **neuromuscular synapse** fatigues first (its neurotransmitter store is depleted). This shows that the synapse has the lowest lability.
