s0019 Remember
s0020 The main method of physiology is **experiment**. An acute experiment gives quick results, but the integrity of the organism is disrupted; a chronic experiment makes it possible to study the organism in its natural state. In humans, mainly **noninvasive** methods are used (ECG, spirography, blood pressure measurement).
s0021 **Negative feedback** returns a deviated parameter to normal (the main mechanism of homeostasis): if blood thyroxine rises, the release of thyroid-stimulating hormone decreases. **Positive feedback** amplifies a process and is rare: oxytocin release during labor, the blood coagulation cascade, the opening of Na^+^ channels in the rising phase of the action potential.
s0022 The main cause of the resting potential is the **diffusion of K^+^ ions out of the cell**. Therefore, if the K^+^ concentration in the extracellular fluid rises (hyperkalemia), the gradient decreases and the membrane **depolarizes**.
s0023 The **critical level of depolarization (CLD)** is the membrane potential at which an action potential arises (≈ −50…−55 mV in a nerve). The difference between the resting potential and the CLD is called the **threshold depolarization** (≈ 15–20 mV): the smaller it is, the higher the excitability. After an action potential the ion concentrations hardly change (very few ions cross in a single impulse); they are restored by the Na^+^/K^+^ pump.
s0024 Memory aid
s0025 Remember the order of the action potential as **“NaK”**: first **Na** enters (depolarization), then **K** leaves (repolarization). At rest, “potassium escapes, the anions stay” — that is why the inside is negative.
s0026 Caution
s0027 Local anesthetics (lidocaine, procaine (novocaine)) **block voltage-gated Na^+^ channels**: no action potential arises and the pain impulse is not conducted. Tetrodotoxin (puffer fish poison) also blocks Na^+^ channels and causes paralysis of the respiratory muscles. Tetraethylammonium, on the other hand, blocks K^+^ channels and slows repolarization.
s0028 1. Physiology as a science: its tasks and methods
s0029 **Physiology** (Greek physis — nature, logos — study) is the science that studies the vital activity (functions) of the living organism, its organs and systems, tissues and cells, the mechanisms that regulate these functions and the interaction of the organism with the external environment. **Normal physiology** studies the functions of the healthy human body and is the theoretical foundation of medicine: to understand disease (pathological physiology), one must first know the norm.

s0045 Methods of physiological research
s0046 **Observation** — the oldest method; recording a process without interfering with it.
s0047 **Acute experiment (vivisection)** — under anesthesia an organ is exposed and acted on directly; the experiment is short-term. Drawback: anesthesia and surgical trauma affect function.
s0048 **Chronic experiment** — long-term observation under natural conditions in an animal prepared in advance by surgery (a fistula, organ transplantation). I.P. Pavlov studied the physiology of digestion with this method and was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1904.
s0049 **Method of isolated organs and tissues** — the frog nerve-muscle preparation, the isolated heart (Langendorff method).
s0050 **Modern instrumental methods** — electrophysiological (ECG, EEG, EMG), the microelectrode technique, patch clamp, telemetry, tomography, molecular genetic methods, computer modeling.
s0051 A brief history of physiology
s0052 **Hippocrates** (5th–4th century BC) — the doctrine of temperaments.
s0053 **Galen** (2nd century) — vivisection experiments on animals.
s0054 **Avicenna (Abu Ali ibn Sina)** (980–1037) — in “The Canon of Medicine” he described in detail the heartbeat, the types of pulse, and questions of nutrition and hygiene.
s0055 **William Harvey** (1628) — discovered the circulation of the blood; scientific physiology dates from this year.
s0056 **René Descartes** (17th century) — founder of the concept of the reflex.
s0057 **Luigi Galvani** (1791) — discovered “animal electricity”.
s0058 **I.M. Sechenov** (1863) — “Reflexes of the Brain”; discovered central inhibition.
s0059 **I.P. Pavlov** — the physiology of digestion, conditioned reflexes and the doctrine of higher nervous activity.
s0060 **P.K. Anokhin** — the theory of functional systems.
s0061 **Claude Bernard** (19th century) — the idea of the “constancy of the internal environment”; **W. Cannon** (1929) — the term “homeostasis”.
s0062 2. Regulation of functions in the body
s0063 Homeostasis
s0064 **Homeostasis** is the relative constancy of the composition and properties of the internal environment of the body (blood, lymph, tissue fluid) and the stability of the basic physiological functions. Homeostatic parameters may be **rigid** (pH, osmotic pressure, body temperature — kept within very narrow limits) or **plastic** (blood pressure, heart rate, blood glucose — vary within wider limits).

s0101 Nervous and humoral regulation do not work separately but as a single **neurohumoral regulation**: the nervous system controls the endocrine glands, and hormones influence the activity of nerve cells.
s0102 A **reflex** is the response of the organism to stimulation of receptors, carried out with the participation of the central nervous system. A reflex is carried out through a **reflex arc**: receptor → afferent (sensory) pathway → nerve center → efferent (motor) pathway → effector (working organ). Information about the result of the response returns to the center — this is called **reverse afferentation (feedback)**; therefore modern physiology uses the concept of a **reflex ring** instead of a “reflex arc”.

s0105 Functional system (P.K. Anokhin)
s0106 A **functional system** is a dynamic association of various organs and tissues directed toward achieving a result useful for the adaptation of the organism. Its components are the **useful result** (the system-forming factor), the **receptors** that evaluate the result, **reverse afferentation**, the **central architecture** (afferent synthesis, decision making, the acceptor of the action result) and the **executive mechanisms** (autonomic, hormonal, behavioral). For example, the functional system that maintains blood pO~2~ includes the respiratory, circulatory and blood systems.
s0107 The integrity of the organism
s0108 The organism is not a sum of separate organs but a single system. Its integrity is ensured by the nervous system (especially its higher divisions), the endocrine system and the circulation of blood and lymph. A change in the function of one organ leads to changes in other systems as well (for example, during physical work breathing, heart rate and sweating change together with muscle activity).
s0109 3. Excitable tissues and their properties
s0110 Tissues that can pass from a state of rest to an active state in response to a stimulus and generate an **action potential** in doing so are called **excitable tissues**. They include **nerve**, **muscle** (skeletal, cardiac, smooth) and **glandular** tissue.

s0130 By their nature, **stimuli** are **physical** (mechanical, thermal, electrical, light, sound), **chemical** (acids, alkalis, hormones, neurotransmitters) and **physicochemical** (changes in osmotic pressure or pH). By biological significance, **adequate** stimuli (to which a receptor is specially adapted — light for the eye) and **inadequate** stimuli (“seeing sparks” after a blow to the eye) are distinguished. In experiments **electric current** is used most often: it can be dosed precisely, does not damage the tissue and is close to natural biocurrents.
s0131 4. The cell membrane and ion channels
s0132 The cell membrane, 7–10 nm thick, consists of a **phospholipid bilayer** with proteins embedded in it (the fluid mosaic model). The lipid layer is almost impermeable to ions; ions pass through **ion channels** and **carrier proteins**.

s0155 **Ion channels** are protein molecules that span the membrane; they have a **selectivity filter** (letting through only a particular ion) and a **gating mechanism**. By the way they open, channels are **voltage-gated** (open when the membrane potential changes — fast Na^+^ channels), **ligand-gated** (open when a neurotransmitter binds — the N-cholinergic receptor for acetylcholine), **mechanosensitive** (open on stretch) and **“leak” channels** (always open — the K^+^ channels at rest). The fast Na^+^ channel has two gates: the outer **activation (m)** gate and the inner **inactivation (h)** gate.

s0158 **The Na^+^/K^+^ pump (Na^+^/K^+^-ATPase)**, using the energy of one ATP molecule, moves **3 Na^+^** ions out of the cell and **2 K^+^** ions into it. As a result: 1) the ion concentration gradients are maintained; 2) the pump is **electrogenic** — one extra positive charge is moved out in each cycle, contributing to the membrane potential (−5…−10 mV); 3) cell volume is regulated. The pump accounts for 30–40% of the energy expenditure of a tissue (up to 70% in nervous tissue).

s0173 5. The resting membrane potential
s0174 The **resting potential (membrane potential)** is the potential difference between the inner and outer surfaces of the membrane of a cell at rest. The inner surface of the membrane is negatively charged relative to the outer surface. Its magnitude is −60…−70 mV in a nerve cell, −80…−90 mV in skeletal muscle and −50…−60 mV in smooth muscle.
s0175 The mechanism of the resting potential (membrane-ion theory: J. Bernstein, A. Hodgkin, A. Huxley)
s0176 **Unequal distribution** of ions inside and outside the cell (the result of the work of the Na^+^/K^+^ pump).
s0177 **Different permeability** of the resting membrane to different ions: the permeability to K^+^ is 25–100 times higher than to Na^+^ (K^+^ “leak” channels are always open).
s0178 K^+^ ions **leave the cell** down their concentration gradient, while the large organic anions cannot cross the membrane and stay inside — the inner surface of the membrane becomes negative and the outer surface positive.
s0179 The electric field that arises opposes further exit of K^+^; the point at which the concentration and electrical forces balance is called the **potassium equilibrium potential** (≈ −90 mV). It can be calculated with the **Nernst equation**.
s0180 A small leak of Na^+^ into the cell makes the potential somewhat less negative than the K^+^ equilibrium potential (−70 mV); the Na^+^/K^+^ pump adds an electrogenic contribution.
s0181 Changes in blood potassium are dangerous for the heart and muscles. In **hyperkalemia** (renal failure, extensive tissue damage) the resting potential decreases and Na^+^ channels become inactivated — cardiac conduction is disturbed and cardiac arrest is possible. In **hypokalemia** (vomiting, diuretics) the membrane becomes hyperpolarized — muscle weakness and arrhythmias appear.
