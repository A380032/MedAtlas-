s7839 A 46-year-old teacher complains that recently he has had to read text in books and on his phone with his arm outstretched, holding it farther away. He sees distant objects as well as before.
s7840 What is the condition? Explain its mechanism and name the method of correction.
s7841 Presbyopia — an age-related decline in the ability to accommodate. To see a near object the ciliary muscle must contract, the zonules of Zinn relax and the lens become convex owing to its own elasticity. With age the lens becomes denser and loses its elasticity — it cannot become sufficiently convex, and the near point of clear vision recedes (more than 20–25 cm at 40 years). Distance vision (without accommodation) does not change. Correction — reading glasses with convex (converging, “+”) lenses.
s7842 A 35-year-old woman has headaches, menstrual irregularity and milk discharge from the breasts. She says that on the road she fails to see cars coming from the side. On perimetry the temporal fields of both eyes are lost.

s7844 Bitemporal hemianopia. Here the central part of the optic chiasm is damaged — where the fibers from the nasal (medial) halves of the retinas cross. The nasal half of the retina receives the temporal (lateral) part of the visual field, so the temporal fields of both eyes are lost. Because the chiasm lies directly above the pituitary gland, the most common cause is a pituitary tumor; in this patient it may be a prolactin-producing tumor (prolactinoma): hyperprolactinemia leads to milk discharge (galactorrhea) and menstrual disturbance.
s7845 A young man undergoing a medical examination for a driving license could not see many of the numbers on the Rabkin plates, especially those made of red and green dots.
s7846 What is the condition? Explain its cause and inheritance.
s7847 A red-green color vision defect (protanopia/protanomaly or deuteranopia/deuteranomaly — Daltonism). According to the three-component theory, colors are perceived through the excitation to different degrees of three kinds of cones (blue, green, red); if the red or green cone pigment is absent or altered, red and green cannot be distinguished. The genes for the green and red cone pigments are located on the X chromosome, and the defect is inherited as an X-linked recessive trait: it is common in men (who have one X chromosome) (≈ 8%), while women are most often carriers. Such people distinguish traffic lights not by color but by position and brightness; this is taken into account in occupational selection.

s7887 1. Structure of the auditory system
s7888 **Sound** is mechanical vibration propagating in air (or another medium). Its physical characteristics: **frequency** (Hz) — perceived as pitch (low or high); **amplitude (intensity)** — perceived as loudness, measured in **decibels (dB)**; **timbre** — the composition of harmonics.

s7901 Functions of the middle ear
s7902 **Amplification of sound pressure (impedance matching)** — when sound passes from air into fluid, 99.9% of its energy is reflected. The middle ear overcomes this: the area of the tympanic membrane (≈ 55 mm^2^) is ≈ 17 times larger than that of the oval window (≈ 3.2 mm^2^), and with the lever system of the ossicles (≈ 1.3 times) the pressure increases **≈ 20–22 times**.
s7903 **Protection** — with loud sound (> 70–80 dB) the **tensor tympani** (nerve V) and the **stapedius muscle** (nerve VII) contract reflexly and limit the movement of the ossicles (the **acoustic reflex**; latency 10–40 ms — it does not protect against sudden explosions).
s7904 **Pressure equalization** — the **Eustachian (auditory) tube** opens during swallowing and yawning and equalizes the pressure in the middle ear with atmospheric pressure.
s7905 When a plane is landing or when diving, the external pressure changes; if the Eustachian tube does not open (cold, inflammation), the tympanic membrane is pressed and pain and hearing loss occur (barotrauma). Swallowing, yawning or the Valsalva maneuver help. In children the Eustachian tube is short and wide, so pharyngeal infections quickly spread to the middle ear (**otitis**).
s7906 Bone conduction
s7907 Sound reaches the inner ear **through the air** (the main route) and **through vibration of the skull bones** (bone conduction). The fact that we hear our own voice differently on a recording is due to the contribution of bone conduction.
s7908 2. The inner ear: the cochlea and the organ of Corti
s7909 The **cochlea** is a bony canal coiled 2.5 times; it contains three cavities: the **scala vestibuli** (connected with the oval window) and the **scala tympani** (with the round window) — filled with **perilymph** (rich in Na^+^, similar to extracellular fluid); and the **scala media (cochlear duct)** — filled with **endolymph** (**rich in K^+^**, ≈ 150 mmol/l). The endolymph has a positive charge of **+80 mV** relative to the perilymph (the **endocochlear potential**; generated by the stria vascularis).
s7910 The **organ of Corti (spiral organ)** lies on the basilar membrane: **inner hair cells** (≈ 3500, one row — the main sensory receptors; 90–95% of the afferent fibers come to them) and **outer hair cells** (≈ 12,000, 3–4 rows — the **cochlear amplifier**: by contracting they enhance the vibration of the basilar membrane and increase sensitivity). Above the stereocilia of the hair cells lies the **tectorial membrane**.

s7913 Mechanoelectrical transduction
s7914 When the basilar membrane vibrates, the stereocilia of the hair cells shift relative to the tectorial membrane. When the stereocilia bend **toward the tallest stereocilium**, the tip links at their ends open the mechanosensitive channels and **K^+^ enters the cell from the endolymph** (because of the high K^+^ and the large electrical gradient) — the cell **depolarizes** (receptor potential), Ca^2+^ channels open, the mediator (**glutamate**) is released and action potentials arise in the neurons of the spiral ganglion. Bending in the opposite direction causes hyperpolarization.

s7917 Coding of pitch and loudness
s7918 **The place (resonance) theory** (H. Helmholtz; the modern **“traveling wave”** theory — G. von Békésy, Nobel Prize 1961): the width and stiffness of the basilar membrane change from its base to its apex. **High-frequency** sounds produce maximal vibration at the **base** of the membrane (next to the oval window, the narrow and stiff part), and **low-frequency** sounds at the **apex** (the wide and soft part). Each frequency excites a particular group of hair cells — **tonotopy** (preserved in the auditory pathways and cortex).
s7919 **The frequency (telephone, volley) theory** — at low frequencies (< 1000–4000 Hz) neurons fire impulses at a rhythm matching the frequency of the sound (several neurons in turn — the “volley” principle).
s7920 **Loudness** is coded by the number of excited neurons and the frequency of impulses.
s7921 Limits of hearing
s7922 Humans hear sounds **from 16–20 Hz to 20,000 Hz** (the upper limit falls with age — 10–12 kHz at 60). The highest sensitivity is in the **1000–4000 Hz** range (speech frequencies 300–4000 Hz). Below 16 Hz — **infrasound**; above 20 kHz — **ultrasound**.
