s6311 What is the condition? Explain its mechanism.
s6312 Central diabetes insipidus. The injury has damaged the hypothalamus–neurohypophysis system and vasopressin release has decreased. Without vasopressin, aquaporin-2 channels are not inserted into the membrane of the collecting ducts and water is not reabsorbed into the hypertonic medulla — a large volume of dilute urine is excreted (polyuria, hyposthenuria). The loss of water raises plasma osmolarity and Na^+^ — the osmoreceptors and the thirst center are excited (polydipsia). Glucose is normal — it is not diabetes mellitus. Treatment — a synthetic vasopressin analogue (desmopressin).
s6313 After a car accident with a blood loss of 1.5 l, a patient has an arterial pressure of 80/50 mm Hg and hourly urine output of 15 ml (normal ≥ 0.5 ml/kg/hour).
s6314 Why has urine output decreased sharply? What compensatory mechanisms have been activated?
s6315 Arterial pressure has fallen below the limit of renal autoregulation (70 mm Hg), and the sympathetic nerves have constricted the afferent arterioles — the hydrostatic pressure in the glomerular capillaries and the effective filtration pressure have decreased sharply — GFR has fallen (oliguria). Compensatory mechanisms: sympathetic activation (tachycardia, vasoconstriction); the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (angiotensin II constricts vessels and, by constricting the efferent arteriole, partly maintains GFR; aldosterone retains Na^+^ and water); vasopressin release (through the baroreceptors in response to reduced blood volume) — water reabsorption increases, and urine decreases and becomes concentrated. These mechanisms are aimed at preserving blood volume; prolonged hypotension can lead to acute kidney injury.
s6316 A patient with diabetes mellitus has blood glucose of 14 mmol/l, urine glucose of 2%, daily diuresis of 3 l and thirst.
s6317 Explain the mechanism of the glucosuria and polyuria.
s6318 In the proximal tubule glucose is reabsorbed through the SGLT2 and SGLT1 transporters, whose number is limited (the transport maximum). When blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold (8.9–10 mmol/l), the amount of filtered glucose exceeds the capacity of the transporters and the excess passes into the urine — hyperglycemic glucosuria. The glucose remaining in the tubules, as an osmotically active substance, retains water and reduces its reabsorption — osmotic diuresis (polyuria). The loss of water causes thirst (polydipsia).

s6360 1. Reflex activity of the central nervous system
s6361 A **reflex** is the body's response to stimulation of receptors with the participation of the CNS. The material basis of the reflex is the **reflex arc**: receptor → afferent (sensory) neuron → nerve center (interneurons) → efferent (motor) neuron → effector.
s6362 Figure 23.1
s6363 Reflex arcs: A — polysynaptic (through an interneuron); B — monosynaptic (Alavia O.T., 2018)
s6364 **Monosynaptic** reflex arc — the afferent neuron ends directly on the motoneuron (one central synapse): **tendon (myotatic) reflexes** — knee, Achilles.
s6365 **Polysynaptic** reflex arc — through one or more interneurons: flexor (protective), skin and autonomic reflexes. **Reflex time** is the time from the stimulus to the beginning of the response. It consists of the excitation of the receptors, the conduction of excitation along the afferent and efferent fibers, and the **central reflex time** (the delay at the synapses of the center). When the stimulus strength increases, the reflex time shortens (summation).
s6366 A **nerve center** is the set of neurons that carry out a particular reflex or function. Functionally, the center of a complex function is located at different levels of the CNS (for example, the respiratory center — spinal cord, medulla oblongata, pons, hypothalamus, cortex).
s6367 2. Central synapses: EPSP and IPSP
s6368 There are thousands of synapses on the surface of a central neuron. Depending on the type of mediator and receptor:
s6369 **Excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)** — **depolarization** of the postsynaptic membrane (Na^+^ entry); the main mediator is **glutamate** (AMPA, NMDA receptors), and also acetylcholine. A single EPSP is small (1–2 mV); for a neuron to be excited, many EPSPs must summate.
s6370 **Inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)** — **hyperpolarization** (Cl^-^ entry or K^+^ efflux); mediators — **GABA** (the main one in the brain) and **glycine** (in the spinal cord and brainstem). The neuron integrates all EPSPs and IPSPs at the **axon hillock**: if the result reaches the critical level, an action potential is generated.

s6403 4. Inhibition in the central nervous system
s6404 **Inhibition** is an active nervous process that prevents the appearance or spread of excitation. It arises as a result of excitation and does not itself spread.
s6405 **Central inhibition was discovered by I.M. Sechenov** (1862): when the frog brain was transected at the level of the optic thalami and a crystal of NaCl was placed on the cut surface, the time of the withdrawal reflex on immersing the foot in sulfuric acid was sharply prolonged. Therefore the higher parts of the brain can inhibit spinal reflexes.

s6454 6. Structure and functions of the spinal cord
s6455 The spinal cord lies in the vertebral canal and consists of 31 segments (8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal). A pair of spinal nerves leaves each segment.
s6456 **Bell–Magendie law**: the dorsal roots consist of **sensory** (afferent) fibers, and the ventral roots of **motor** (efferent) fibers.
s6457 **Gray matter: dorsal horns** — sensory (inter)neurons; **ventral horns** — **α-motoneurons** (extrafusal fibers of skeletal muscles) and **γ-motoneurons** (muscle spindles); **lateral horns** (Th~1~–L~2~ — sympathetic, S~2~–S~4~ — parasympathetic preganglionic neurons). **White matter** — conducting pathways.
s6458 A **dermatome** is the area of skin receiving sensory innervation from one spinal segment; a **myotome** is the muscles innervated by one segment. One muscle usually receives innervation from several segments (the principle of reliability).
