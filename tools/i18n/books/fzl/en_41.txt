s8478 **Specifically human types of HNA** (according to the ratio of the signal systems): the **artistic type** (the first signal system predominates — imaginative, emotional thinking), the **thinking type** (the second signal system predominates — abstract, logical thinking) and the **intermediate (mixed) type**.
s8479 5. Signal systems
s8480 **The first signal system** is the perception of environmental stimuli in the form of concrete images through **direct sensations** (vision, hearing, smell, taste, skin). It is common to animals and humans.
s8481 **The second signal system** is signaling through **speech (the word)**; the word is the “signal of signals” (Pavlov). It is specific to humans and is the basis of **abstract thinking**, the formation of concepts, generalization, upbringing and education, and the transmission of social experience to the next generations. A word can evoke a conditioned reflex just like first-signal stimuli (for example, the word “lemon” increases salivation). The two signal systems are closely interconnected; the second signal system develops on the basis of the first and controls it.
s8482 **Speech centers:** Broca's (motor) and Wernicke's (sensory) areas — topic 25; for reading and writing, the angular gyrus and the corresponding areas of the frontal lobe.

s8527 Exercise 1: forming a conditioned blink reflex in humans (demonstration)
s8528 The subject sits in front of a device that blows a puff of air into the eye; the blinking movement is recorded.
s8529 An indifferent stimulus (a sound signal or light) is given, and it is checked that it does not cause blinking.
s8530 0.5–1 s after the sound signal a puff of air is blown into the eye (the unconditioned stimulus — the blink reflex); this is repeated 10–20 times.
s8531 Then the sound signal alone is given.
s8532 **Result and analysis:** after several pairings, blinking appears to the sound alone — a **conditioned defensive reflex** has been formed. Presenting the sound several times without reinforcement leads to **extinction** of the reflex (internal inhibition).
s8533 Exercise 2: determining the capacity of short-term memory
s8534 Twelve unrelated two-digit numbers (or words) are read to the subject at 1 s intervals.
s8535 The subject immediately writes down those he remembers, in any order.
s8536 The number written correctly is counted. **Interpretation:** normally **7 ± 2** items (Miller's number). Material linked by meaning (grouping) is easier to remember.
s8537 Exercise 3: determining functional asymmetry
s8538 **Dominant hand**: clapping the hands (which hand is on top), crossing the arms on the chest, interlocking the fingers (which thumb is on top).
s8539 **Dominant eye**: look at a distant object through a hole in a sheet of paper with both eyes, then close one eye at a time — the eye with which the object does not “shift” is dominant.
s8540 **Dominant leg**: kicking a ball, hopping on one leg.
s8541 Summarize the results: right-handed, left-handed or ambidextrous (using both hands equally).
s8542 Exercise 4: determining the type of HNA (based on questionnaires and observation)

s8544 Place the result in the Eysenck circle and determine the temperament type (stable extravert — sanguine, unstable extravert — choleric, stable introvert — phlegmatic, unstable introvert — melancholic).
s8545 Perform a reaction speed (sensorimotor reaction time) test and compare the results.
s8546 Skill: drawing up a plan for forming a conditioned reflex
s8547 Choose an unconditioned reflex (for example, food — salivation).
s8548 Choose an indifferent signal and check that it does not evoke a response by itself.
s8549 Give the signal 1–5 s before the unconditioned stimulus and repeat the pairing.
s8550 Ensure an alert state of the cortex, motivation (hunger) and the absence of extraneous stimuli.
s8551 Test with the signal alone to check that the reflex has formed; if you present it without reinforcement, observe its extinction.
s8552 Skill: taking HNA features into account when working with patients
s8553 Observe the patient's temperament and emotional reactions.
s8554 Give information to a patient of the melancholic type step by step, calmly and in a supportive tone.
s8555 Speak patiently, clearly and briefly with a patient of the choleric type.
s8556 Help turn the treatment regimen (times of taking drugs, meals) into a dynamic stereotype.
s8557 Take into account the effect of the word (the second signal system): the doctor's word can both improve and worsen the patient's condition (iatrogenesis).

s8635 After several courses of chemotherapy, a patient began to experience nausea and vomiting as soon as he entered the hospital building or saw the nurse (before the drug had been given).
s8636 Explain this phenomenon from the standpoint of HNA physiology. How can it be reduced?
s8637 This is a conditioned reflex (conditioned nausea). The chemotherapy drug is an unconditioned stimulus that excites the vomiting center; indifferent stimuli such as the hospital environment, the sight of the nurse and the smell of the room acted several times before and together with the drug administration — a temporary connection was formed in the cortical and subcortical structures. Now these signals by themselves excite the vomiting center. Ways to reduce it: effective use of antiemetic drugs from the first course (preventing the unconditioned response — the reflex is not formed), changing the environment, relaxation and psychotherapy (presenting the signal without reinforcement to develop extinctive inhibition).
s8638 A student wakes up every morning at 7, has breakfast and goes to class at 8. Even on vacation he wakes at 7 without an alarm clock, and his appetite appears at that time. When he moved to a new country (a 5-hour time difference), he had daytime sleepiness and nighttime insomnia for a week.
s8639 How can these phenomena be explained?
s8640 On the basis of a regular daily routine, temporal conditioned reflexes and a dynamic stereotype have formed: waking at a certain time and the appearance of appetite (secretion of digestive juices) have become conditioned reflexes. Circadian rhythms (the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus — the “biological clock”, melatonin) also underlie this. When the time zone changes abruptly, the internal biological clock and the system of conditioned reflexes no longer match external time — desynchronosis (jet lag): the sleep–wake cycle is disturbed. Readjustment of the new stereotype and the biological clock (roughly 1 day for every hour of difference) takes several days; morning light and a regular new regimen speed up adaptation.
s8641 After a stroke a patient cannot move his right arm and leg and cannot speak, but he can hum tunes and songs and recognizes familiar people by their faces.
s8642 Which hemisphere is damaged? Why are some functions preserved?
s8643 The left hemisphere is damaged. Paralysis of the right arm and leg — damage to the motor cortex or pyramidal tract of the left hemisphere (the pathways cross). Loss of speech (aphasia) — in 95% of right-handed people the speech centers are in the left hemisphere. Humming the melody of tunes and songs and face recognition are mainly functions of the right hemisphere, which is not damaged. This demonstrates functional asymmetry; teaching patients to speak through singing (melodic intonation therapy) in speech rehabilitation is based on using the capacities of the right hemisphere.
