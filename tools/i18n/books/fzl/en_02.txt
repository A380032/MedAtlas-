s0185 6. The action potential
s0186 The **action potential** is a rapid, short-lasting change in the membrane potential during excitation, in which the membrane charge is reversed (the inner surface becomes positive). In a nerve fiber it lasts 0.5–2 ms and its amplitude is 100–120 mV.

s0236 7. Laboratory work
s0237 Experiment 1: preparing the frog nerve-muscle preparation
s0238 Immobilize the frog as instructed by the teacher (destruction of the spinal cord — a “spinal frog”).
s0239 Cut the body just below the forelimbs and remove the internal organs; leave the hind limbs and the lumbar part of the vertebral column.
s0240 Pull the skin downward like a stocking.
s0241 Divide the vertebral column in the middle and separate the two legs into individual preparations.
s0242 Carefully isolate the sciatic nerve in the thigh region, and dissect out the calf muscle (m. gastrocnemius) together with the Achilles tendon.
s0243 Keep the preparation moist with Ringer's solution (if it dries, excitability is lost).
s0244 Experiment 2: Galvani's first experiment
s0245 Take bimetallic forceps made of copper and zinc.
s0246 Touch the tips of the forceps simultaneously to the nerves of the preparation (in front of the vertebral column).
s0247 Watch the muscle contract at each touch. **Results and analysis:** L. Galvani explained the contraction by “animal electricity”. A. Volta, however, proved that the cause of the contraction is the **current of a galvanic cell** formed by two different metals and the tissue fluid (an electrolyte). Thus the first experiment is not yet proof of animal electricity.
s0248 Experiment 3: Galvani's second experiment (without metal)
s0249 Lift the sciatic nerve of a nerve-muscle preparation with a glass hook.
s0250 Lower the nerve simultaneously onto the **injured** (cut) surface of the muscle and onto its uninjured surface.
s0251 Watch the muscle contract. **Results and analysis:** the muscle contracts even without metal. The injured surface is negatively charged relative to the uninjured one (the **injury current**, demarcation potential); the nerve acts as a bridge between the two surfaces and the current stimulates it. This experiment was the first proof of the **existence of bioelectric potentials** in tissues.
s0252 Experiment 4: Matteucci's secondary contraction experiment
s0253 Prepare two nerve-muscle preparations.
s0254 Place the nerve of the second preparation on the muscle of the first preparation.
s0255 Stimulate the nerve of the first preparation with an electric current. **Results and analysis:** both muscles contract. The **action potentials** generated when the first muscle contracts stimulate the second nerve lying on it. The experiment proves that bioelectric potentials are generated during excitation.

s0371 Case 1
s0372 In a patient with chronic renal failure, blood potassium reached 7.2 mmol/l. The patient developed muscle weakness and a disturbance of the heart rhythm.
s0373 How does a rise in blood potassium affect the resting potential? Why is the activity of excitable tissues disturbed?
s0374 The resting potential is generated mainly by the diffusion of K^+^ out of the cell. When extracellular K^+^ increases, the concentration gradient decreases and less K^+^ leaves — the membrane depolarizes. At first excitability may increase, but with prolonged depolarization the voltage-gated Na^+^ channels are inactivated and a full action potential cannot arise. The result is muscle weakness, disturbed cardiac conduction and, in severe cases, cardiac arrest.
s0375 Case 2
s0376 Before dental treatment, a dentist injected lidocaine. A few minutes later the patient no longer felt pain in that area, while sensation was preserved in other areas.
s0377 By what mechanism does lidocaine abolish pain? Which phase of the action potential is disturbed?
s0378 Lidocaine blocks the voltage-gated Na^+^ channels in the membrane of nerve fibers. The (painful) stimulus cannot open the Na^+^ channels, depolarization does not reach the critical level, and the rapid depolarization phase of the action potential does not develop. Excitation does not spread along the fiber and the pain signal does not reach the center. The effect is limited to the injected area, so sensation is preserved elsewhere.
s0379 Case 3
s0380 A student performed Galvani's first experiment: when the copper-zinc forceps touched the nerve, the muscle contracted. He explained this as “the muscle has its own electricity”.
s0381 Is the student's conclusion correct? Which experiment actually proves the presence of bioelectric potentials in tissue?
s0382 The conclusion is wrong. As A. Volta showed, in the first experiment the two different metals and the tissue fluid form a galvanic cell, and its current stimulates the nerve. The presence of bioelectric potentials in tissue is proved by Galvani's second experiment (without metal): when the nerve touches the injured and uninjured surfaces of the muscle, the muscle contracts under the action of the injury current. Matteucci's secondary contraction experiment shows that action potentials are generated during excitation.

s0410 Laws of stimulation
s0411 To teach the student the laws by which excitable tissues respond to stimuli — the laws of strength, duration and rate of rise of stimulus strength (the gradient law), the “all-or-none” law, the polar law of direct current, the changes in excitability during excitation and the concept of lability; to teach the determination of threshold strength and chronaxie.
s0412 the relationship between the stimulation threshold (threshold strength) and excitability;
s0413 the law of strength and the “all-or-none” law, and the limits of their application;
s0414 the strength–duration curve (the Hoorweg–Weiss–Lapicque curve) and the concepts of rheobase, utilization time and chronaxie;
s0415 the phenomenon of accommodation and the gradient law;
s0416 Pflüger's polar law, catelectrotonus and anelectrotonus, and Verigo's cathodal depression;
s0417 the phasic changes in excitability during excitation;
s0418 the concept of lability (N.E. Vvedensky) and its value in different tissues.
s0419 determine the stimulation threshold of a nerve and a muscle in a nerve-muscle preparation;
s0420 demonstrate the law of strength experimentally (threshold, submaximal and maximal contraction);
s0421 demonstrate the polar law with direct current;
s0422 explain the clinical significance of chronaxie.
s0423 Excitability is **inversely proportional** to the threshold. The threshold of a nerve is lower than that of a muscle — the nerve is more excitable; this is why, in a preparation, it is easier to stimulate a muscle through its nerve (indirect stimulation).
s0424 The “all-or-none” law is **relative**: 1) a subthreshold stimulus is not “nothing” either — it evokes a local response and increases excitability; 2) the size of the response depends not on the stimulus but on the state of the tissue at that moment (fatigue, temperature, ions). A whole skeletal muscle obeys not this law but the law of strength, while the heart, as a syncytium, obeys the “all-or-none” law.
s0425 The rate of accommodation differs between tissues: it is high in motor nerve fibers and low in smooth muscles and sensory fibers. Tissues with low accommodation respond even to slowly rising stimuli.
s0426 **“Cathode on make, anode on break”**: when the circuit is closed (current switched on) excitation arises at the cathode, and when it is opened (current switched off) — at the anode. The cathode “warms up” the membrane (depolarization), the anode “cools it down” (hyperpolarization).
