s7544 A patient with bronchial asthma was prescribed a non-selective β-blocker (propranolol) for hypertension, after which his attacks of breathlessness increased.
s7545 Why did the asthma worsen? Which drug would have been a better choice?
s7546 The smooth muscles of the bronchi contain β~2~-adrenergic receptors, which dilate the bronchi under the action of adrenaline. A non-selective β-blocker blocks the β~2~ receptors together with β~1~ (heart) — the bronchodilating sympathetic influence is lost and the parasympathetic (M~3~ — bronchoconstricting) influence predominates — bronchospasm increases. Non-selective β-blockers are contraindicated in asthmatic patients; if necessary, cardioselective β~1~-blockers are chosen with caution, or antihypertensive drugs of other groups (calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors).

s7590 1. General concept of the sensory systems
s7591 An **analyzer** (I.P. Pavlov) is the set of nervous structures that receive, process and analyze stimuli from the external and internal environment. It consists of three divisions:
s7592 **The peripheral (receptor) division** — converts the energy of the stimulus into a nerve impulse.
s7593 **The conducting division** — afferent pathways and intermediate centers (brainstem, thalamus).
s7594 **The central (cortical) division** — the corresponding area of the cerebral cortex; here the **sensation** is formed. In modern physiology the term “**sensory system**” is also used.

s7596 By location
s7597 Exteroceptors (external environment), interoceptors (internal organs), proprioceptors (muscles, tendons, joints)
s7598 By distance of the stimulus
s7599 Distant (vision, hearing, smell) and contact (taste, touch)

s7602 By structure

s7606 Properties of receptors:
s7607 **High sensitivity to the adequate stimulus** (the eye can detect a few photons of light).
s7608 **Transformation** — the energy of the stimulus is converted into a **receptor potential** (a graded, local response) and then into action potentials; the strength of the stimulus is coded by the **frequency** of impulses.
s7609 **Adaptation** — a decrease in sensitivity to a constant stimulus.
s7610 **Rhythmic response** (spontaneous activity). **Weber–Fechner law:** the intensity of sensation is proportional to the **logarithm** of the stimulus strength. Weber's law: the smallest noticeable change in stimulus strength is a constant fraction of the initial strength (for example, ≈ 1/30 for a weight).

s7651 The pupil and its reflexes
s7652 The pupil acts as the diaphragm of a camera: it regulates the amount of light entering the eye, reduces spherical aberration and increases the depth of focus. Its diameter is 1.5–8 mm. The **constrictor** muscle is parasympathetic (nerve III), the **dilator** muscle sympathetic (α~1~). The pupil constricts in light (direct and crossed reaction) and when looking near; it dilates in darkness, fear and pain.
s7653 3. The receptor apparatus of the eye
s7654 The **retina** is a 10-layer structure; the photoreceptors are located in its outermost layer (next to the pigment epithelium), and light passes through the other layers of the retina to reach them. Signal pathway: **photoreceptors → bipolar cells → ganglion cells** (whose axons form the optic nerve); horizontal and amacrine cells provide **lateral inhibition** (contrast enhancement).

s7679 Phototransduction
s7680 In darkness the photoreceptors contain much **cGMP**, and the cGMP-gated Na^+^ channels are open — the **“dark current”** flows; the membrane is relatively depolarized (−40 mV) and **glutamate** is released continuously.
s7681 Light converts **11-cis-retinal into all-trans-retinal** in **rhodopsin**; rhodopsin is activated.
s7682 Activated rhodopsin activates the G protein **transducin** → **phosphodiesterase** breaks down cGMP.
s7683 cGMP decreases and the Na^+^ channels close — the photoreceptor **hyperpolarizes** (−70 mV) and glutamate release decreases.
s7684 This change is processed in the bipolar and ganglion cells, and action potentials are generated in the ganglion cells.
s7685 Dark and light adaptation
s7686 **Dark adaptation** — on passing from light to darkness, sensitivity increases thousands of times; first through the cones (5–10 minutes), then through the rods (up to 30–40 minutes) — resynthesis of rhodopsin, pupil dilation, neuronal convergence.
s7687 **Light adaptation** is fast (a few minutes): breakdown of rhodopsin, pupil constriction.
s7688 Color vision
s7689 **The three-component theory** (T. Young, H. Helmholtz): the retina has three kinds of cones, sensitive to **blue (short-wave, ≈ 420 nm)**, **green (medium, ≈ 530 nm)** and **red (long-wave, ≈ 560 nm)** light. Any color is perceived as a combination of their excitation to different degrees. **The opponent theory** (E. Hering) explains the processing of signals in the ganglion cells and thalamus in opposing pairs (red–green, blue–yellow, white–black); the two theories complement each other.

s7707 4. Visual pathways and centers
s7708 The axons of the ganglion cells form the **optic nerve**. In the **optic chiasm** the fibers from the **nasal (medial) halves** of the retinas cross, while those from the temporal (lateral) halves do not. Thus each **optic tract** carries information about the opposite half of the visual field of both eyes. The fibers go to the **lateral geniculate body** (thalamus) and partly to the superior colliculi of the midbrain (orienting reflexes) and the pretectal area (pupillary reflex). From the lateral geniculate body the **optic radiation** (Gratiolet's bundle) goes to the **primary visual cortex** (area 17, V~1~) around the calcarine sulcus of the **occipital lobe**; areas 18–19 are the secondary (associative) visual areas.

s7721 Exercise 1: determining visual acuity (Sivtsev chart)
s7722 The subject sits **5 m** from a well-lit chart; one eye is covered with an occluder.
s7723 The examiner points with a pointer to the letters on the chart from top to bottom; the subject reads them.
s7724 Write down the V number beside the lowest line the subject can read correctly; the second eye is also tested.
s7725 **Interpretation: Snellen formula**: V = d / D, where d is the test distance (5 m) and D the distance at which a normal eye should read that line. If line 10 is read from 5 m, V = 1.0 (normal). Visual acuity is the reciprocal of the smallest angle needed to see two points separately (normally 1 minute of arc).
s7726 Exercise 2: determining the visual field (perimetry)
s7727 The subject rests the chin on the support in front of the perimeter, with one eye closed, and looks at the central mark with the other.
s7728 A white (then red, green, blue) object is moved slowly along the perimeter arc from the periphery toward the center.
s7729 The angle at which the object is first seen is marked on the form; the arc is rotated every 15–30° and the test is repeated. **Interpretation:** normal limits for white: 90° outward, 60° inward, 50–55° upward, 65–70° downward. The field for colored objects is narrower (blue > red > green), because the cones are mainly in the center of the retina.
s7730 Exercise 3: detecting the blind spot (Mariotte's experiment)
s7731 Draw a cross on the left of a sheet of paper and a black circle 8–10 cm to its right.
s7732 Close the left eye and look at the cross with the right eye.
s7733 Slowly bring the paper closer to the eye and then move it away. **Result and analysis:** at a certain distance (≈ 20–25 cm) the circle disappears — its image falls on the optic disc (the blind spot), where there are no photoreceptors. In everyday life the blind spot is not noticed because of binocular vision, the constant small movements of the eyes and the “filling in” of the image by the cortex.
