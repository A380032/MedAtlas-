s6921 In a patient under general anesthesia, the anesthesiologist regularly checks the state of the pupils and their reaction to light.
s6922 What is the pathway of the pupillary reflex? What might its loss indicate?
s6923 Light excites the photoreceptors of the retina; impulses travel along the optic nerve to the pretectal nucleus in the midbrain and from there to the Yakubovich–Edinger–Westphal nuclei on both sides; parasympathetic fibers in cranial nerve III go to the ciliary ganglion and from there to the pupillary constrictor muscle — the pupil constricts (direct and crossed reaction). Because the reflex center is in the midbrain, its loss and dilation of the pupils may indicate deep depression of brainstem function (excessively deep anesthesia), severe hypoxia or damage to the midbrain. That is why the state of the pupils is an important indicator of the depth of anesthesia and the condition of the brain.

s6960 1. The cerebellum
s6961 The cerebellum lies behind the pons and medulla oblongata; it consists of two hemispheres and the vermis. Its cortex has three layers (molecular, Purkinje cell and granular), and inside there are nuclei: the **fastigial**, **globose**, **emboliform** and **dentate** nuclei.

s7035 3. The basal (subcortical) ganglia
s7036 The **basal ganglia** are clusters of gray matter located within the white matter of the cerebral hemispheres: the **caudate nucleus** and the **putamen** — together the **striatum**; the **globus pallidus (pallidum)**; and, functionally related to them, the **subthalamic nucleus** and the **substantia nigra** in the midbrain.
s7037 **Connections:** cortex (almost all areas) → **striatum** → globus pallidus / substantia nigra → **thalamus ** → cortex (motor and prefrontal). This **cortex–basal ganglia–thalamus–cortex loop** works through two pathways:
s7038 **The direct pathway** **enhances** the influence of the thalamus on the cortex — it “starts up” the required movements;
s7039 **The indirect pathway** (through the subthalamic nucleus) **inhibits** the thalamus — it suppresses unwanted movements. **Dopamine** from the **substantia nigra** activates the direct pathway (D~1~ receptors) and inhibits the indirect pathway (D~2~) — as a result it increases motor activity.
s7040 **Functions of the basal ganglia:** initiating and stopping movements; selecting motor programs; automatic movements (arm swing during walking, facial expression); regulating muscle tone; setting the amplitude and speed of movement; motor learning; cognitive and motivational functions.

s7056 4. The cerebral cortex
s7057 The cortex is a layer of gray matter 1.5–4.5 mm thick, with a surface area of ≈ 2200 cm^2^ and 14–16 billion neurons. Phylogenetically, the **ancient cortex (archicortex)**, the **old cortex (paleocortex)** and the **new cortex (neocortex — 96%)** are distinguished. The neocortex consists of **6 layers**; layer IV (internal granular) mainly receives afferent inputs (from the thalamus), and layer V (internal pyramidal — with the giant Betz pyramidal cells in the motor cortex) is the main efferent output. K. Brodmann divided the cortex cytoarchitectonically into **52 areas**.
s7058 **Functional organization:** neurons are grouped into **vertical columns** — each column processes a particular type of stimulus (V. Mountcastle).

s7102 Somatotopic organization — the homunculus
s7103 By stimulating the cortex electrically in patients during surgery, W. The projection area of a body part depends not on its size but on its **functional significance** (receptor density, precision of movements) — the hand, fingers, lips and tongue occupy a very large area.

s7108 5. The limbic system
s7109 The **limbic system** (the “visceral brain”, P. MacLean) is a set of cortical and subcortical structures: the **hippocampus**, the cingulate gyrus, the **amygdala**, the septum, the hypothalamus (mammillary bodies), the anterior thalamic nuclei and others. They are connected through the **Papez circuit** (hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → anterior thalamic nuclei → cingulate gyrus → hippocampus).

s7112 **Functions: emotions** (fear, anger, pleasure — amygdala); **motivation** and instinctive behavior (feeding, defensive, sexual); **memory** — the hippocampus transfers short-term memory into long-term memory (consolidation); coordination of autonomic and endocrine reactions with emotions.
s7113 In bilateral damage to the **hippocampus** (the famous patient H.M. after epilepsy surgery), the ability to remember new events is lost (anterograde amnesia), while old memories and motor skills are preserved. **Alzheimer's disease** begins with the early death of hippocampal neurons.
s7114 6. Electroencephalography
s7115 **Electroencephalography (EEG)** is a method of recording the biopotentials of the brain from the scalp (H. Berger, 1929). EEG waves mainly reflect the sum of the **postsynaptic potentials ** of cortical pyramidal neurons.
