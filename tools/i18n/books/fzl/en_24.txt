s5076 **Main stimulants: acetylcholine** (vagus nerve; M~3~-cholinergic receptors), **gastrin** (G cells; released under the influence of peptides, amino acids, gastric distension and the vagus) and **histamine** (ECL cells; H~2~ receptors — the most powerful stimulant). **Inhibitors:** somatostatin, secretin, cholecystokinin, GIP, prostaglandins, a fall of pH in the stomach cavity below 1.5–2 (negative feedback), sympathetic influence.

s5078 Motor activity of the stomach
s5079 When food enters, the proximal part of the stomach relaxes reflexly — **receptive relaxation** (via the vagus) — so food accumulates without a rise in pressure. In the distal part **peristaltic contractions** (≈ 3 /min; pacemaker — the Cajal cells on the greater curvature) mix the chyme and pass it in small portions through the pylorus into the duodenum. Food remains in the stomach for 3–10 hours: carbohydrate food is evacuated quickly, protein food more slowly, and fatty food most slowly. **Evacuation** is inhibited by acid, fats and hypertonic solutions in the duodenum (the **enterogastric reflex**, secretin, CCK).
s5080 **Vomiting** is a protective reflex; its center is in the medulla oblongata (the vomiting center and the trigger zone on the floor of the fourth ventricle, which is sensitive to toxins in the blood).

s5204 After radiotherapy for a head and neck tumor, a 55-year-old patient developed dry mouth, difficulty swallowing food and altered taste. Within six months caries developed in many teeth.
s5205 What are these changes associated with? Which functions of saliva are disturbed?
s5206 The radiotherapy damaged the salivary gland tissue — xerostomia (hyposalivation) developed. The water of saliva moistens food and dissolves taste substances — when it decreases, taste is impaired; mucin lubricates the bolus — when it is lacking, swallowing becomes difficult. The salivary buffer systems (bicarbonate, phosphate) neutralize the acids formed by bacteria in dental plaque, calcium and phosphate remineralize the enamel, and lysozyme, lactoferrin and IgA provide antibacterial protection — because these functions were lost, caries developed rapidly. Help: saliva substitutes, fluoride preparations, oral hygiene.
s5207 A 40-year-old patient has epigastric pain on an empty stomach and at night; the pain decreases after eating. FEGDS revealed a duodenal ulcer and H. pylori. Gastric juice acidity is increased.
s5208 Explain the physiological mechanism of ulcer development on the basis of the balance of protective and aggressive factors. What is the effect of a proton pump inhibitor?
s5209 An ulcer develops when the balance between aggressive factors (HCl, pepsin, H. pylori) and protective factors (the mucus-bicarbonate barrier, prostaglandins, blood flow, epithelial regeneration) is disturbed. H. pylori reduces the somatostatin-producing D cells in the antrum — gastrin release and HCl secretion increase; the excess acid enters the duodenum and damages the mucosa. Food buffers the acid — the pain decreases. Proton pump inhibitors irreversibly block the H^+^/K^+^-ATPase in the parietal cell — the final stage of HCl secretion stops (the response to any stimulant decreases) and the ulcer heals; H. pylori is eliminated with antibiotics.
s5210 An elderly patient who has lost many teeth and does not wear a denture swallows food without chewing it well. He complains of heaviness in the stomach, bloating and constipation.
s5211 How does impaired chewing affect the subsequent stages of digestion?
s5212 Chewing grinds food, increases the surface on which digestive enzymes act and mixes it with saliva. Poorly chewed large pieces are digested slowly in the stomach and stay there long (a feeling of heaviness). The reflex influence from the receptors of the oral cavity (the complex reflex phase) weakens — the “start-up” of gastric and pancreatic secretion and bile release is reduced. Undigested residues enhance fermentation and putrefaction in the large intestine (bloating). That is why dental treatment and prosthetics matter for the whole digestive system.

s5252 1. Motor activity of the gastrointestinal tract
s5253 Properties of smooth muscles
s5254 The wall of the gastrointestinal tract (except the upper esophagus and the external anal sphincter) contains smooth muscles: an inner **circular** and an outer **longitudinal** layer. They work as a **functional syncytium** (gap junctions).
s5255 Smooth muscles show rhythmic oscillations of the membrane potential — **slow waves** (the basic electrical rhythm); they are generated by the **interstitial cells of Cajal** (pacemakers). Slow waves do not cause contraction by themselves; if the membrane reaches threshold at their peak, **action potentials (spikes)** appear and the muscle contracts. Frequency: ≈ 3 /min in the stomach, 11–12 /min in the duodenum, 8–9 /min in the ileum.
s5256 The enteric nervous system
s5257 The **enteric (metasympathetic) nervous system** is an independent network of ≈ 100 million neurons in the gastrointestinal wall (the “second brain”):
s5258 **The myenteric (Auerbach) plexus** — between the circular and longitudinal layers; mainly regulates **motor** activity.
s5259 **The submucosal (Meissner) plexus** — regulates **secretion**, absorption and local blood flow.
s5260 The enteric nervous system carries out peristaltic and secretory reflexes even without the central nervous system; the autonomic nerves only modify its activity.
