s0427 Absolute refractoriness ensures that excitation spreads in **one direction** and sets the upper limit of the lability of a tissue. Because the refractory period of the heart is long, cardiac muscle **cannot contract tetanically**.
s0428 1. Stimulation threshold and excitability
s0429 To evoke excitation, a stimulus must meet three conditions: **sufficient strength**, **sufficient duration** and **a sufficiently fast rise in strength**. Each has its own law.
s0430 The **stimulation threshold (threshold strength)** is the smallest strength of a stimulus that can evoke excitation. The threshold is the main measure of excitability, and the two are **inversely proportional**: the lower the threshold, the higher the excitability.

s0450 2. The law of strength and the “all-or-none” law
s0451 **The law of strength:** a tissue of complex structure (a whole skeletal muscle, a nerve trunk) **increases its response** as the stimulus strength rises from threshold to maximal; beyond the maximal strength the response no longer changes. The reason is that a muscle consists of thousands of fibers with different excitability: as the strength rises, more and more fibers are recruited into excitation. With a very strong stimulus the response may decrease — the phenomenon of **pessimum**.

s0454 **The “all-or-none” law** (H. Bowditch, 1871): a single excitable structure (one nerve or muscle fiber) or a functional syncytium (cardiac muscle) does not respond to a subthreshold stimulus (“none”), while to a threshold or stronger stimulus it **always gives the same, maximal** response (“all”).
s0455 3. The law of duration: the strength–duration curve
s0456 To evoke excitation, a stimulus must last for a certain time. There is an **inverse relationship** between the strength of a stimulus and its duration: the shorter the stimulus, the greater the strength required. This relationship is expressed by the **strength–duration curve** (the Hoorweg–Weiss–Lapicque hyperbola).

s0479 **Chronaximetry** is a method of electrodiagnosis in neurology. When a nerve is damaged, the chronaxie of the muscle increases (excitability decreases) and it becomes difficult to stimulate the muscle through its nerve; when the nerve recovers, chronaxie returns to normal. It is used to follow peripheral nerve injuries and to choose the parameters of electrical stimulation in physiotherapy.
s0480 4. The gradient (accommodation) law
s0481 **The gradient law (du Bois-Reymond):** excitation depends not only on the absolute strength of a stimulus but also on its **rate of rise per unit of time**. If the strength rises steeply (quickly), excitation occurs; if it rises slowly, excitation may not occur even when the strength exceeds the threshold.
s0482 **Accommodation** is the adaptation of a tissue to a slowly rising stimulus; the critical level of depolarization rises (the threshold increases). Mechanism: during slow depolarization the Na^+^ channels are gradually **inactivated** and K^+^ permeability increases; as a result the regenerative Na^+^
s0483 current does not arise.

s0486 5. The polar law of direct current
s0487 **Pflüger's polar law** (1859): direct current stimulates a tissue only when the circuit is **made (closed)** and **broken (opened)**; while the current is flowing there is no excitation. When the circuit is **closed, excitation arises under the cathode**, and when it is **opened — under the anode**. The cathodal effect on closing is stronger than the anodal effect on opening.

s0510 The appearance of excitation under the anode **when the current is switched off** is explained as follows: while the current flows, the membrane is hyperpolarized, the inactivation of Na^+^ channels is removed and the CLD falls. When the current is switched off, the membrane potential is quickly restored, reaches the new, lowered CLD and evokes excitation.
s0511 In physiotherapy, **galvanization** and the introduction of drugs by current (**electrophoresis**) are based on the polar law. Because excitability is reduced under the anode, the anode is placed over a painful area to relieve pain, while the cathode is used to increase excitability.

s0537 7. Lability (functional mobility)
s0538 **Lability** (N.E. Vvedensky, 1886) is the maximum number of action potentials that an excitable tissue can generate per unit of time (per 1 s) in step with the rhythm of stimulation, without transforming it.
s0539 Lability depends primarily on the **duration of the absolute refractory period**: the shorter it is, the higher the lability.

s0548 8. Laboratory work
s0549 Experiment 1: determining the stimulation threshold of a nerve and a muscle
s0550 Prepare a frog nerve-muscle preparation and attach the muscle to the lever of a myograph (or kymograph).
s0551 Place the electrodes of the electrical stimulator on the sciatic nerve; increase the voltage gradually, starting from zero.
s0552 Record the voltage at which the muscle first contracts noticeably — this is the **threshold of the nerve** (indirect stimulation).
s0553 Place the electrodes directly on the muscle and repeat the experiment — the **threshold of the muscle** (direct stimulation).
s0554 Compare the two values. **Expected results and analysis:** the threshold of the nerve is lower than that of the muscle, i.e., the excitability of the nerve is higher. This is related to the membrane properties of nerve fibers (closer to the CLD).
s0555 Experiment 2: studying the law of strength
s0556 Starting from the threshold strength, increase the stimulus strength step by step (for example, by 0.1 V each time).
s0557 At each strength record the amplitude of the single contraction on the kymograph tape.
s0558 Mark the strength at which the amplitude stops increasing — the **maximal strength**.
s0559 Increase the strength further and show that the amplitude does not change (supramaximal strength). **Expected results and analysis:** from threshold to maximal strength the contraction amplitude increases, because the muscle consists of fibers of different excitability and, as the strength rises, more fibers are excited (the law of strength). At maximal strength all fibers are excited, so a further increase does not enhance the response. Each individual fiber, however, obeys the “all-or-none” law.
s0560 Experiment 3: demonstrating Pflüger's polar law
s0561 Place the nerve of a nerve-muscle preparation on two nonpolarizable electrodes; connect a direct current source through a switch.
s0562 Place the cathode closer to the muscle and the anode farther away; close and open the circuit and record the contractions.
s0563 Reverse the poles (anode closer to the muscle) and repeat the experiment.
s0564 Vary the current strength between weak, moderate and strong and record the results in a table. **Expected results and analysis:** with a weak current, contraction is seen only on closing the circuit (excitation under the cathode); with a moderate current — both on closing and on opening. With a strong current, if the anode is close to the muscle, the cathodal excitation on closing cannot pass through the hyperpolarized region under the anode (anodal block) — contraction occurs only on opening.
s0565 Skill: determining chronaxie from the strength–duration curve
s0566 Mark the stimulus strength on the ordinate and the duration on the abscissa.
s0567 Find the rheobase level from the flat part of the curve.
s0568 Multiply the rheobase by two and mark this point on the ordinate.
s0569 From this point draw a horizontal line until it crosses the curve.
s0570 From the point of intersection drop a perpendicular onto the abscissa — this is the **chronaxie**.
s0571 Compare the result with the type of tissue and draw a conclusion about its excitability.

s0662 A patient injured in the forearm has damage to the ulnar nerve. A neurologist performed electrodiagnosis and found that the chronaxie of the muscles innervated by this nerve was 10 times higher than normal. On repeat examination three months later, the chronaxie had begun to decrease.
s0663 What does chronaxie show? What do its increase and subsequent decrease mean?
s0664 Chronaxie is the shortest time for which a current equal to twice the rheobase must act to evoke excitation; it is a measure of excitability in terms of time. When a nerve is damaged, the muscle loses its innervation, indirect stimulation through nerve fibers becomes impossible, and the muscle's own excitability is low — so chronaxie rises sharply. The beginning of a decrease in chronaxie indicates regeneration of the nerve fibers and restoration of innervation. That is why chronaximetry is used to follow the course of nerve injuries.
