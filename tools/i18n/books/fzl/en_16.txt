s3442 What is the condition? Explain its mechanism. Is it a pathology?
s3443 Respiratory sinus arrhythmia is a physiological condition, especially well expressed in young people. During inspiration the stretch receptors of the lungs and the activity of the respiratory center lower the tone of the vagal centers; in addition, venous return increases and the Bainbridge reflex is triggered — HR increases. During expiration vagal tone is restored and HR decreases. The rhythm comes from the sinus node (the P waves are identical) — it is not a pathology.
s3444 A nurse mistakenly gave a rapid intravenous injection of potassium chloride solution to a patient with kidney failure. The patient developed bradycardia, followed by cardiac arrest.
s3445 Explain the mechanism of the effect of potassium on the heart. In which phase does the heart stop?
s3446 When blood K^+^ rises sharply, the resting potential of the cardiomyocyte membrane decreases (depolarization) and the fast Na^+^ channels are inactivated — excitability and conduction decrease and action potentials can no longer be generated. Excess K^+^ also slows diastolic depolarization in the sinus node. As a result, bradycardia, conduction blocks and cardiac arrest in diastole develop. That is why potassium preparations are given intravenously only slowly, diluted and under ECG monitoring.

s3484 The criteria for arterial hypertension differ between international guidelines. According to WHO and European guidelines, an office blood pressure of **140/90 mm Hg or higher** is arterial hypertension; 120–139/80–89 is high-normal or elevated pressure. In the American (ACC/AHA, 2017) guidelines the threshold for hypertension is 130/80. Arterial hypotension is below 90/60.
s3485 By the speed of regulation of arterial pressure there are three groups of mechanisms: **fast** (seconds — baroreceptor and chemoreceptor reflexes), **intermediate** (minutes–hours — the renin–angiotensin system, fluid shifts in capillaries, stress relaxation of vessels) and **long-term** (days — regulation of blood volume through the kidneys: aldosterone, vasopressin, natriuretic peptide, pressure natriuresis).

s3487 1. Basic laws of hemodynamics
s3488 The main cause of blood movement in the vessels is the **pressure difference** between the initial (aorta) and final (venae cavae) parts of the vascular system. Blood flow (Q) is directly proportional to the pressure difference (ΔP) and inversely proportional to vascular resistance (R): **Q = ΔP / R**.
s3489 According to **Poiseuille's law**, resistance R = 8ηl / πr4 — it is directly proportional to viscosity (η) and vessel length (l) and inversely proportional to **the fourth power of the radius**. Therefore, if the radius of a vessel decreases 2 times, the resistance increases 16 times. By changing the radius of its vessels the body regulates blood flow very effectively.
s3490 **Continuity of flow:** the same volume of blood passes through every cross-section of the vascular system per unit time. The **linear velocity** is inversely proportional to the **total cross-sectional area** of the vessels: 50 cm/s in the aorta, 0.05 cm/s in the capillaries (total cross-section 500–800 times larger), and it rises again in the veins (20–25 cm/s in the venae cavae). The circulation time (one complete circuit) is ≈ 20–23 s.

s3544 3. The arterial pulse
s3545 The **arterial pulse** is the rhythmic oscillation of the arterial walls caused by the ejection of blood into the aorta during cardiac systole. The pulse wave travels much faster than the blood itself: 4–6 m/s in the aorta, 8–12 m/s in the peripheral arteries (the velocity increases when the wall stiffens).
s3546 The **sphygmogram** is a graphic recording of the pulse. It shows the **anacrotic limb** (the ascending part — ejection of blood), the **catacrotic limb** (the descending part) and on it the **dicrotic wave** (closure of the aortic valves and the rebound of blood from them; preceded by the incisura).

s3570 4. Microcirculation
s3571 The **microcirculatory bed** is a functional unit consisting of arterioles, metarterioles, precapillary sphincters, capillaries, venules and arteriovenous anastomoses. At rest only 25–35% of capillaries are open; in a working organ their number increases.
s3572 The **capillary** wall consists of a single layer of endothelium; its length is 0.5–1 mm, its diameter 5–10 µm, and blood stays in it for 1– 2 s. Types of capillaries: **somatic** (continuous — muscle, skin, lung; in the CNS the blood–brain barrier), **visceral** (fenestrated — renal glomeruli, intestine, endocrine glands) and **sinusoidal** (discontinuous — liver, spleen, bone marrow).
s3573 **Mechanisms of exchange of substances:** diffusion (the main one — O~2~, CO~2~, glucose), filtration and reabsorption (water and substances dissolved in it), micropinocytosis (large molecules).
s3574 Starling equilibrium
s3575 Fluid movement across the capillary wall is determined by the balance of four forces: the **hydrostatic pressure** in the capillary (pushes fluid out), the **oncotic pressure** of plasma (holds fluid in), and the hydrostatic and oncotic pressures of tissue fluid.
s3576 Figure 13.5
s3577 Filtration and reabsorption in a capillary (Starling equilibrium)
s3578 At the arterial end of the capillary the hydrostatic pressure exceeds the oncotic pressure — **filtration**; at the venous end the hydrostatic pressure has fallen — **reabsorption**. Filtration slightly exceeds reabsorption; the excess fluid and proteins return to the blood through the **lymphatic vessels** (2–4 l of lymph per day).
s3579 Edema develops when the Starling equilibrium is disturbed: **increased hydrostatic pressure** (heart failure, venous thrombosis), **decreased oncotic pressure** (hypoalbuminemia — liver cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, starvation), **increased capillary permeability** (inflammation, allergy — histamine) and **impaired lymph flow** (lymphostasis).
s3580 5. Blood flow in the veins
s3581 Pressure in the veins is low (15–20 mm Hg in venules, 0–5 mm Hg in the venae cavae, negative during inspiration). Factors that ensure venous return:
s3582 **The residual force of the heart** (vis a tergo — the force pushing from behind) — the main factor.
s3583 **The suction effect of the chest** — during inspiration the negative pressure in the thoracic cavity increases, dilating the venae cavae and drawing in blood.
s3584 **Movement of the diaphragm** — during inspiration intra-abdominal pressure rises and squeezes blood out of the abdominal veins.
s3585 **The skeletal muscle “pump”** — when muscles contract the veins are compressed, and the valves allow blood to flow only toward the heart.
s3586 **Venous valves** (especially in the leg veins).
s3587 **The suction effect of the heart** — during ventricular systole the downward displacement of the AV boundary dilates the atria.
s3588 **Pulsation of the arteries** — compresses the adjacent veins.
s3589 During prolonged standing and immobility blood pools in the leg veins; with valve insufficiency **varicose disease** develops. During prolonged bed rest and travel venous flow slows and the risk of **deep vein thrombosis** increases — therefore early mobilization, leg exercises and compression stockings are recommended.
s3590 6. Regulation of vascular tone
s3591 **Vascular tone** is the constant state of partial contraction of vascular smooth muscle. It consists of two parts:
s3592 **Basal (myogenic) tone** — independent of innervation; the automaticity of smooth muscle and its response to stretch (the **Bayliss effect**: when pressure rises and the vessel is stretched, it contracts). This is the basis of **autoregulation**: in the kidney, brain and heart blood flow is kept constant even when pressure changes within 60–160 mm Hg.
s3593 **Neurohumoral tone** — under the influence of autonomic nerves and humoral factors.
s3594 Local (metabolic) regulation
s3595 In working tissue a decrease in O~2~, an increase in CO~2~, H^+^, lactate, adenosine and K^+^, and a rise in temperature dilate the arterioles — **working (functional) hyperemia**. The endothelium produces **nitric oxide (NO)**, prostacyclin (vasodilators) and **endothelin** (a powerful vasoconstrictor). NO is released continuously under the action of the shear stress of blood flow.
s3596 Nervous regulation
s3597 **Vasoconstrictors ** — mainly **sympathetic ** adrenergic fibers (noradrenaline, α~1~- adrenergic receptors). They are tonically active: at rest they send impulses at 1–3 Hz. If the stimulation frequency increases, the vessels constrict; if it decreases, they dilate.
s3598 **Vasodilators** — parasympathetic fibers (in the tongue, salivary glands, genital organs — acetylcholine, NO), sympathetic cholinergic fibers (in skeletal muscles), β~2~-adrenergic receptors (in the vessels of skeletal muscle, heart and liver under the action of adrenaline), the axon reflex. The **vasomotor (circulatory) center** is in the **medulla oblongata** (F.V. Ovsyannikov, 1871): it has **pressor ** (ventrolateral, exciting sympathetic neurons) and **depressor** parts. It is constantly tonically active; after transection above the medulla oblongata the pressure hardly changes, after transection below it the pressure falls sharply. Higher centers — the hypothalamus, limbic system and cerebral cortex (changes in pressure during emotions).
s3599 Reflex regulation of pressure
s3600 **Depressor reflexes** — from the **baroreceptors** of the aortic arch and carotid sinus (topic 12): when pressure rises, sympathetic tone decreases and the vagus is activated — pressure falls. The baroreceptors provide short-term (“second-to-second”) regulation.
s3601 **Pressor reflexes** — from the carotid and aortic **chemoreceptors** (hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis) — pressure rises.
