s6534 Spinal shock
s6535 **Spinal shock** is the temporary loss of all reflexes below the level of transection (or injury) of the spinal cord. Its cause is the sudden interruption of the activating influence of the higher centers (brainstem, cortex). Its duration depends on the evolutionary level of the animal: minutes in the frog, days in the dog, weeks in the monkey, and **weeks–months** in humans. Later the reflexes recover and even become exaggerated (hyperreflexia), while voluntary movements and sensation do not recover.
s6536 **Brown-Séquard syndrome** — in transverse damage to one half of the spinal cord: on the injured side — central paralysis (pyramidal tract) and loss of deep (proprioceptive) sensation (dorsal columns); on the opposite side — loss of pain and temperature sensation (the spinothalamic tract crosses in the spinal cord). It is a classic example for understanding where these pathways cross.
s6537 Exercise 1: determining the reflex time in a spinal frog (Türck method)
s6538 Suspend a spinal frog (with the brain removed) by the jaw on a stand; wait 5–10 minutes for spinal shock to pass.
s6539 Dip the tip of the hind foot into 0.5% sulfuric acid solution and measure the time until the leg is withdrawn with a metronome or stopwatch.
s6540 Wash the foot with water after each measurement.
s6541 Repeat the experiment with 0.3% and 1% acid. **Result and analysis:** as the acid concentration increases, the reflex time shortens — a strong stimulus excites more receptors, and summation in the center reaches threshold faster.
s6542 Exercise 2: the importance of an intact reflex arc
s6543 In a spinal frog make a circular cut in the skin of the leg and remove the skin from the foot (the receptors are removed) — there is no reflex on dipping into acid.
s6544 Cut the sciatic nerve in the other leg — the reflex disappears.
s6545 Destroy the spinal cord (with a probe) — all reflexes disappear. **Result and analysis:** when any part of the reflex arc (receptor, afferent pathway, center, efferent pathway) is damaged, the reflex is not carried out.
s6546 Exercise 3: Sechenov's experiment
s6547 Transect the frog brain in front of the optic thalami (removing the cerebral hemispheres) (a “thalamic frog”).
s6548 Determine the reflex time by the Türck method.
s6549 Place a NaCl crystal on the cut surface of the optic thalami and measure the reflex time again.
s6550 Remove the crystal, wash the surface with Ringer's solution and measure again. **Result and analysis:** when the NaCl crystal is applied, the reflex time is sharply prolonged or the reflex disappears; after washing it recovers. Excitation of neurons in the region of the optic thalami inhibits spinal reflexes — proof of **central inhibition**.
s6551 Exercise 4: examining tendon reflexes in humans
s6552 **Knee reflex**: the subject sits and crosses one leg over the other (or lets the legs hang freely); the tendon below the kneecap is struck with a neurological hammer — contraction of the quadriceps and extension of the lower leg.
s6553 **Achilles reflex**: the subject kneels on a chair with the feet hanging freely; the Achilles tendon is struck — plantar flexion of the foot.
s6554 **Biceps reflex**: the arm is half-flexed at the elbow; the examiner places a thumb on the biceps tendon and strikes it — flexion at the elbow.
s6555 **Triceps reflex**: the arm is flexed at the elbow at a right angle; the triceps tendon is struck above the olecranon — extension.
s6556 **Abdominal reflexes**: with the subject lying down, the abdominal skin is stroked quickly from the side toward the center (upper, middle, lower) — contraction of the abdominal muscles.
s6557 Compare the reflexes on the two sides (symmetry); **the Jendrassik maneuver** — the subject hooks the hands together and pulls, which enhances the knee reflex (distraction of attention and activation of γ- motoneurons). **Interpretation:** normally the reflexes are moderately brisk and symmetrical. Reduction or absence — damage to the reflex arc (peripheral nerve, root, segment); exaggeration (hyperreflexia, clonus) — loss of the inhibitory influence of the higher centers (upper motor neuron lesion).

s6650 After a motorcycle accident a patient has a complete spinal cord injury in the thoracic region (Th10). In the first weeks there were no movements, sensation or reflexes in the legs, and urinary retention was observed. After 2 months the knee and Achilles reflexes returned and even became exaggerated, but voluntary movements and sensation did not recover.
s6651 What is the condition in the first weeks called? Why did the reflexes later return and become exaggerated?
s6652 In the first weeks spinal shock developed — the spinal neurons below the injury were suddenly deprived of the constant activating impulses from the higher centers (brainstem, cortex), and their excitability fell sharply. Over time the excitability of the lower neurons recovers (increased receptor sensitivity, new synaptic connections — plasticity); the arcs of the knee (L~3~–L~4~) and Achilles (S~1~–S~2~) reflexes lie below the injury and are intact — the reflexes recover. Because the inhibitory influence of the higher centers is absent, they become exaggerated (hyperreflexia). Voluntary movement and sensation do not recover because the descending and ascending pathways needed for them have been severed.
s6653 After a stab wound a patient was found to have paralysis and loss of joint-position sensation in the right leg, and loss of pain and temperature sensation in the left leg.
s6654 Where and how is the spinal cord damaged? Explain the signs on the basis of the conducting pathways.
s6655 The right half of the spinal cord has been damaged transversely (Brown-Séquard syndrome). The corticospinal (pyramidal) tract crosses in the medulla oblongata and descends in the spinal cord on its own side — paralysis on the injured (right) side. The dorsal columns (Goll and Burdach), which conduct proprioceptive sensation, do not cross in the spinal cord — deep sensation is lost on the right side. Pain and temperature sensation are conducted by the spinothalamic tract, which crosses in the spinal cord at the level of the entering segment — therefore pain and temperature sensation are lost on the opposite (left) side.
s6656 During a neurological examination the patient's knee reflex was very weak on both sides. The doctor asked the patient to hook his hands together in front of him and pull hard, and the reflex became clearly visible.
s6657 What is this maneuver called? Explain its mechanism.
s6658 The Jendrassik maneuver. While pulling the hands hard, the patient's attention is distracted from the leg being tested (the voluntary inhibitory influence of the cortex decreases); in addition, excitation irradiates in the CNS, and spinal motoneurons and γ-motoneurons are activated — the sensitivity of the muscle spindles increases. As a result the knee reflex (a monosynaptic myotatic reflex) is enhanced. The maneuver helps to determine whether the reflex is truly reduced or only appeared weak because of the examination conditions.

s6698 1. General description of the brainstem
s6699 The **brainstem** comprises the medulla oblongata, the pons and the midbrain (some authors also include the diencephalon). It connects the spinal cord with the higher parts of the brain and performs the following functions:
s6700 **Conducting** — all ascending and descending pathways pass through it.
s6701 **Reflex** — the nuclei of cranial nerves III–XII are located here; centers of vital reflexes.
s6702 **Regulation of muscle tone and posture** — through the vestibular, reticular and red nucleus systems.
s6703 **Integrative** — regulation of the general activity of the CNS through the reticular formation (sleep– wakefulness).
