s2333 A patient with coronary heart disease takes 100 mg of aspirin daily. An elective tooth extraction has been scheduled. The dentist advised stopping aspirin 7 days beforehand.
s2334 How does aspirin act on platelets? Why should it be stopped a week before the operation?
s2335 Aspirin irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase in platelets and stops the formation of thromboxane A~2~ from arachidonic acid. Thromboxane A~2~ is a powerful inducer of platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction; without it the platelet plug forms slowly and the bleeding time is prolonged. Because platelets have no nucleus, they cannot synthesize new enzyme — the effect lasts for the whole life of each platelet (7–10 days). After aspirin is stopped, most platelets are renewed within 5–7 days and hemostasis is restored. (In patients at high cardiac risk, the decision to stop aspirin is made by a cardiologist.)

s2375 The classical scheme of “extrinsic and intrinsic pathways” is convenient for understanding laboratory tests, but in the body they do not work separately. s2432 3. Coagulation hemostasis (blood coagulation)
s2433 Blood coagulation is an enzymatic **cascade**: each activated factor activates the next, and the signal is amplified at every step. In the classical scheme blood coagulation proceeds in three stages:
s2434 **Formation of prothrombinase** (the longest stage, 5–7 minutes) — through the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways.
s2435 **Formation of thrombin** — under the action of prothrombinase, prothrombin (II) is converted into thrombin (IIa) (2–5 seconds).
s2436 **Formation of fibrin** — thrombin cleaves peptides from fibrinogen and the fibrin monomers polymerize; factor XIIIa links them covalently, turning them into insoluble fibrin (2–5 seconds).

s2453 Functions of thrombin
s2454 Thrombin is the central enzyme of hemostasis: it converts fibrinogen into fibrin; activates factors V, VIII, XI and XIII (positive feedback); strongly activates platelets; and, by binding to thrombomodulin on the endothelium, activates **protein C** (an anticoagulant effect — negative feedback).
s2455 After the clot has formed, it is compacted by the contraction of platelet thrombosthenin and serum is squeezed out of it (**retraction**). This draws the edges of the vessel together and makes the thrombus firmer.
s2456 **Hemophilia A** (factor VIII deficiency, 80–85%) and **hemophilia B** (factor IX deficiency) are X-linked recessive hereditary diseases; they occur mainly in boys, with mothers as carriers. Hemorrhages into the joints (hemarthrosis), hematomas in the muscles and prolonged bleeding after injury are typical. The APTT is prolonged, while the prothrombin time and bleeding time are normal. Treatment: concentrates of the missing factor.
