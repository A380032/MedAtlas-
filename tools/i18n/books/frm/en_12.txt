s2466 4. Peripheral muscle relaxants (curare-like agents)
s2467 **Peripheral muscle relaxants** block NM-cholinergic receptors at the neuromuscular junction of skeletal muscles and cause **muscle relaxation (paralysis)**. They do not act on the CNS — the patient does not lose consciousness and feels pain. Therefore muscle relaxants are used **only during general anesthesia** and with mechanical ventilation.
s2468 **History.** Curare is the arrow poison of South American Indians. In 1942, Griffith and Johnson first used tubocurarine during surgery; this was a revolution in anesthesiology.
s2469 **Order of muscle relaxation:** first the **facial and eye** muscles, then the **fingers**, limbs, neck and trunk muscles, the **intercostal muscles** and, last of all, the **diaphragm**. Recovery is in the reverse order.

s2472 4.1. Nondepolarizing (competitive) muscle relaxants
s2473 **Mechanism:** they block NM receptors **competitively** with ACh and do not cause depolarization themselves. No end-plate potential is generated. Increasing the ACh concentration in the synapse (anticholinesterase agents — **neostigmine**) reverses the block.

s2513 4.2. Depolarizing muscle relaxant — suxamethonium (Ditilin)
s2514 **Suxamethonium** — a compound of two ACh molecules. It **stimulates** NM receptors and causes **prolonged depolarization** of the end plate. First, brief **muscle fasciculations** (disorganized contractions) are seen, then the membrane remains depolarized and does not respond to new impulses — **paralysis** (phase I block). With prolonged or repeated administration, the block may become competitive in character (**phase II** block).
s2515 The action begins **very quickly** (**30–60 seconds**) and is **short** (5–10 minutes).
s2516 It is rapidly broken down in the blood by **pseudocholinesterase** (butyrylcholinesterase).
s2517 Uses: **rapid sequence intubation** (full stomach, emergency surgery), short procedures (laryngospasm, endoscopy, electrical cardioversion).

s2556 4.4. Other drugs affecting neuromuscular transmission
s2557 **Botulinum toxin A** — blocks ACh release from the presynaptic nerve ending (cleaves SNARE proteins). It is injected locally: blepharospasm, spasticity, dystonias, hyperhidrosis, overactive bladder, chronic migraine, cosmetology. Its effect lasts 3–6 months.
s2558 **Dantrolene** — blocks calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum inside skeletal muscle; for malignant hyperthermia and neuroleptic malignant syndrome.

s2660 A 28-year-old patient underwent general anesthesia for appendectomy; suxamethonium was used for intubation. The operation ended in 30 minutes, but 2 hours later spontaneous breathing had still not returned. The patient's uncle had also previously “not woken up for a long time” after anesthesia.
s2661 1) What is the probable cause? 2) Can neostigmine be given in this situation? 3) Management.
s2662 1) A hereditary atypical form (deficiency) of pseudocholinesterase — suxamethonium is not broken down and acts for a long time. 2) No — neostigmine also inhibits pseudocholinesterase and may enhance the depolarizing block. 3) Keep the patient on mechanical ventilation under sedation and wait until the block wears off on its own (several hours); if necessary, fresh frozen plasma (a source of pseudocholinesterase); test the patient and relatives (dibucaine number), avoid suxamethonium and mivacurium in the future, and make a note in the medical records.
