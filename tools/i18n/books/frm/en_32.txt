s6509 5.3. Paracetamol
s6510 **Paracetamol (acetaminophen)** is an analgesic and antipyretic; its anti-inflammatory effect is very weak. Its mechanism is not fully clear: it inhibits COX mainly in the CNS (in the periphery, the high peroxide levels in the inflammatory focus inactivate it) and acts on the serotonergic and cannabinoid systems. It has almost no effect on the gastric mucosa, platelets or kidneys — it is the **first-choice** analgesic-antipyretic **in children, pregnant women and the elderly** and in gastric diseases.
s6511 **Dose:** 500–1000 mg for adults, **no more than 4 g a day** (2–3 g with liver disease, alcoholism, starvation, low body weight); **10–15 mg/kg** for children, every 4–6 hours, no more than 60 mg/kg a day.
s6512 5.4. Metamizole (Analgin)
s6513 A strong analgesic and antipyretic, antispasmodic. Because of **agranulocytosis** (rare but potentially fatal), it is banned or restricted in many countries; it is widely used in CIS countries. Rapid intravenous injection causes hypotension and anaphylaxis.
s6514 6. Glucocorticoids
s6515 6.1. Mechanism
s6516 **Glucocorticoids** (cortisol and its synthetic analogs), being lipophilic molecules, enter the cell and bind to the cytoplasmic **glucocorticoid receptor**; the complex moves into the nucleus and changes gene transcription:
s6517 **transrepression** — suppresses the expression of inflammatory genes (via NF-kappaB, AP-1): cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-alpha), COX-2, iNOS and adhesion molecules decrease;
s6518 **transactivation** — increases the synthesis of anti-inflammatory proteins (**lipocortin-1 (annexin-1)**, which inhibits phospholipase A~2~ — as a result **both prostaglandins and leukotrienes** decrease); the metabolic adverse effects also arise mainly through this pathway.

s6727 A 19-year-old female student has type 1 diabetes. On the day of an exam she skipped breakfast but injected her usual insulin dose. Two hours later she developed tremor, heavy sweating and confusion, and then lost consciousness. The glucometer shows 1.9 mmol/l.
s6728 1) Condition? 2) Emergency care?
s6729 1) Severe hypoglycemia (hypoglycemic coma). 2) Do not pour anything into her mouth (aspiration); glucagon 1 mg intramuscularly or subcutaneously (or 3 mg intranasally) — bystanders can give it; a health worker — 40% glucose 40–60 ml as an intravenous push, then an infusion of 5–10% glucose; once conscious — a carbohydrate meal; analyze the cause and teach the patient not to inject insulin without eating; she should always carry a glucagon kit.
s6730 A 45-year-old woman has been taking prednisolone 15 mg/day for 6 months for rheumatoid arthritis. Because she felt better, she stopped the drug abruptly on her own. Three days later she developed severe weakness, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, BP 80/50 mm Hg, glucose 3.2 mmol/l, sodium 128 mmol/l.
s6731 1) Diagnosis? 2) Pathogenesis? 3) Treatment and prevention.
s6732 1) Acute adrenal insufficiency (glucocorticoid withdrawal syndrome, secondary). 2) Long-term exogenous glucocorticoid intake suppressed the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (ACTH secretion decreased, the adrenal cortex atrophied); when the drug was stopped, her own cortisol production was insufficient. 3) Hydrocortisone 100 mg intravenously immediately, then 200 mg/day as an infusion or every 6 hours; infusion of 0.9% NaCl and glucose; once stable, switch to oral prednisolone and reduce the dose gradually (over several weeks to months). Explain to the patient that glucocorticoids must not be stopped abruptly and that the dose must be increased in stressful situations; give her a “steroid card”.
