s6518 6. Glucocorticoids
s6519 6.1. Mechanism
s6520 **Glucocorticoids** (cortisol and its synthetic analogs), being lipophilic molecules, enter the cell and bind to the cytoplasmic **glucocorticoid receptor**; the complex moves into the nucleus and changes gene transcription:
s6521 **transrepression** — suppresses the expression of inflammatory genes (via NF-kappaB, AP-1): cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-alpha), COX-2, iNOS and adhesion molecules decrease;
s6522 **transactivation** — increases the synthesis of anti-inflammatory proteins (**lipocortin-1 (annexin-1)**, which inhibits phospholipase A2 — as a result **both prostaglandins and leukotrienes** decrease); the metabolic adverse effects also arise mainly through this pathway.

s6734 A 45-year-old woman has been taking prednisolone 15 mg/day for 6 months for rheumatoid arthritis. Because she felt better, she stopped the drug abruptly on her own. Three days later she developed severe weakness, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, BP 80/50 mm Hg, glucose 3.2 mmol/l, sodium 128 mmol/l.
s6735 1) Diagnosis? 2) Pathogenesis? 3) Treatment and prevention.
s6736 1) Acute adrenal insufficiency (glucocorticoid withdrawal syndrome, secondary). 2) Long-term exogenous glucocorticoid intake suppressed the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (ACTH secretion decreased, the adrenal cortex atrophied); when the drug was stopped, her own cortisol production was insufficient. 3) Hydrocortisone 100 mg intravenously immediately, then 200 mg/day as an infusion or every 6 hours; infusion of 0.9% NaCl and glucose; once stable, switch to oral prednisolone and reduce the dose gradually (over several weeks to months). Explain to the patient that glucocorticoids must not be stopped abruptly and that the dose must be increased in stressful situations; give her a “steroid card”.
