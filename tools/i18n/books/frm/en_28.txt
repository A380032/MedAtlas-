s5815 A 68-year-old man takes furosemide 80 mg/day, digoxin and enalapril for chronic heart failure and atrial fibrillation. In recent days he has felt weakness, muscle cramps, nausea and an irregular heartbeat. Potassium 2.9 mmol/l, magnesium low.
s5816 1) The cause of the electrolyte disturbance? 2) Why is it especially dangerous for this patient? 3) Correction.
s5817 1) The loop diuretic (furosemide) causes large losses of potassium and magnesium (increased Na^+^ delivery to the collecting duct, secondary hyperaldosteronism). 2) Hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia increase digoxin toxicity and the risk of ventricular arrhythmias. 3) Potassium and magnesium preparations (slowly intravenously if needed), adding spironolactone (it also reduces mortality in HF and retains potassium), reducing the furosemide dose to the lowest effective dose; monitoring of the digoxin level and ECG; recheck electrolytes in 1 week.
s5818 A 55-year-old woman takes hydrochlorothiazide 50 mg/day for AH. She has developed acute pain, redness and swelling in the first metatarsophalangeal joint of the big toe. Blood uric acid is raised. In addition, glucose is at a borderline level.
s5819 1) Are these conditions related to the diuretic? 2) How should the antihypertensive treatment be changed?
s5820 1) Thiazides (especially at high doses) cause hyperuricemia (competition for tubular secretion of uric acid and increased reabsorption with hypovolemia) and hyperglycemia — an attack of gout has developed. 2) Stop hydrochlorothiazide; for AH choose losartan (it has a uricosuric effect) and/or amlodipine; treat the gout attack (colchicine or an NSAID or a glucocorticoid); later, allopurinol if needed.
s5821 A 30-year-old mountaineer is planning to climb to an altitude of 4,500 m. On his previous trip he had headache, nausea and insomnia.
s5822 Which diuretic would you recommend for prevention, and by what mechanism?
s5823 Acetazolamide (125 mg 2 times a day, starting 1 day before the ascent and continuing for 2 days after reaching the highest point). Mechanism: by excreting bicarbonate it causes metabolic acidosis — this stimulates the respiratory center, increases ventilation and speeds up acclimatization (the hypoxic ventilatory response). Adverse effects: paresthesias, altered taste of carbonated drinks, frequent urination; contraindicated in sulfonamide allergy. The main prevention is a gradual ascent.

s5849 Thromboses are the main cause of myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. Antithrombotic drugs (antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, fibrinolytics) save millions of lives, but they are also among the drugs that cause the most serious adverse effects (bleeding). Anemia — especially iron deficiency anemia — is also widespread among women and children in Uzbekistan. This topic covers drugs that affect blood coagulation, hemostasis and hematopoiesis.

s5858 **Vitamin K-dependent clotting factors** — **II, VII, IX, X** (and proteins C and S). To remember them: **“1972”** — 10, 9, 7, 2. Warfarin disrupts the synthesis of exactly these factors.
s5859 Arterial and venous thrombi: in arteries (high velocity) — a **platelet-rich “white” thrombus** → **antiplatelet agents** (prevention of infarction and stroke); in veins (slow flow) — a **fibrin- and red cell-rich “red” thrombus** → **anticoagulants** (deep vein thrombosis, PE, atrial fibrillation).
s5860 **The antiplatelet dose of aspirin is 75–100 mg/day** (low dose). At high doses (> 300 mg), prostacyclin synthesis in the endothelium is also inhibited and gastrointestinal adverse effects rise sharply, while the antiplatelet effect does not increase. In acute coronary syndrome and stroke, a **loading dose** of 150–300 mg is chewed.
s5861 **The antidote to heparin is protamine sulfate**: a strongly basic protein that binds heparin directly and inactivates it (chemical antagonism). **1 mg of protamine neutralizes 100 units of heparin** (the amount of heparin given in the last 2–3 hours is taken into account). It neutralizes LMWH only partly (≈ 60%).
s5862 **Warfarin has very many interactions** (narrow therapeutic range, metabolism via CYP2C9 and CYP3A4, 99% protein binding): **enhance its effect (bleeding risk)**: amiodarone, fluconazole and other azoles, metronidazole, many antibiotics (co-trimoxazole, fluoroquinolones, macrolides — they also reduce the intestinal flora), NSAIDs and aspirin (pharmacodynamic), SSRIs, acute alcohol intake, liver diseases; **reduce its effect**: rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, St John's wort, foods rich in vitamin K (spinach, cabbage, broccoli, green tea — intake should be kept stable). Whenever a new drug is prescribed, the INR should be checked frequently.
s5863 **Absolute contraindications to thrombolysis**: a history of intracerebral hemorrhage at any time; ischemic stroke in the last 3–6 months (other than the acute stroke); a brain tumor or vascular malformation; active internal bleeding (except menstruation); suspected aortic dissection; severe trauma, head injury or major surgery in the last month; uncontrolled AH (> 185/110 mm Hg). The main complication is **bleeding, especially intracerebral hemorrhage**; antidote — tranexamic acid, cryoprecipitate (fibrinogen).
s5864 **Acute iron poisoning** is one of the common causes of fatal poisoning in children (sweet tablets!): vomiting (with blood), diarrhea, then a “false improvement”, followed by shock, metabolic acidosis and liver failure. **Antidote — deferoxamine** (intravenously). Activated charcoal does not bind iron.
s5865 In B~12~ deficiency, giving **folic acid** alone improves the anemia but **worsens the neurological disorders**. In megaloblastic anemia, the B~12~ level should be measured first, or both should be given together.
s5866 1. The hemostatic system
s5867 **Hemostasis** is the system that stops bleeding when a vessel is damaged and keeps the blood fluid. It consists of three stages:
s5868 **Vascular-platelet hemostasis.** Collagen and von Willebrand factor under the damaged endothelium cause platelet **adhesion**. Activated platelets release **thromboxane A~2~** (TXA~2~), **ADP** and serotonin and recruit other platelets. The final common pathway is activation of the **GP IIb/IIIa** receptors on the platelet membrane, which link platelets to each other through fibrinogen (**aggregation**) — a white thrombus forms.
s5869 **Coagulation hemostasis.** The cascade of plasma clotting factors: tissue factor (with factor VII) → **factor Xa** → prothrombin (II) → **thrombin** (IIa) → fibrinogen → **fibrin** → factor XIIIa stabilizes the fibrin — a red thrombus.
s5870 **Fibrinolysis.** Under the influence of tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA), plasminogen is converted into **plasmin**, which breaks down fibrin.

s5913 2.1. Acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin)
s5914 **Mechanism.** It **irreversibly acetylates** **cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1)** in platelets → TXA~2~ synthesis stops. Platelets have no nucleus and cannot synthesize new enzyme, so the effect lasts for **the whole life of the platelet** (7–10 days). Endothelial cells, in contrast, resynthesize COX and restore prostacyclin production.
s5915 **Uses:** acute coronary syndrome, stable IHD, ischemic stroke and TIA, peripheral artery disease — **secondary prevention**; after coronary stenting (with a P2Y~12~ blocker — dual antiplatelet therapy); prevention of preeclampsia (in high-risk pregnant women). In **primary prevention** (in people without cardiovascular disease), the risk of bleeding may outweigh the benefit — it is now **not routinely recommended**.
s5916 **Adverse effects:** gastrointestinal erosions and ulcers, **bleeding**, bronchospasm (**“aspirin-induced asthma”** — NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease), **Reye syndrome** in children (during a viral infection — liver and brain damage; contraindicated for fever under 16 years).
