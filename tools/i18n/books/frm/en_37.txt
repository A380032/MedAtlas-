s7493 A 22-year-old student presents with fever (38.8 °C) for 2 days, severe sore throat and difficulty swallowing. No cough. The tonsils are enlarged with purulent exudate, and the anterior cervical lymph nodes are enlarged and tender. The rapid strep test is positive. At age 10 she had a “rash” after amoxicillin (no urticaria or swelling).
s7494 1) Diagnosis? 2) Is an antibiotic needed, and which one? 3) How do you assess the “allergy” history?
s7495 1) Acute streptococcal (group A) tonsillitis (Centor score 4, positive test). 2) Yes — to prevent complications (rheumatic fever, peritonsillar abscess) and to shorten the duration of symptoms. A mild childhood rash (without urticaria or anaphylaxis) is low-risk — probably a viral rash; it can be clarified with an allergist. Cephalexin (a 1st-generation cephalosporin) with caution, or phenoxymethylpenicillin for 10 days after allergy has been excluded; if the patient or doctor is worried — azithromycin for 5 days or clindamycin. 3) A detailed history: the type of reaction, its timing, its treatment; for low-risk rashes, allergy testing (a drug challenge) to remove the “allergy” label.
s7496 A 65-year-old patient received clarithromycin for community-acquired pneumonia. He takes simvastatin 40 mg, amlodipine and warfarin. After 5 days he developed severe muscle pain and weakness, dark urine and nosebleeds. CK is markedly raised, INR 5.8.
s7497 1) The cause of these conditions? 2) Management, and which antibiotic would have been preferable?
s7498 1) Clarithromycin is a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor: the simvastatin concentration rose and rhabdomyolysis developed (muscle pain, myoglobinuria, raised CK); warfarin metabolism slowed and the INR rose — bleeding. The amlodipine concentration may also rise. 2) Stop clarithromycin and simvastatin, stop warfarin temporarily and give vitamin K (INR 4.5–10 with minor bleeding); in rhabdomyolysis — plenty of intravenous fluids, monitoring of creatinine and potassium. Replace the antibiotic with amoxicillin (± azithromycin — it hardly affects CYP3A4). Whenever an antibiotic is prescribed, interactions with the patient's other drugs must be checked.
s7499 A 70-year-old woman received ceftriaxone and levofloxacin in hospital for pneumonia. On day 8 of treatment she developed watery diarrhea 8–10 times a day, abdominal pain, fever of 38.5 °C and leukocytosis of 18 × 10^9^/l. Stool C. difficile toxin is positive.

s7501 1) Clostridioides difficile infection (antibiotic-associated colitis), severe form (white cells > 15 × 10^9^/l is a severity criterion; creatinine is also monitored). 2) Stop other antibiotics where possible; oral vancomycin 125 mg 4 times a day for 10 days or fidaxomicin; fluid and electrolyte replacement; contact isolation, handwashing with soap (alcohol does not act on spores); stop the PPI if it is not needed. 3) Loperamide and other antiperistaltic agents — risk of toxic megacolon; intravenous vancomycin (it does not reach the intestine).

s7554 Antituberculosis drugs. Antiviral drugs
s7555 **Tuberculosis** ranks among the leading causes of death from infectious diseases; **drug-resistant tuberculosis** is a serious problem in Uzbekistan and the Central Asian region. Treating tuberculosis requires a long-term combination of several drugs and strict adherence by the patient. **Antiviral drugs** have changed radically in recent decades: HIV infection has become a chronic, manageable disease, and hepatitis C is cured completely with 8–12 weeks of tablets. At the same time, many “immunomodulators” and “antivirals” are widely sold without proven effectiveness — a GP must be able to tell evidence-based treatment apart.

s7564 **The basic principles of tuberculosis treatment**: 1. **Combination therapy** — at least 3–4 drugs: with monotherapy, **resistance** develops quickly. 2. **Long-term treatment** — to eliminate persistent forms (in drug-susceptible tuberculosis — at least 4–6 months). 3. **Two phases**: **intensive** (2 months — rapid reduction of the number of mycobacteria) and **continuation** (4 months — elimination of the rest). 4. **Directly observed therapy (DOT)** — a health worker watches every dose being taken (or video observation). 5. **Drug susceptibility testing** before treatment (rapid molecular tests — Xpert MTB/RIF detects rifampicin resistance in 2 hours).
s7565 Older textbooks divided antituberculosis drugs into “first-line” and “second-line (reserve)”, and streptomycin and kanamycin were widely used. Current WHO recommendations do not recommend injectable drugs (kanamycin, capreomycin) for drug-resistant tuberculosis because of **ototoxicity** and low effectiveness; **oral regimens** (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, moxifloxacin) are used instead.
s7566 The first-line drugs — **“RIPE”**: **R**ifampicin, **I**soniazid, **P**yrazinamide, **E**thambutol. Their adverse effects: **R** — **R**ed urine and **R**aised (induced) enzymes; **I** — **I**nflamed nerves (neuropathy) and liver; **P** — **P**odagra (gout) and liver; **E** — **E**yes. Three drugs (H, R, Z) are hepatotoxic, while ethambutol is harmless to the liver.
s7567 **Rifampicin has very many interactions**: it sharply **reduces** the concentrations of **oral contraceptives** (unexpected pregnancy — nonhormonal contraception is needed), warfarin, DOACs, glucocorticoids, sulfonylureas, antiretroviral drugs (protease inhibitors, most integrase inhibitors), azole antifungals, methadone, cyclosporine, tacrolimus, digoxin, beta-blockers and calcium antagonists. In tuberculosis with HIV, **rifabutin** (a weak inducer) is sometimes used instead of rifampicin.
s7568 Stopping treatment on one's own or taking drugs incorrectly is the main cause of **drug-resistant tuberculosis**. A patient infected with a resistant strain transmits it to others. That is why explaining to the patient the need for the full course of treatment and ensuring DOT are of crucial importance.
s7569 The effectiveness of **umifenovir (Arbidol), Ingavirin, Kagocel, oxolinic ointment, tilorone** and similar “antiviral” and “immunomodulatory” preparations widely sold in CIS countries **has not been proven in quality randomized trials** for influenza and ARVI; they are not included in WHO or international clinical recommendations. For most ARVIs (rhinovirus, adenovirus) there is no specific antiviral treatment — symptomatic treatment is enough.
s7570 **The main adverse effect of acyclovir** is **crystalluria and acute kidney injury** with large intravenous doses (prevention — adequate hydration, slow infusion, dosing according to renal function); rarely neurotoxicity (in the elderly and in renal failure). Herpesviruses **are never completely eliminated** from the body — the drugs only stop replication and do not act on latent infection.
s7571 Hepatitis C used to be treated with pegylated interferon and ribavirin for 24–48 weeks — low effectiveness and severe adverse effects (flu-like syndrome, depression, cytopenias, hemolytic anemia). Now **interferon-free DAA regimens** are the standard; the WHO aims to eliminate hepatitis C by 2030.
s7572 **Principles of antiretroviral therapy (ART)**: ART is started **as soon as possible in every patient diagnosed with HIV, regardless of the CD4 count**. The standard first-line regimen (WHO) is **tenofovir + lamivudine (or emtricitabine) + dolutegravir** (TLD, in a single tablet, once a day). The goal is to suppress the viral load to an undetectable level: **“Undetectable = Untransmittable”** (U = U). ART is taken for life; adherence prevents resistance.
s7573 **Intranasal and rectal interferon preparations** and **interferon inducers** (tilorone, Kagocel, Cycloferon) are widely used in CIS countries to treat and prevent ARVI and influenza, but their effectiveness has not been confirmed in studies that meet the standards of modern evidence-based medicine.

s7575 1.1. Features of mycobacteria and principles of treatment
s7576 Mycobacterium tuberculosis is a slowly multiplying bacterium (division time 18–24 hours) whose cell wall is rich in **mycolic acids** (acid-fast, impermeable to many drugs) and which can live inside macrophages. The disease focus contains different populations: rapidly multiplying (in cavities), slowly multiplying (in an acidic environment, inside macrophages) and “dormant” (persistent) forms.
