s1426 3.4. The dose–effect relationship
s1427 Figure 3.7
s1428 The dose–effect relationship: a — potency (EC50) and efficacy (Emax), b — therapeutic index (compiler's diagram)
s1429 **Potency** — the smaller the dose that produces the desired effect, the more potent the drug. Its measure is **EC50** (the concentration that produces 50% of the maximal effect).
s1430 **Efficacy** — the **maximal** effect a drug can produce (**Emax**). Clinically, efficacy is more important than potency.
s1431 **Therapeutic index (TI)** = TD50 / ED50 (in animals LD50 / ED50). Drugs with a small TI (**“with a narrow therapeutic range”**): digoxin, lithium, theophylline, warfarin, aminoglycosides, phenytoin, carbamazepine — their blood concentrations are monitored.

s1459 3.6. Drug interactions
s1460 **Pharmaceutical** — outside the body, in one syringe or solution (precipitate, inactivation).
s1461 **Pharmacokinetic** — absorption (antacids reduce the absorption of tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones), distribution (displacement from proteins), metabolism (induction, inhibition), excretion.
s1462 **Pharmacodynamic** — **synergism** (additive — the sum; potentiation — more than the sum) and **antagonism**.
s1463 Examples of dangerous interactions: MAO inhibitors + tyramine-containing foods (cheese) → hypertensive crisis; SSRIs + tramadol or MAO inhibitors → **serotonin syndrome**; sildenafil + nitrates → severe hypotension; warfarin + NSAIDs → bleeding; ACE inhibitors + potassium-sparing diuretics → hyperkalemia.
s1464 3.7. Factors affecting drug action
s1465 **Age.** In newborns, liver enzymes (glucuronosyltransferase) are immature — chloramphenicol (Levomycetin) causes the “gray baby syndrome”; the BBB is permeable; renal function is low. In the elderly, renal and hepatic clearance decrease, adipose tissue increases (the Vd of lipophilic drugs rises), and CNS sensitivity increases.
s1466 **Pregnancy and lactation.** Teratogenic drugs: thalidomide, isotretinoin, warfarin, valproate, ACE inhibitors, tetracyclines, methotrexate.
s1467 **Pharmacogenetics.** Slow and fast **acetylators** (isoniazid, hydralazine, procainamide); **G6PD deficiency** (primaquine, sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin → hemolysis); the atypical form of **pseudocholinesterase** (suxamethonium → prolonged apnea); CYP2D6 ultrarapid metabolizers (codeine → much more morphine is formed — respiratory arrest in children).
s1468 **Diseases** (renal, hepatic, heart failure), diet, circadian rhythms, the placebo effect.
s1469 3.8. Adverse effects
s1470 **Type A** (dose-related, predictable) — an exaggeration of the pharmacological effect: insulin — hypoglycemia, anticoagulant — bleeding.
s1471 **Type B** (not dose-related, unexpected) — **allergic reactions** (anaphylaxis, Stevens–Johnson syndrome) and **idiosyncrasy** (an unusual response associated with a hereditary enzyme defect).
s1472 **Toxic effects** — nephrotoxicity (aminoglycosides), ototoxicity (aminoglycosides, furosemide), hepatotoxicity (paracetamol, isoniazid), cardiotoxicity (doxorubicin), myelotoxicity (cytostatics, chloramphenicol).
s1473 **Teratogenic, embryotoxic, fetotoxic, mutagenic and carcinogenic** effects.
s1474 **Dysbiosis, superinfection** — with broad-spectrum antibiotics.
s1475 3.9. General principles of treating acute drug poisoning
s1476 **Stopping absorption:** gastric lavage (within 1 hour, in a conscious patient, in the absence of corrosive substances), **activated charcoal** (1 g/kg), washing the skin and eyes.
s1477 **Speeding up the elimination of absorbed poison:** forced diuresis, alkalinization of the urine (salicylates, phenobarbital), hemodialysis (methanol, ethylene glycol, lithium, salicylates), hemosorption.
s1478 **Antidote therapy** (specific neutralization).
s1479 **Symptomatic support of vital functions:** airway, breathing, circulation, stopping seizures.

s1538 4.1. Local anesthetics
s1539 **Local anesthetics** — drugs that **reversibly** abolish pain sensation (and later other sensations) at the site of application. They do not cause loss of consciousness.
s1540 **History.** In 1879, the Russian scientist **V.K. Anrep** discovered the local anesthetic effect of cocaine; in 1884, K. Koller introduced it into ophthalmology. In 1905, A. Einhorn synthesized **procaine (Novocain)**, which is less toxic than cocaine; in 1943, N. Löfgren created the first amide anesthetic — **lidocaine**.
s1541 **Chemical structure.** The molecule consists of three parts: **a lipophilic aromatic ring** — **an intermediate chain (ester or amide bond)** — **a hydrophilic amine group**. Two groups are distinguished according to the intermediate bond.

s1560 **Mechanism of action.** Local anesthetics block the **voltage-gated sodium channels** of the nerve fiber membrane from the inside. Sodium ions stop entering the cell, no action potential is generated, and impulse conduction is blocked.
s1561 Figure 3.10
s1562 Effect of anesthetics on conduction of excitation along a nerve fiber: A — normal Na^+^ entry; B — anesthetic molecules block the sodium channels (teaching material of the TMA Department of Pharmacology)
s1563 Important details of the mechanism:
s1564 The anesthetic crosses the membrane **in the non-ionized (base) form**, and inside the cell it blocks the channel **in the ionized (cationic) form**.
s1565 In inflamed tissue (pus), the medium is **acidic**, the anesthetic is more ionized and crosses the membrane poorly — anesthesia is **weak**.
s1566 Channels are blocked better in the **open and inactivated state** (**“use-dependent block”**) — pain fibers that conduct impulses frequently are blocked first.
s1567 **Sensitivity of fibers:** thin unmyelinated C fibers and A-delta fibers (pain, temperature) are blocked first, then touch and pressure, and **motor** (thick A-alpha) fibers last. Order of loss of sensations: **pain → temperature → touch → deep pressure → motor function**.
s1568 **Adding a vasoconstrictor.** If **epinephrine** (adrenaline, usually 1:100,000 – 1:200,000) is added to a local anesthetic solution: 1) the blood vessels constrict, the anesthetic is absorbed slowly — the effect is **prolonged**; 2) less enters the blood — **systemic toxicity decreases**, and the maximum dose is increased; 3) bleeding decreases. However, epinephrine-containing solutions are **not used** for anesthesia of the **fingers, auricle, tip of the nose and penis** (end arteries — risk of necrosis), in severe heart disease, thyrotoxicosis and in patients taking MAO inhibitors.
