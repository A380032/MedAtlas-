s4908 7.3. Hypertensive crisis
s4909 **Hypertensive crisis** is a sharp rise in BP (usually > 180/120 mm Hg). Two types are distinguished:
s4910 **complicated (emergency) crisis** — with acute target-organ damage: hypertensive encephalopathy, stroke, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary edema, aortic dissection, eclampsia, acute kidney injury. BP is lowered **stepwise** with **intravenous** drugs (in the first hour mean BP by no more than 25%; in aortic dissection — rapidly, to systolic < 120);
s4911 **uncomplicated crisis** — without organ damage; BP is lowered gradually over 24–48 hours with **oral** drugs.

s5088 1) Management? 2) Which drugs can she be switched to?
s5089 1) Stop enalapril immediately — ACE inhibitors (especially in the 2nd–3rd trimesters) impair fetal kidney development and may lead to oligohydramnios, skull hypoplasia and fetal death. s5137 Drugs acting on the digestive system
s5138 Gastrointestinal diseases are among the most common reasons for visiting a GP: dyspepsia, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), gastric and duodenal ulcer, constipation, diarrhea, nausea and vomiting, biliary tract and liver diseases. Proton pump inhibitors are among the most prescribed drugs in the world, but they are often used without reason and for too long. s5147 PPIs block only **actively working** pumps. Therefore they are taken **30–60 minutes before meals** (usually before breakfast) — when food activates the pumps, the drug is at its peak blood concentration. The full effect develops in 3–5 days; a PPI is not a quick “switch-off” (symptomatic, as-needed) drug.
s5148 Antacids reduce the absorption of many drugs (**tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones**, iron preparations, digoxin, levothyroxine, bisphosphonates). Other drugs are taken **at least 2 hours before or 4 hours after** an antacid.
s5149 The effectiveness of eradication is checked **at least 4 weeks** after it (and 2 weeks after stopping the PPI) with a **urea breath test** or a stool antigen test. A serological test (antibodies) is unsuitable for assessing the result of eradication — antibodies persist for months.
s5150 The drug according to the cause of vomiting: **“Chemo — Ondansetron, Sea — Dimenhydrinate and scopolamine, Slow stomach — Metoclopramide/domperidone, Delayed chemo — Aprepitant”**.
s5151 Laxatives are **contraindicated** in **intestinal obstruction**, an acute abdomen (suspected appendicitis), and abdominal pain of unknown cause. In treating constipation, diet (fiber), fluids and physical activity come first; of the drugs — bulk-forming and osmotic laxatives. Regular long-term use of contact (stimulant) laxatives is not recommended.
s5152 Choleretic drugs (especially cholekinetics) are contraindicated in **gallstone disease** — a stone may become lodged in the bile duct and lead to obstructive jaundice and pancreatitis. They are also not used in acute cholecystitis, acute hepatitis and pancreatitis. The effectiveness of most herbal choleretics has not been proven in modern studies.
