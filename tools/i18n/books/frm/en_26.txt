s5358 5.2. Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA)
s5359 **UDCA** is a hydrophilic bile acid. Mechanism: it reduces cholesterol saturation of bile (**dissolves cholesterol stones**), displaces toxic hydrophobic bile acids and protects hepatocytes and cholangiocytes, choleresis and immunomodulation.
s5360 **Uses: primary biliary cholangitis** (the main drug), small (< 15 mm) radiolucent cholesterol stones (with preserved gallbladder function), intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, cholestatic liver diseases, prevention of stone formation after bariatric surgery.
s5361 5.3. Hepatoprotectors
s5362 Hepatoprotectors are a heterogeneous group intended to protect liver cells from harmful influences: **ademetionine** (Heptral), **silymarin** (milk thistle — Karsil), **essential phospholipids** (Essentiale), ornithine aspartate (Hepa-Merz — reduces ammonia in hepatic encephalopathy).
s5363 6. Drugs acting on the pancreas and the intestinal microflora
s5364 **Pancreatin** — a mixture of **lipase, amylase and protease** obtained from the porcine pancreas. Uses: **exocrine pancreatic insufficiency** (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, after pancreatectomy). The dose is set in **lipase units** (25,000–50,000 units per main meal) and taken **with food**. Enteric-coated microsphere capsules (Creon) are preferred. In cystic fibrosis, very large doses may cause fibrosing colonopathy.
s5365 **Probiotics** are live microorganisms that confer a benefit when taken in adequate amounts (lactobacilli, bifidobacteria, Saccharomyces boulardii). **Prebiotics** are nondigestible substances that stimulate the growth of beneficial microflora (inulin, lactulose, oligosaccharides). Their effectiveness depends on the strain; proven indications are limited (antibiotic-associated diarrhea, acute gastroenteritis in children).

s5577 Diuretics
s5578 Diuretics are among the most commonly used drugs in cardiology, nephrology and intensive care. They relieve edema and pulmonary congestion in heart failure, reduce complications in arterial hypertension, and are used to treat ascites in liver cirrhosis, cerebral edema and glaucoma. At the same time, diuretics significantly change the electrolyte balance: hypokalemia can lead to glycoside toxicity and arrhythmias, and hyperkalemia to cardiac arrest. Knowing which part of the nephron each diuretic acts on is the key to understanding its effectiveness and adverse effects logically.

s5586 The **potency** of a diuretic depends on how much sodium is reabsorbed in the segment it acts on: loop diuretics (25%) — **strong**, thiazides (5–8%) — **moderate**, potassium-sparing agents (2–3%) — **weak**. Agents acting on the proximal tubule are also weak, because the later segments “catch” the sodium.
s5587 Giving furosemide intravenously **slowly** (at no more than 4 mg per minute) reduces the risk of ototoxicity. Giving it together with aminoglycosides, vancomycin or cisplatin increases **ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity**. NSAIDs reduce the effect of loop diuretics.
s5588 The “opposite relationship” with calcium: **“Loops Lose calcium, Thiazides Take it back”**. That is why loop diuretics are useful in hypercalcemia, and thiazides in calcium kidney stones and a tendency to osteoporosis.
s5589 **Risk of hyperkalemia**: potassium-sparing diuretics must be used with caution or not at all together with **potassium preparations, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, NSAIDs, trimethoprim and heparin**, and **in renal failure** (GFR < 30). If potassium exceeds 5.5 mmol/l — risk of cardiac arrhythmias and arrest. Regular monitoring of blood potassium and creatinine is mandatory.
