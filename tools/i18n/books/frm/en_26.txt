s5364 6. Drugs acting on the pancreas and the intestinal microflora
s5365 **Pancreatin** — a mixture of **lipase, amylase and protease** obtained from the porcine pancreas. Uses: **exocrine pancreatic insufficiency** (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, after pancreatectomy). The dose is set in **lipase units** (25,000–50,000 units per main meal) and taken **with food**. Enteric-coated microsphere capsules (Creon) are preferred. In cystic fibrosis, very large doses may cause fibrosing colonopathy.
s5366 **Probiotics** are live microorganisms that confer a benefit when taken in adequate amounts (lactobacilli, bifidobacteria, Saccharomyces boulardii). **Prebiotics** are nondigestible substances that stimulate the growth of beneficial microflora (inulin, lactulose, oligosaccharides). Their effectiveness depends on the strain; proven indications are limited (antibiotic-associated diarrhea, acute gastroenteritis in children).

s5581 Diuretics
s5582 Diuretics are among the most commonly used drugs in cardiology, nephrology and intensive care. They relieve edema and pulmonary congestion in heart failure, reduce complications in arterial hypertension, and are used to treat ascites in liver cirrhosis, cerebral edema and glaucoma. At the same time, diuretics significantly change the electrolyte balance: hypokalemia can lead to glycoside toxicity and arrhythmias, and hyperkalemia to cardiac arrest. Knowing which part of the nephron each diuretic acts on is the key to understanding its effectiveness and adverse effects logically.

s5590 The **potency** of a diuretic depends on how much sodium is reabsorbed in the segment it acts on: loop diuretics (25%) — **strong**, thiazides (5–8%) — **moderate**, potassium-sparing agents (2–3%) — **weak**. Agents acting on the proximal tubule are also weak, because the later segments “catch” the sodium.
s5591 Giving furosemide intravenously **slowly** (at no more than 4 mg per minute) reduces the risk of ototoxicity. Giving it together with aminoglycosides, vancomycin or cisplatin increases **ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity**. NSAIDs reduce the effect of loop diuretics.
s5592 The “opposite relationship” with calcium: **“Loops Lose calcium, Thiazides Take it back”**. That is why loop diuretics are useful in hypercalcemia, and thiazides in calcium kidney stones and a tendency to osteoporosis.
s5593 **Risk of hyperkalemia**: potassium-sparing diuretics must be used with caution or not at all together with **potassium preparations, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, NSAIDs, trimethoprim and heparin**, and **in renal failure** (GFR < 30). If potassium exceeds 5.5 mmol/l — risk of cardiac arrhythmias and arrest. Regular monitoring of blood potassium and creatinine is mandatory.
s5594 Mannitol is **absolutely contraindicated in heart failure and pulmonary edema**: it first increases the circulating volume and worsens pulmonary edema. It is also not used in anuria (no urine output), severe dehydration and the active phase of intracerebral hemorrhage.
s5595 **“Diuretics that cause acidosis — A, K, and Stop”**: **A**cetazolamide, **K**-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, amiloride, triamterene) — **S**top, that's all. The other diuretics (loop, thiazide) cause **alkalosis**.
s5596 1. Nephron physiology and the sites of action of diuretics
s5597 About **180 liters** of primary urine are filtered in the glomeruli each day, but only 1–1.5 liters are excreted — **more than 99%** of filtered sodium is reabsorbed. Diuretics inhibit sodium reabsorption; water leaves together with sodium.
