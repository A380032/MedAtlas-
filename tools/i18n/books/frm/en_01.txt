s0004 The importance of prescription writing in training general practitioners. The prescription and its structure. Solid and soft dosage forms and the rules for prescribing them
s0005 A general practitioner (GP) writes dozens of prescriptions every day. A single error in a prescription (a misplaced decimal point, a tenfold dose overshoot, a mix-up of dosage forms) can endanger the patient's life. That is why prescription writing is the most practical branch of pharmacology.
s0006 The student should know:
s0007 the concepts of prescription writing, drug substance, medicinal product, drug preparation and dosage form;
s0008 the essence of the State Pharmacopoeia, lists A and B, and officinal and magistral drugs;
s0009 the types of doses and the rules for writing doses in a prescription;
s0010 the parts of a prescription and the procedure for filling in a prescription form;
s0011 the abbreviations used in writing prescriptions in Latin and the genitive case;
s0012 the classification, advantages and disadvantages of solid and soft dosage forms.
s0013 The student should be able to:
s0014 write prescriptions for powders, tablets, dragées, capsules, granules, ointments, pastes, creams, gels, liniments, suppositories and plasters;
s0015 calculate the amount of a substance from a percentage concentration;
s0016 find and correct errors in a written prescription.
s0017 Remember
s0018 Drug substance → medicinal product → drug preparation. The same drug substance may be produced in different dosage forms: for example, diclofenac is available as tablets, a solution for injection, a gel, suppositories and eye drops.
s0019 Memory aid
s0020 Common INN stems help to recognize the drug group: **-olol** — beta-blockers (metoprolol), **-pril** — ACE inhibitors (enalapril), **-sartan** — angiotensin receptor blockers (losartan), **-statin** — statins (atorvastatin), **-prazole** — proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole), **-floxacin** — fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin), **-mab** — monoclonal antibodies (rituximab).
s0021 There must be at least one digit after the decimal point in a prescription: **1.0** (not “1”), **0.5** (not “.5”). Shifting the decimal point by one place changes the dose 10-fold! Drugs dosed in units of action (U): some antibiotics (benzylpenicillin, nystatin), heparin, insulin, and certain hormones and enzymes.
s0022 Caution
s0023 The formulas above are for rough estimates only. In modern pediatrics, doses for each drug are set in **mg/kg** or **mg/m^2^** (body surface area), based on the package insert and pediatric reference books. For most drugs, calculating a child's dose from age alone can be dangerous.
s0024 A prescription is read “from top to bottom”: **Stamp → Date → Patient → Physician → Rp. → Drug → Instructions → Signatura → Physician's signature**. Initial letters of the Latin names: **I-D-N-N-I-D-S-S-S**. Only **parts 5, 6 and 7** (Rp., drug names, instructions to the pharmacist) are written in Latin; the rest is in the state language.
s0025 Vague expressions such as “internally” or “as directed” must not be written in the **signatura**. The amount, the number of times a day, the timing in relation to meals and the duration must be stated clearly: “Take 1 tablet 3 times a day after meals for 5 days”.
s0026 **Electronic prescription.** In Uzbekistan, the “Electronic prescription” system was launched in December 2025 in Tashkent and a number of pilot districts and is planned to be rolled out nationwide by the end of 2026. The physician confirms the prescription electronically, a QR code is assigned automatically and the prescription is sent to the pharmacy automatically; it was first made mandatory for antibiotics and hormonal preparations. A separate procedure remains for narcotic and potent drugs. Because these rules keep changing, the current orders of the Ministry of Health should be checked. The skill of composing the Latin part of a prescription correctly remains the foundation in the electronic system as well.
s0027 Anions of salts belong to the 3rd declension: **-as → -atis** (Natrii sulfas → Natrii sulfatis), **-is → -itis** (Natrii nitris → Natrii nitritis), **-idum → -idi** (Natrii chloridum → Natrii chloridi, 2nd declension). The cation (metal) is always in the genitive: Natrii, Kalii, Calcii, Magnii, Zinci.
s0028 Formula for calculating the amount of a substance from a percentage concentration: **amount of substance (g) = total mass × percentage / 100**. This formula is the same for ointments, pastes, liniments and solutions.
s0029 Different manuals use different spellings for cream and gel, such as “Crem”, “Cremoris”, “Gel”, “Geli”. According to Latin grammar, Cremor, oris m → genitive **Cremoris**, Gelum, i n → **Geli**. Some Uzbek manuals also give the undeclined form “Gel Troxevasin”. Follow your department's requirements.
s0030 If the amount of base is not specified in a suppository prescription, a rectal suppository is made from 3.0 and a vaginal suppository from 4.0. If the exact amount of base is not known, **q.s.** (quantum satis — as much as needed) is written: Olei Cacao q.s. ut fiat suppositorium.
s0031 Fentanyl is a narcotic analgesic. It is prescribed on a **special form** and is strictly accounted for. Cutting or heating the patch (heating pad, sauna) is forbidden: when heated, absorption rises sharply and respiratory depression may occur.
s0032 Substances that cannot be prescribed as powders: strongly **hygroscopic** substances (calcium chloride, potassium acetate), substances that **liquefy** when mixed together (eutectic mixtures, e.g., camphor with menthol), and **easily decomposing** and **explosive** combinations (potassium permanganate with organic substances).
s0033 **D.t.d. N. ... in tabulettis** — “**D**a **t**ales **d**oses, **N**umero ...” (give such doses, in number ...). The dose of one tablet is written after Rp., and their number after D.t.d.
s0034 Common prescription errors: (1) a missing or misplaced decimal point in the dose; (2) writing the substance name in the nominative case (“Analginum 0.5” instead of “Analgini 0.5”); (3) omitting the dosage form or the number in the D.t.d. part; (4) writing the signatura in Latin or vaguely; (5) prescribing a list A or narcotic drug on an ordinary form; (6) no confirmation mark when the maximum dose is exceeded.
s0035 1. Prescription writing and its basic concepts
s0036 **Prescription writing (recipe writing)** (Lat. recipere — to take) — the branch of pharmacology that studies the rules for prescribing, preparing and dispensing medicines to the patient. It is divided into two parts:
s0037 **general prescription writing** — dosage forms and the general rules for prescribing them;
s0038 **special prescription writing** — prescribing specific drug groups (studied in each subsequent topic). **A prescription** is the physician's **written instruction** to the pharmacist (dispensing chemist) to prepare or dispense a medicinal product in a certain form and dose, and on how to use it. A prescription is simultaneously a medical, legal and financial document.

s0050 Key terms
s0051 **Drug substance (active substance)** — an individual chemical compound or natural substance that has pharmacological activity and is approved for medical use (e.g., paracetamol).
s0052 **Medicinal product** — a substance or mixture of substances approved for the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of disease.
s0053 **Drug preparation** — a ready-to-use medicinal product brought into a particular dosage form (e.g., “Paracetamol 500 mg tablets”).
s0054 **Dosage form** — a state of the medicinal product convenient for use (tablet, solution, ointment, etc.).
s0055 **Medicinal plant raw material** — plants or their parts containing biologically active substances.
s0056 **Galenic preparations** — preparations obtained by processing medicinal plant raw material and containing ballast substances (infusion, decoction, tincture, extract). **Neogalenic preparations** — preparations maximally purified from ballast substances that can be given parenterally.
s0057 State Pharmacopoeia
s0058 **Pharmacopoeia** (Gr. pharmacon — drug, poieo — I make) — a collection of state standards **having the force of law** that set the quality requirements for medicines, the methods of preparing, storing and analyzing them, the maximum doses and the lists. Compliance with the pharmacopoeia is mandatory for all pharmaceutical companies and pharmacies. There is also the WHO **International Pharmacopoeia**, which is advisory in nature.
s0059 In the pharmacopoeia, drugs are classified by their degree of danger:
s0060 **List A (Venena — poisonous)** — poisonous drugs that are prescribed, stored and dosed with great care. They are stored separately in a lockable cabinet.
s0061 **List B (Heroica — potent)** — drugs that may cause complications when used without medical supervision.
s0062 In addition, **narcotic drugs, psychotropic substances and precursors** are controlled by separate legislation. **Officinal drugs** (Lat. officina — pharmacy) — drugs whose composition is set by the pharmacopoeia or a regulatory document and which are produced ready-made in a factory. **Magistral drugs** (Lat. magister — master) — drugs prepared individually in the pharmacy according to a physician's prescription. There are also magistral formulations named after their authors: Votchal's drops, Pavlov's mixture, Vishnevsky's liniment.

s0147 Doses for children and the elderly
s0148 Traditional prescription-writing manuals give simplified formulas for calculating a child's dose from the adult dose:
s0149 by age: **child's dose = adult dose × child's age / 24**;
s0150 by weight: **child's dose = adult dose × child's weight / 70**. Elderly patients (over 60), especially for drugs acting on the CNS, cardiac glycosides and diuretics, are prescribed **1/2–3/4 of the adult dose**.
s0151 3. The prescription and its structure
s0152 A prescription is written on a special **prescription form** clearly and legibly with a ballpoint pen or ink. Corrections are not allowed. The size of an ordinary form is 105 × 148 mm.

s0155 Parts of a prescription
s0156 **Inscriptio** — the stamp and code of the medical institution.
s0157 **Datum** — the date the prescription was written.
s0158 **Nomen aegroti** — the patient's surname, first name, patronymic and age.
s0159 **Nomen medici** — the physician's surname, first name and patronymic.
s0160 **Invocatio** — the physician's address to the pharmacist: **Rp.:** (Recipe — take).
s0161 **Designatio materiarum** — the names and amounts of the drug substances (in Latin, in the genitive case).
s0162 **Subscriptio** — instructions to the pharmacist about the dosage form and the amount to be dispensed (M.f., D.t.d. N.).
s0163 **Signatura** — the method of use for the patient (after S. or D.S., in the state language).
s0164 **Signum et sigillum medici** — the physician's signature and personal seal.
s0165 General rules for writing prescriptions
s0166 Each drug substance is written **on a new line and with a capital letter**.
s0167 Drug substances are written **in the genitive case** (Genetivus), because it means “Take [of what?] ...”: Rp.: Analgini 0.5 — “Take 0.5 of analgin”.
s0168 Order of the ingredients in compound prescriptions: **main drug (basis) → auxiliary drug (adjuvans) → taste and smell corrector (corrigens) → form-giving substance (constituens)**.
s0169 If several substances are prescribed in equal amounts, the amount is placed opposite the last one, preceded by **ana** (**āā**) — “in equal amounts”.
s0170 If two drugs are written on one form, they are separated by the **#** sign (except list A and narcotic drugs, which are written on separate forms).
s0171 If the drug must be dispensed urgently, **Cito!** (quickly) or **Statim!** (immediately) is written at the top of the form.
s0172 If the prescription is to be repeated, **Repetatur!** (let it be repeated) is written.
s0173 If the dose of a drug substance exceeds the maximum dose, the physician writes the amount **in words**, puts an exclamation mark and confirms it with a signature.
s0174 Types of prescription forms
s0175 **Ordinary forms** — for most drugs; certified with the institution's “For prescriptions” seal.
s0176 **Subsidized and free prescriptions** — the form is stamped “free” or “subsidized” and signed by the physician and the head of the institution.
s0177 **Special forms** (serially numbered, strictly accounted for) — for narcotic and psychotropic substances and certain potent drugs. They are certified with the institution's official seal with the coat of arms and are kept in a safe by the chief physician.
s0178 Abbreviations used in prescriptions
s0179 Abbreviation rule: a word is abbreviated **at a consonant** (extr. — extractum, ung. — unguentum). Names of drug substances, plant names and chemical elements are **not abbreviated** (exceptions: aq. — aqua, sp. — spiritus, ac. — acidum).
