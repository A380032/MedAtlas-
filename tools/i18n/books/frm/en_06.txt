s1170 General pharmacology is the “grammar” of all pharmacology. When studying each drug group, we keep returning to the same questions: how the drug is absorbed, where it is distributed, how it is broken down and excreted (pharmacokinetics); which target it acts on and what effect it produces (pharmacodynamics). A student who knows these concepts can understand even a new drug logically.

s1181 The four stages of pharmacokinetics — **ADME**: **A**bsorption, **D**istribution, **M**etabolism (biotransformation), **E**limination (excretion). Pharmacokinetics is “the drug's journey”, pharmacodynamics is “the drug's work”.
s1182 **“Like is absorbed in like, trapped in the opposite”**: an acid is non-ionized (absorbed) in an acidic medium and ionized (trapped and excreted) in an alkaline medium. By this rule, alkalinizing the urine (sodium bicarbonate) in poisoning speeds up the excretion of **weak acids** (salicylates, phenobarbital).
s1183 **Displacement from protein binding.** Drugs that are more than 90% protein-bound (warfarin, sulfonylureas, phenytoin), when given together with another strongly bound drug (NSAIDs, sulfonamides, valproate), may have a sharp rise in their free fraction: risk of bleeding or hypoglycemia. In newborns, sulfonamides and ceftriaxone can displace bilirubin from albumin and cause **bilirubin encephalopathy**.
s1184 **Prodrug** — a drug that is inactive itself and is converted into an active substance in the body: enalapril → enalaprilat, clopidogrel → active thiol metabolite, levodopa → dopamine, codeine → morphine (CYP2D6), prednisone → prednisolone. Some metabolites are toxic: paracetamol → N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) — hepatotoxic.
s1185 Inducers: **“Rifa, Pheno, Carba and Pheny drank, smoked and ate St John's wort”** — rifampicin, phenobarbital, carbamazepine, phenytoin, alcohol, smoking, St John's wort. Inhibitors: **“MAKC CIMA GRAPE”** — **MA**crolides, **K**etoconazole (azoles), **C**iprofloxacin, **CIM**etidine, **A**miodarone, **GRAPE**fruit.
s1186 **The “4–5 half-lives” rule:** with repeated use at a constant dose, steady-state concentration (Css) is reached after about **4–5 × t½**; when the drug is stopped, it also takes **4–5 × t½** for it to be almost completely (94–97%) eliminated from the body. 1 t½ → 50%, 2 → 75%, 3 → 87.5%, 4 → 93.75%, 5 → 96.9%.
s1187 Zero-order kinetics: **“PEA”** — **P**henytoin, **E**thanol, **A**spirin (at high doses). Zero order is a “saturated pump” that removes the same amount every hour.
s1188 G proteins and receptors: **“QISS”** — **Q**q (α~1~, M~1~, M~3~, H~1~), **I**i (α~2~, M~2~, D~2~), **S**s (β~1~, β~2~, D~1~, H~2~, V~2~). Odd-numbered M receptors (M~1~, M~3~) — G~q~; even (M~2~) — G~i~.
s1189 Do not confuse potency with efficacy: furosemide 40 mg is **more effective** than hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg) (it produces a stronger diuresis), even though it is less potent per mg. Bumetanide (1 mg) is **more potent** than furosemide (40 mg), but their efficacy is the same.
s1190 **Amide anesthetics have two “i”s in their names**: l**i**doca**i**ne, art**i**ca**i**ne, bup**i**vaca**i**ne, rop**i**vaca**i**ne, mep**i**vaca**i**ne. Esters have one “i”: procaine, tetracaine, benzocaine, cocaine.
s1191 Maximum doses differ slightly between sources (especially for procaine). Always calculate the dose in **mg/kg** and reduce it according to the patient's condition (old age, liver disease, pregnancy, heart failure). The doses of anesthetics injected into different sites at the same time **add up**.
s1192 **Management of systemic toxicity:** 1) stop injecting the anesthetic, call for help; 2) secure the airway, 100% oxygen; 3) for seizures — **benzodiazepines** (midazolam, diazepam); 4) **20% lipid emulsion** intravenously: 1.5 ml/kg bolus, then an infusion of 0.25 ml/kg/min; 5) in cardiac arrest — resuscitation (epinephrine in small doses; lidocaine is **not used** as an antiarrhythmic).
s1193 The procaine metabolite **PABA** **reduces** the antibacterial effect of sulfonamides (competitive antagonism). Because procaine is broken down by pseudocholinesterase, its effect and toxicity increase in patients with an atypical form of this enzyme and in those taking anticholinesterase agents.
s1194 Adsorbents also bind other drugs and reduce their absorption. Therefore, other drugs are taken **at least 1–2 hours before or after** the adsorbent.

s1248 2.2. Mechanisms of absorption
s1249 **Absorption** — the passage of a drug from the site of administration into the blood. Main mechanisms:
s1250 **Passive diffusion** — along the concentration gradient, without energy expenditure, through the lipid membrane. The most common mechanism. Lipophilic and **non-ionized** substances pass well.
s1251 **Filtration** (through aqueous pores) — water-soluble substances of small molecular weight (up to 100–200 Da).
s1252 **Active transport** — with the help of carrier proteins, with energy (ATP) expenditure, against the gradient; it can be saturated and competitively inhibited (levodopa, iron, 5-fluorouracil).
s1253 **Facilitated diffusion** — with a carrier but along the gradient, without energy (glucose).
s1254 **Pinocytosis (endocytosis)** — engulfment of large molecules by the membrane (vitamin B~12~ — intrinsic factor complex). **Ionization and pH.** Most drugs are weak acids or bases. Their non-ionized (lipophilic) fraction crosses the membrane. The degree of ionization depends on the pH of the medium and the pKa of the substance (**Henderson–Hasselbalch equation**):
s1255 weak **acids** (acetylsalicylic acid, barbiturates) are poorly ionized in an **acidic** medium and are well absorbed;
s1256 weak **bases** (alkaloids: morphine, atropine) are poorly ionized in an **alkaline** medium.
s1257 2.3. Bioavailability and the first-pass effect
s1258 **Bioavailability (F)** — the fraction (%) of the administered dose that reaches the **systemic circulation** unchanged. With intravenous administration, F = 100%. With oral intake, F depends on: the dosage form, completeness of absorption, and metabolism in the intestinal wall and liver.
s1259 **First-pass effect (presystemic elimination)** — a drug absorbed from the intestine first reaches the liver through the portal vein, and part of it is broken down before reaching the systemic circulation. Drugs with a strong first-pass effect: nitroglycerin (given sublingually), propranolol, morphine, lidocaine (unsuitable for oral use), verapamil.

s1262 **Bioequivalence** — two preparations with the same active substance (original and generic) at the same dose produce the same Cmax, Tmax and AUC in the blood. Generic preparations must prove their bioequivalence to be registered.
s1263 2.4. Distribution
s1264 After absorption, the drug is distributed to the tissues through the blood. Factors affecting distribution:
s1265 **blood flow** (the brain, heart, kidneys and liver are saturated quickly; adipose tissue slowly);
s1266 **lipophilicity** and molecular size;
s1267 **binding to plasma proteins** (acidic drugs — to albumin, basic drugs — to alpha-1-acid glycoprotein). Only the **free (unbound) fraction** is active, is metabolized and is filtered;
s1268 **biological barriers**: the blood–brain barrier (BBB), the placental barrier, the blood–ocular and blood–testis barriers.
s1269 **The blood–brain barrier** — tight junctions of the endothelium of brain capillaries and the P-glycoprotein pump. Lipophilic substances (diazepam, thiopental) cross well; quaternary ammonium compounds (neostigmine, metocinium) and many antibiotics cross poorly. In inflammation (meningitis), the permeability of the barrier increases — benzylpenicillin reaches the cerebrospinal fluid.
s1270 **The placental barrier** — lipophilic, small-molecule drugs cross easily. Therefore, during pregnancy any drug is regarded as potentially dangerous.
s1271 **Volume of distribution (Vd)** — a notional volume that relates the amount of drug in the body to its plasma concentration: **Vd = D / C~0~**. Small Vd (5–15 l) — the drug is mainly in the blood (heparin, warfarin); large Vd (hundreds of liters) — the drug accumulates in the tissues (digoxin, amiodarone, chloroquine). In poisoning with drugs of large Vd, hemodialysis is ineffective.
s1272 **Depot formation** — accumulation of a drug in a tissue: thiopental — in adipose tissue, tetracyclines — in bone and teeth, iodine — in the thyroid gland, amiodarone — in fat and the lungs.

s1306 2.6. Excretion
s1307 Drugs are excreted mainly through the **kidneys**, and also with the **bile** through the intestine, through the lungs (volatile substances — ether, halothane), sweat, saliva and **breast milk**.
s1308 **Renal excretion** is the sum of three processes:
s1309 **Glomerular filtration** — small-molecule substances not bound to protein;
s1310 **Tubular secretion** (in the proximal tubule, active) — separate carriers for acids (penicillins, diuretics) and bases. Probenecid competitively inhibits penicillin secretion;
s1311 **Tubular reabsorption** (passive) — lipophilic, non-ionized substances are reabsorbed. Reabsorption can be controlled by changing the urine pH.

s1316 **Enterohepatic circulation** — a conjugate excreted into the intestine with the bile is broken down by intestinal bacteria, and the drug is reabsorbed. This prolongs the action of drugs (digitoxin, estrogens, morphine). Antibiotics can alter the intestinal flora and reduce the enterohepatic circulation of estrogen-containing contraceptives.
s1317 **In renal failure**, the doses of drugs excreted unchanged mainly by the kidneys are reduced according to creatinine clearance: aminoglycosides, vancomycin, digoxin, metformin, lithium, enoxaparin. **In hepatic failure** — drugs that undergo extensive hepatic metabolism (morphine, benzodiazepines, propranolol).
