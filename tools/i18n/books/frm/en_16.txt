s3262 2.5. Combined anesthesia and premedication
s3263 **Premedication** — preparation with drugs before surgery:
s3264 **anxiolytic and sedative** — benzodiazepines (midazolam);
s3265 **analgesic** — opioids (fentanyl, Promedol);
s3266 **vagolytic** (reducing salivary and bronchial secretion and reflex bradycardia) — atropine, glycopyrronium;
s3267 **antiemetic and antacid** (risk of aspiration) — ondansetron, dexamethasone, H2 blockers or proton pump inhibitors;
s3268 **antihistamine** (in an allergic history).
s3269 **Balanced anesthesia** scheme: induction (propofol) → analgesia (fentanyl) → muscle relaxation (rocuronium) and intubation → maintenance of anesthesia (sevoflurane or propofol infusion + fentanyl) → awakening (muscle relaxant antagonist). **Neuroleptanalgesia** — a combination of a neuroleptic (droperidol) + a potent opioid (fentanyl); the patient is conscious but indifferent and pain-free.
s3270 3. Laboratory class: studying the stages of anesthesia
s3271 This section corresponds to the 5th-semester class “Laboratory study of the stages of general anesthetics”.
s3272 Course of the experiment (in a mouse or rat):
s3273 The animal's baseline state is recorded: motor activity, respiratory rate, response to a painful stimulus (tail pinch), corneal and righting reflexes, muscle tone.
s3274 The animal is placed under a glass bell jar and a cotton ball soaked in the anesthetic (ether or sevoflurane) is placed inside.
s3275 Signs are recorded every 30–60 seconds: **stage I** — reduced activity, impaired coordination; **stage II** — motor excitement, irregular breathing; **stage III** — loss of the righting reflex, no response to pain, relaxed muscles, rhythmic and slow breathing.
s3276 If anesthesia deepens (breathing slows sharply), the animal is immediately taken out into fresh air and the order of awakening is observed.
s3277 The results are entered into a table and compared with an intravenous agent (thiopental): with it, stage II is hardly seen.
s3278 4. Neuroleptics (antipsychotic agents)
s3279 **Neuroleptics** — drugs that eliminate **psychoses** (hallucinations, delusions, psychomotor agitation, thought disorders). They do not cause loss of consciousness and do not produce anesthesia even at large doses.
s3280 **History.** In 1952, in France, Delay and Deniker introduced chlorpromazine (aminazine) into psychiatry — the “psychopharmacological revolution” began.
s3281 4.1. Mechanism of action
s3282 According to the **dopamine theory** of psychoses, the positive symptoms of schizophrenia are associated with increased dopamine activity in the **mesolimbic** pathway. Neuroleptics **block D2 dopamine receptors**. Depending on which dopaminergic system is blocked, both therapeutic and adverse effects arise.

s3360 5. Anxiolytics (tranquilizers)
s3361 **Anxiolytics** — drugs that reduce anxiety, fear and emotional tension. They do not eliminate psychoses.
s3362 5.1. Benzodiazepines
s3363 **Mechanism of action.** Benzodiazepines bind to a special **benzodiazepine (allosteric) binding site** of the **GABA-A receptor–chloride channel complex** and increase the affinity of GABA for the receptor. As a result, the **frequency of chloride channel opening** under the action of GABA increases, and the neuron is hyperpolarized — inhibition is enhanced. Benzodiazepines do not open the channel by themselves in the absence of GABA — that is why they are relatively safe when used alone.
