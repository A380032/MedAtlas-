s7174 In 1928 A. Fleming discovered penicillin, in the 1940s it entered clinical practice, and mortality from bacterial infections fell sharply. Today antibiotics are the most prescribed group of drugs, but about half of them are prescribed without justification (mainly for viral infections). The most important skills for a GP are correctly determining whether an antibiotic is needed, choosing the first-line drug for common infections, and knowing the restrictions related to allergy and special groups (children, pregnant women, the elderly).

s7183 Drugs acting on **30S** — “**AT 30**” — **A**minoglycosides, **T**etracyclines. Drugs acting on **50S** — “**CELL at 50**” — **C**hloramphenicol, **E**rythromycin (macrolides), **L**incosamides (clindamycin), **L**inezolid.
s7184 Giving bactericidal and bacteriostatic antibiotics together (for example, penicillin + tetracycline) is usually inappropriate: beta-lactams act only on the cell wall of **growing** bacteria, while bacteriostatic drugs stop growth — antagonism is possible. Exception: special combinations (for example, penicillin + clindamycin in severe streptococcal infections — to stop toxin synthesis).
s7185 **Aminopenicillins and infectious mononucleosis**: when ampicillin or amoxicillin is given to patients with Epstein–Barr virus infection (often misdiagnosed as “tonsillitis”), a **maculopapular rash** appears in 70–100% of cases; this is not a true allergy, but it is the reason to exclude mononucleosis when treating “tonsillitis” in a young patient.
s7186 With each generation: **gram-negative activity increases**, **gram-positive activity** (up to the 3rd generation) **decreases**, and resistance to beta-lactamases and penetration of the BBB increase. Cephalosporins do not act on **“LAME”**: **L**isteria, **A**typicals (mycoplasma, chlamydia), **M**RSA (except the 5th generation), **E**nterococci.
s7187 **Ceftriaxone**: 1) in newborns (especially premature infants) it is not given intravenously together with **calcium-containing solutions** — ceftriaxone-calcium precipitates in the lungs and kidneys; 2) in newborns it displaces bilirubin from albumin (contraindicated in hyperbilirubinemia); 3) it may cause **pseudolithiasis** (sludge) in the gallbladder. **Cefoperazone** — a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol and hypoprothrombinemia.
s7188 The tradition of **intradermal penicillin testing** (before every injection in CIS countries) does not reliably predict allergy in patients without a history and may itself cause sensitization. Whenever any antibiotic is given for the first time, an **anaphylaxis kit** (epinephrine) must be ready and the patient must be observed for 30 minutes.
s7189 **Adverse effects of aminoglycosides — “NOTN”**: **N**ephrotoxicity (proximal tubules, usually reversible), **O**totoxicity (cochlear — irreversible hearing loss; vestibular — dizziness, ataxia; even one dose in people with a mitochondrial DNA mutation), **T**eratogenicity (damage to the fetal auditory nerve — contraindicated in pregnancy), **N**euromuscular blockade (enhances the effect of muscle relaxants, dangerous in myasthenia; treatment — calcium preparations). **Once-daily dosing** (the same total dose) reduces toxicity while preserving effectiveness; monitoring of renal function and blood concentration; the course is as short as possible (5–7 days); caution together with furosemide, vancomycin and other nephrotoxic drugs.
s7190 **Erythromycin and clarithromycin are strong CYP3A4 inhibitors** (azithromycin — very weak): they increase the concentrations of **statins** (simvastatin, atorvastatin — rhabdomyolysis), **warfarin**, carbamazepine, cyclosporine, tacrolimus, colchicine, calcium antagonists, ergot alkaloids, some antiarrhythmics and DOACs. When there is a risk of interaction, **azithromycin** is preferred.
s7191 **Tetracyclines are contraindicated in children under 8, pregnant women and breastfeeding women**: they bind calcium and accumulate in growing **bones and teeth** — **yellow-brown discoloration of tooth enamel**, enamel hypoplasia, slowed bone growth. Other adverse effects: **photosensitivity** (sun rash — doxycycline), esophageal ulcer (take the capsule with plenty of water, upright), gastrointestinal disturbances, candidiasis and superinfections, hepatotoxicity (in pregnant women), raised intracranial pressure; expired tetracycline — Fanconi syndrome. Absorption is reduced by chelation with **calcium, magnesium, aluminum, iron and milk**.
s7192 According to modern data, **a short course of doxycycline** (≤ 21 days) hardly causes tooth discoloration in children under 8; therefore in life-threatening **rickettsioses** (for example, Rocky Mountain spotted fever) doxycycline is the drug of choice at any age.
s7193 Dangerous adverse effects of chloramphenicol (levomycetin): 1) **aplastic anemia** — dose-independent, idiosyncratic, often irreversible and fatal (one in 40,000 cases), described even after eye drops; 2) dose-dependent **reversible myelosuppression**; 3) **“gray baby syndrome”** in newborns — accumulation due to immature hepatic glucuronidation, with gray-bluish skin color, hypothermia, vomiting, abdominal distension and collapse. In CIS countries chloramphenicol tablets were widely used for diarrhea — this **is not recommended**.
s7194 Antibiotics contraindicated in children: **tetracyclines** (under 8; a short course of doxycycline is an exception), **fluoroquinolones** (under 18, except for special indications — cartilage), **chloramphenicol** (newborns — gray baby syndrome), **ceftriaxone** (in newborns with hyperbilirubinemia and with calcium-containing solutions), **co-trimoxazole** and sulfonamides (under 2 months — kernicterus).

s7213 2. Beta-lactam antibiotics
s7214 2.1. General mechanism
s7215 Beta-lactams have a **beta-lactam ring** in their molecule. They bind to bacterial **penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)** — the **transpeptidases** that form the cross-links of peptidoglycan — and block the last step of cell wall synthesis. As a result, the growing bacterium cannot withstand the osmotic pressure and undergoes **lysis** (bactericidal). Human cells have no cell wall — that is why beta-lactams are **very little toxic**.
s7216 **Resistance: beta-lactamases** (break the beta-lactam ring), altered PBPs (**MRSA** — PBP2a; penicillin-resistant pneumococci), reduced porins and efflux in gram-negatives.

s7266 2.4. Carbapenems and monobactams
s7267 **Carbapenems** (meropenem, imipenem/cilastatin, ertapenem, doripenem) are **the broadest-spectrum** beta-lactams: gram-positives, gram-negatives (including **ESBL-producing** enterobacteria), anaerobes; meropenem and imipenem — Pseudomonas (not ertapenem). They do not act on MRSA, Stenotrophomonas or atypicals. **Reserve antibiotics** — severe hospital infections, sepsis, ESBL infections. **Imipenem** is broken down in the kidney by dehydropeptidase — it is given together with **cilastatin**; risk of seizures (especially with imipenem). Carbapenems sharply reduce valproate concentrations.
s7268 **Monobactams** — **aztreonam**: only against gram-negatives (including Pseudomonas); **almost no cross-allergy with penicillins** (except for the side chain it shares with ceftazidime) — an alternative for gram-negative infections in severe penicillin allergy.
