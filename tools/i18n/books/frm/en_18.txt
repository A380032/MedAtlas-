s3702 3.4. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)
s3703 SSRIs are **first-line** drugs for depression, anxiety disorders, panic disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), post-traumatic stress disorder, bulimia and premenstrual dysphoria. They are safer: they hardly act on M, H1 and alpha-1 receptors and are of low toxicity in overdose.

s3716 3.5. Serotonin syndrome
s3717 **Serotonin syndrome** — an excessive increase of serotonin in the CNS. Causes: combinations of serotonergic drugs — SSRIs/SNRIs + **MAO inhibitors** (the most dangerous), tramadol, triptans, linezolid, methylene blue, lithium, St John's wort, MDMA (ecstasy).

s3726 3.6. SNRIs, MAO inhibitors and atypical antidepressants
s3727 **SNRIs** (venlafaxine, duloxetine) — severe depression, anxiety; **duloxetine** — also for the pain of diabetic neuropathy and fibromyalgia. Adverse effects: rise in BP (venlafaxine, at high doses), nausea, sweating, strong withdrawal syndrome.
s3728 **MAO inhibitors.** Irreversible nonselective agents (phenelzine) are now used only in treatment-resistant cases. While taking them, eating **tyramine-rich foods** (aged cheese, smoked fish and meat, red wine, beer, soy sauce) causes a **hypertensive crisis** (“cheese reaction”). A **2-week** break is needed before switching to an SSRI (5 weeks when switching from fluoxetine to an MAOI). **Moclobemide** (reversible, MAO-A) — low risk of the “cheese reaction”.
s3729 **Mirtazapine** — sedative, increases appetite, little effect on sexual function; convenient in depression with insomnia and weight loss and in the elderly. **Bupropion** — does not affect sexual function, causes no weight gain, also used for smoking cessation; lowers the seizure threshold. **Trazodone** — in small doses for insomnia; rarely priapism. **Agomelatine** — restores the circadian rhythm; liver enzymes must be monitored.
s3730 3.7. Principles of antidepressant treatment
s3731 In **bipolar disorder**, antidepressants are given only together with mood stabilizers (lithium, valproate, lamotrigine) — otherwise there is a risk of switching to **mania**.

s3846 A 19-year-old female student was brought in by her relatives: 2 hours ago she took 30 of her mother's amitriptyline tablets. The patient is drowsy, her speech is slurred, the pupils are dilated, the skin is dry and hot, HR 135/min, BP 90/55 mm Hg. The ECG shows QRS 130 ms. She had one seizure during transport.
s3847 1) Diagnosis? 2) Mechanism of the signs? 3) Treatment.
s3848 1) Acute poisoning with a tricyclic antidepressant (amitriptyline). 2) Mydriasis, dry hot skin, tachycardia — anticholinergic effect; hypotension — alpha-1 blockade; QRS widening — sodium channel blockade (cardiotoxicity); seizures and drowsiness — CNS effect. 3) Secure the airway (intubation if necessary), activated charcoal; sodium bicarbonate 8.4% 1–2 mmol/kg intravenously (until the QRS narrows, blood pH 7.45–7.55); for seizures — benzodiazepines; for hypotension — fluids and norepinephrine; ECG monitoring; physostigmine and flumazenil are contraindicated. After recovery — psychiatric consultation (suicide attempt).
s3849 A 45-year-old woman has taken sertraline for depression for 3 months. An orthopedic trauma surgeon prescribed tramadol for low back pain. Two days later the patient developed restlessness, profuse sweating, tremor, diarrhea, a temperature of 38.8 °C, HR 118/min, and clonus and hyperreflexia in the legs.
s3850 1) Your diagnosis? 2) The cause? 3) Treatment.
s3851 1) Serotonin syndrome. 2) Sertraline (an SSRI) and tramadol (an opioid + inhibits serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake) — a combination of two serotonergic agents. 3) Stop both drugs, benzodiazepines (for agitation and muscle hyperactivity), cooling, fluids; cyproheptadine for marked signs; in severe hyperthermia — intensive care. Later, choose a non-serotonergic analgesic for the pain (paracetamol, NSAIDs).

s3887 Drugs acting on the respiratory system
s3888 Cough, sputum and bronchial obstruction are among the most common complaints at the GP's appointment. Bronchial asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) affect millions of people, and correctly chosen inhalation therapy radically changes their quality of life. At the same time, errors such as using antitussives together with expectorants, or treating asthma only with a “reliever”, are widespread. This topic covers respiratory pharmacology on the basis of modern clinical guidelines.

s3897 Older textbooks recommended analeptics for restoring breathing in poisoning with anesthetics, hypnotics and alcohol. In modern practice, respiratory failure is treated by **securing the airway, oxygen and mechanical ventilation**. The effect of analeptics is short and their therapeutic range is narrow; in a severely depressed CNS they are ineffective, and at large doses they cause **seizures** and increase oxygen demand. Doxapram is used by specialists in some cases (respiratory depression after anesthesia, hypercapnia in COPD); the specific antidote in opioid poisoning is **naloxone**.
s3898 **Antitussives and expectorants (mucolytics) must not be given together**: the mucolytic thins the sputum and increases its volume, while the antitussive suppresses the reflex that expels it — sputum accumulates in the bronchi (**“bogging”**), and the risk of pneumonia and obstruction increases.
s3899 **In bronchial asthma, using a LABA as monotherapy without an ICS is forbidden** — the risk of death from asthma increases (the inflammation is not treated and the symptoms are masked). In addition, current GINA recommendations (since 2019) **do not recommend treating asthma with a SABA (salbutamol) alone** in adults and adolescents; every patient should receive ICS-containing treatment.
