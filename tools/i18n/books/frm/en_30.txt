s6219 A 72-year-old woman takes warfarin for atrial fibrillation (stable at INR 2.4). She was prescribed co-trimoxazole for a urinary tract infection and fluconazole for foot candidiasis. After 6 days she developed nosebleeds and many bruises under the skin; INR 7.8.
s6220 1) The cause of the INR rise? 2) Management? 3) What should be done in the future?
s6221 1) Co-trimoxazole and fluconazole inhibited CYP2C9 and slowed warfarin metabolism (co-trimoxazole also displaces it from proteins and reduces the intestinal flora) — the anticoagulant effect increased sharply. 2) Stop warfarin temporarily; INR 4.5–10 with minor bleeding — vitamin K 1–2.5 mg orally; local control of the nosebleed; recheck the INR after 24 hours; if there is major bleeding — prothrombin complex concentrate + intravenous vitamin K. 3) Choose antibiotics with few interactions (nitrofurantoin or fosfomycin); monitor the INR frequently when a new drug is prescribed; consider switching to a DOAC (apixaban) in nonvalvular fibrillation.
s6222 A 60-year-old man is receiving enoxaparin after knee replacement surgery. On day 8 his platelets fell from 250 to 90 × 10^9^/l, and deep vein thrombosis of the left leg was found.
s6223 1) Diagnosis? 2) Management.
s6224 1) Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — an immune mechanism (antibodies to the heparin-PF4 complex), a platelet fall of more than 50%, on days 5–14 and with paradoxical thrombosis. 2) Stop all heparins immediately (including heparin solutions for flushing catheters); a non-heparin anticoagulant — intravenous argatroban or bivalirudin, or fondaparinux; once platelets recover, a DOAC or warfarin (warfarin only when platelets are > 150, otherwise there is a risk of skin necrosis and venous gangrene); platelet transfusion is not recommended; antibody testing; record heparin allergy in the medical records.
s6225 A 28-year-old woman complains of weakness, dizziness, brittle nails and a craving to eat chalk. Her periods are heavy and prolonged. Hemoglobin 88 g/l, MCV 68 fl, ferritin 6 ng/ml. She is planning a pregnancy.

s6227 1) Iron deficiency anemia (microcytic, low ferritin) caused by menorrhagia. 2) An oral iron(II) preparation — elemental iron 100 mg once a day or every other day, on an empty stomach, with ascorbic acid, without tea or milk; the effect is assessed by a rise in hemoglobin after 2–4 weeks; after hemoglobin normalizes, continue for another 3 months (to replenish stores); warn about dark stools; a gynecologist's consultation to find the cause of the menorrhagia (tranexamic acid if needed). s6274 This topic brings together two large and practically very important sections. Among **protein and polypeptide hormones**, the most important is **insulin**: diabetes mellitus affects about 10% of the population, and insulin and other glucose-lowering drugs are part of a GP's daily practice. Hypothalamic and pituitary hormones, and thyroid and parathyroid hormones are used in endocrinology, obstetrics and pediatrics. **Anti-inflammatory drugs** — nonsteroidal (NSAIDs) and steroidal (glucocorticoids) — are among the most consumed drugs in the world, and their adverse effects (gastrointestinal bleeding, kidney injury, corticosteroid complications) are also very common.

s6284 Protein and polypeptide hormones **are broken down in the gastrointestinal tract** — so they are given mainly **parenterally** (injection, intranasally). Exceptions: desmopressin (nasal spray, sublingual and oral tablets), the oral form of semaglutide (with a special absorption enhancer).
s6285 During treatment with **desmopressin** (especially in the elderly and in children with enuresis) there is a risk of **hyponatremia** and water intoxication: fluid intake in the evening must be restricted.
s6286 Using levothyroxine **for weight loss** is dangerous and prohibited.
s6287 Treatment of thyrotoxicosis in pregnancy: **in the 1st trimester — propylthiouracil** (thiamazole is teratogenic), **in the 2nd–3rd trimesters — thiamazole** (PTU is hepatotoxic). During treatment with thionamides, the patient must be warned about **signs of agranulocytosis** (fever, sore throat, mouth ulcers), and in such cases the drug must be stopped immediately and the white cell count checked.
s6288 **Only short-acting (regular, soluble) insulin can be given intravenously**; NPH and premixed insulins are suspensions — not for intravenous use. In diabetic ketoacidosis: **fluids (0.9% NaCl)** → **intravenous infusion of short-acting insulin** (0.1 U/kg/hour) → **potassium** (insulin shifts potassium into cells and causes hypokalemia) → glucose is added when blood glucose falls to 14 mmol/l.
s6289 **Care in hypoglycemia (the “rule of 15”)**: if conscious — **15–20 g of rapidly absorbed carbohydrate** (3–4 glucose tablets, 150–200 ml of sweet juice), recheck glucose after 15 minutes, repeat if needed, then a meal with complex carbohydrates. If **unconscious** — **40% glucose 20–40 ml intravenously** (or 10% glucose) or **glucagon 1 mg** intramuscularly or subcutaneously (nasal powder 3 mg) — relatives can also give it at home. Fluids must not be poured into the mouth of an unconscious patient (aspiration).
s6290 Older textbooks presented sulfonylureas (especially glibenclamide) and biguanides such as buformin and phenformin as the main drugs. **Phenformin and buformin were withdrawn from the market because of lactic acidosis**; glibenclamide is not recommended in the elderly because it causes prolonged hypoglycemia — **gliclazide MR** or glimepiride is preferred.
s6291 **Paracetamol poisoning** — hepatotoxicity. At therapeutic doses, paracetamol is conjugated mainly with glucuronide and sulfate; a small part is converted by CYP2E1 into the toxic **NAPQI**, which is detoxified by **glutathione**. In overdose glutathione is depleted → NAPQI destroys hepatocytes → **liver necrosis** (on days 2–4; almost no signs on the first day!). **Antidote — N-acetylcysteine** (restores glutathione stores), most effective within 8 hours; measure the blood paracetamol concentration (using the nomogram). Risk factors: alcoholism, starvation, CYP inducers. Many combined “cold” remedies contain paracetamol — risk of **hidden overdose**.
s6292 Equivalent doses: **“20 – 5 – 4 – 0.75”** — hydrocortisone 20 mg = prednisolone 5 mg = methylprednisolone 4 mg = dexamethasone 0.75 mg. Dexamethasone is chosen for fetal lung maturation and in cerebral edema (no mineralocorticoid effect, potent, crosses the placenta).
s6293 **The rule for withdrawing glucocorticoids**: if systemic glucocorticoids have been taken **for more than 3 weeks** (or > 20 mg/day of prednisolone equivalent for more than 1 week), they **must not be stopped abruptly** — the dose is reduced gradually (to allow the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis to recover). Abrupt withdrawal causes **acute adrenal insufficiency** (severe weakness, hypotension, shock, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia) and relapse of the disease. In such patients the dose must be increased during **stress** (surgery, severe infection, trauma) (“stress dose”, intravenous hydrocortisone).
