s5590 Mannitol is **absolutely contraindicated in heart failure and pulmonary edema**: it first increases the circulating volume and worsens pulmonary edema. It is also not used in anuria (no urine output), severe dehydration and the active phase of intracerebral hemorrhage.
s5591 **“Diuretics that cause acidosis — A, K, and Stop”**: **A**cetazolamide, **K**-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, amiloride, triamterene) — **S**top, that's all. The other diuretics (loop, thiazide) cause **alkalosis**.
s5592 1. Nephron physiology and the sites of action of diuretics
s5593 About **180 liters** of primary urine are filtered in the glomeruli each day, but only 1–1.5 liters are excreted — **more than 99%** of filtered sodium is reabsorbed. Diuretics inhibit sodium reabsorption; water leaves together with sodium.

s5648 3. Loop diuretics
s5649 **Mechanism.** In the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle they block the **Na^+^-K^+^-2Cl– cotransporter** from the luminal side. Up to 25% of filtered sodium is reabsorbed here — that is why they are the **most potent** diuretics. In addition:
s5650 the **hyperosmolarity** of the renal medulla is disrupted — the ability to concentrate urine decreases;
s5651 paracellular reabsorption of Ca^2+^ and Mg^2+^ in this segment also decreases — **calcium and magnesium excretion increases** (unlike thiazides!);
s5652 by increasing prostaglandin synthesis in the kidney they increase renal blood flow; **when given intravenously — venodilation before diuresis** (immediate relief in pulmonary edema) — NSAIDs reduce this effect.
s5653 **acute left ventricular failure and pulmonary edema** (intravenously);
s5654 edema and congestion in chronic heart failure (torasemide acts longer and has higher bioavailability);
s5655 edema in liver cirrhosis and nephrotic syndrome (with spironolactone);
s5656 edema and AH **in renal failure** (GFR < 30 ml/min — thiazides are ineffective);
s5657 **hypercalcemia** (after fluid replacement);
s5658 forced diuresis (in poisoning; now rarely), hyperkalemia.

s5674 4. Thiazide and thiazide-like diuretics
s5675 **Mechanism.** They block the **Na^+^-Cl– cotransporter** in the distal convoluted tubule. In addition, they **increase calcium reabsorption** (urinary calcium decreases) — the opposite of loop diuretics.
s5676 **arterial hypertension** — first-line drugs (indapamide and chlorthalidone are preferred; Topic 11);
s5677 mild and moderate edema (heart failure);
s5678 **prevention of kidney stones** — idiopathic hypercalciuria (calcium stones);
s5679 **nephrogenic diabetes insipidus** (paradoxically reduces urine volume — by increasing proximal reabsorption);
s5680 in severe resistant edema, combined with loop diuretics (metolazone — “sequential nephron blockade”).
s5681 **Adverse effects (mainly at high doses):** hypokalemia, **hyponatremia** (especially in elderly women — thiazides more than loop diuretics), hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis, **hyperuricemia and gout**, **hyperglycemia** (reduced insulin secretion), dyslipidemia, **hypercalcemia**, photosensitivity, erectile dysfunction, sulfonamide allergy; they increase **lithium** concentration.
s5682 When **GFR is below 30 ml/min**, thiazides are usually **ineffective** (chlorthalidone is a partial exception) — loop diuretics are used in such patients. There is evidence that long-term, high-dose hydrochlorothiazide slightly increases the risk of non-melanoma skin cancer (especially squamous cell) — sun protection is recommended.
s5683 5. Potassium-sparing diuretics
s5684 5.1. Aldosterone (mineralocorticoid receptor) antagonists
s5685 **Spironolactone** is a **competitive antagonist** of aldosterone: by blocking mineralocorticoid receptors in collecting duct cells, it reduces the synthesis of Na^+^ channels and Na^+^/K^+^-ATPase. Sodium excretion increases slightly, and **potassium and hydrogen secretion decreases**. Its effect develops slowly (over 2–3 days) and it is **the more effective the higher the aldosterone level**.
s5686 **chronic heart failure** (reduced ejection fraction) — reduces mortality;
s5687 **ascites in liver cirrhosis** (secondary hyperaldosteronism) — the diuretic of first choice;
s5688 **primary hyperaldosteronism** (Conn syndrome);
s5689 resistant arterial hypertension;
s5690 with thiazide or loop diuretics — prevention of hypokalemia;
s5691 hyperandrogenism in women (hirsutism, acne) — because of its antiandrogenic effect. **Adverse effects: hyperkalemia** (especially with renal failure, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, potassium preparations, NSAIDs), metabolic acidosis; **antiandrogenic and progestogenic effects** — **gynecomastia** and impotence in men, menstrual disorders and breast pain in women.
s5692 **Eplerenone** is more selective for mineralocorticoid receptors; it does not cause gynecomastia. **Finerenone** is a nonsteroidal antagonist; used in diabetic kidney disease.
s5693 5.2. Sodium channel blockers
s5694 **Amiloride** and **triamterene** block the epithelial sodium channels (ENaC) of the collecting duct **directly** — their effect does not depend on aldosterone. Mainly in combination with thiazides to prevent hypokalemia (Triampur — triamterene + hydrochlorothiazide). Amiloride — in **Liddle syndrome** and lithium-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Adverse effects: hyperkalemia; triamterene — kidney stones, folic acid antagonism.
s5695 6. Other diuretics
s5696 6.1. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
s5697 **Acetazolamide (Diacarb)** inhibits **carbonic anhydrase** in the proximal tubule → H^+^ formation and Na^+^/H^+^ exchange decrease → excretion of **bicarbonate**, sodium and potassium increases, the urine becomes alkaline, and **metabolic acidosis** develops. The diuretic effect is weak and decreases within 2–3 days as acidosis develops (self-limiting).
s5698 **Uses** (mainly non-diuretic): **glaucoma** (reduces the production of aqueous humor; in an attack of angle-closure glaucoma — orally or intravenously; topical analogs — dorzolamide and brinzolamide drops), **prevention of mountain (altitude) sickness**, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, as an add-on in epilepsy, correction of metabolic alkalosis, alkalinization of urine (in uric acid and cystine stones).
s5699 **Adverse effects:** metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia, paresthesias (numbness of the hands and feet), **calcium phosphate stones**, sulfonamide allergy; in liver cirrhosis — ammonia accumulation (hepatic encephalopathy).
s5700 6.2. Osmotic diuretics
s5701 **Mannitol** is filtered but not reabsorbed; by increasing the osmotic pressure in the tubules, it stops **water** reabsorption (mainly in the proximal tubule and the thin limb of the loop of Henle). By also increasing osmotic pressure in the blood, it **draws water from the tissues into the vessels**.
s5702 **Uses** (intravenous only): **cerebral edema and raised intracranial pressure**, **an acute attack of angle-closure glaucoma** (rapid lowering of intraocular pressure), prevention of acute kidney injury with oliguria (rhabdomyolysis — historically).
s5703 6.3. SGLT2 inhibitors and aquaretics
s5704 **SGLT2 inhibitors** (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) block the sodium-glucose cotransporter in the proximal tubule and cause glucosuria and moderate natriuresis. Although they are not strong diuretics, they reduce hospitalizations and mortality in **heart failure** (regardless of ejection fraction) and **chronic kidney disease** (Topics 11 and 15).
s5705 **Vaptans** (tolvaptan) block vasopressin V~2~ receptors in the collecting duct and increase the excretion of **free water** (aquaresis). Uses: **hypervolemic and euvolemic hyponatremia** (syndrome of inappropriate vasopressin secretion — SIADH, heart failure), autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease. Raising sodium too quickly (> 10 mmol/l/day) carries a risk of **osmotic demyelination**; hepatotoxicity.

s5750 8.2. Diuretic resistance
s5751 **Causes:** high salt intake, taking NSAIDs, low perfusion (hypotension), hypoalbuminemia (furosemide reaches the tubule bound to protein), impaired intestinal absorption (bowel edema), adaptive hypertrophy of the distal nephron (the “braking” phenomenon).
s5752 **Ways to overcome it:** salt restriction; stopping NSAIDs; giving the diuretic intravenously or switching to a continuous infusion; increasing the dose; **sequential nephron blockade** — adding a thiazide (metolazone) or acetazolamide to a loop diuretic; with albumin in hypoalbuminemia.
s5753 **Daily weight monitoring** during diuretic therapy is the simplest and most reliable method: a 1 kg decrease in weight corresponds to a loss of about 1 liter of fluid. Patients with heart failure are taught to see a doctor (or to increase the diuretic dose themselves) if their weight increases by more than 2 kg in 2–3 days.
s5754 Skill 1. Monitoring electrolytes during diuretic therapy
s5755 Before starting treatment: sodium, potassium, creatinine (GFR), glucose, uric acid.
s5756 When starting loop and thiazide diuretics or increasing the dose — potassium and creatinine after 1–2 weeks.
s5757 When starting spironolactone — potassium and creatinine at 3 days and 1 week, then at 1, 2, 3 and 6 months.
s5758 Hypokalemia (< 3.5 mmol/l) — add potassium preparations or a potassium-sparing diuretic; especially important in a patient taking glycosides.
s5759 Hyperkalemia (> 5.5 mmol/l) — reduce the dose of the potassium-sparing diuretic or stop it; > 6.0 — urgent treatment (calcium gluconate, insulin + glucose, salbutamol).
