s5773 9.7. Clinical section
s5774 A child with a murmur — diagnostic approach
s5775 **Determine the phase of the murmur:** diastolic or continuous — **organic**; systolic — continue.
s5776 **Assess its features:** soft, short, non-radiating, position-dependent — most likely functional; harsh, long, radiating, with a thrill — organic.
s5777 **Additional signs:** changes in the heart sounds, cardiomegaly, cyanosis, signs of failure, changes in the peripheral pulses, an arm–leg BP difference — organic.
s5778 **Exclude extracardiac causes**: anemia (CBC), fever, thyrotoxicosis.
s5779 **Investigations:** ECG, **echocardiography** (the main one), X-ray if necessary.
s5780 In an infant, **any murmur**, and also a murmur accompanied by **dyspnea, cyanosis, poor weight gain or sweating**, requires a cardiology consultation and echocardiography. In large defects (e.g., a large VSD) there may be **no** murmur in the first weeks of life, because pulmonary vascular resistance is still high.
s5781 A child who has fainted
s5782 Types of fainting (syncope): **vasovagal** (the most common — prolonged standing, pain, fear, a stuffy room; preceded by nausea, sweating, pallor), **orthostatic** (on standing up abruptly), **sinocarotid**, **post-tussive**, **cardiogenic** (aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, tetralogy of Fallot, arrhythmias — complete AV block, long QT, paroxysmal tachycardia). **Dangerous signs of cardiogenic syncope:** fainting during physical exertion, preceding palpitations, fainting while lying down, sudden death at a young age in the family — ECG, Holter and echocardiography are needed.
s5783 Skill 1. Auscultation of the heart
s5784 A warm, quiet room; warm the stethoscope; the child lies on the back, the doctor on the right.
s5785 Assess the rhythm and HR (if necessary, palpate the pulse simultaneously to detect a pulse deficit).
s5786 Listen in order: apex → 2nd space right → 2nd space left → base of the xiphoid process → Botkin–Erb point.
s5787 At each point identify S1 and S2 (S1 coincides with the carotid pulse) and assess their loudness and ratio, splitting and extra sounds.
s5788 If there is a murmur — describe it by all criteria.
s5789 Repeat auscultation lying on the left side, standing, and after exertion.
s5790 Listen over the great vessels (carotid and subclavian arteries, neck veins).
s5791 Skill 2. Examining the pulse
s5792 Palpate both radial arteries simultaneously with the 2nd–4th fingers and assess synchrony and equality.
s5793 Count the rate in one arm for 1 minute.
s5794 Assess the rhythm; in arrhythmia repeat while the breath is held.
s5795 Determine the tension, filling, size and shape.
s5796 Skill 3. Measuring arterial pressure
s5797 Choose an age-appropriate cuff; the child has been sitting quietly for 5 minutes.
s5798 Place the cuff 2 cm above the antecubital fossa; the arm at heart level.
s5799 Find the brachial artery and place the stethoscope.
s5800 Inflate to 30–40 mm above the point at which the radial pulse disappears; deflate at 2–3 mm per second.
s5801 Record the systolic and diastolic BP; repeat 2–3 times and take the average.
s5802 Measure in the other arm and in the legs.
s5803 Assess by the formula and centile tables.
s5804 Skill 4. Performing the Martinet (or Shalkov) functional test
s5805 Measure the pulse (over 10 seconds) and BP at rest.
s5806 Ask the child to do 20 deep squats in 30 seconds (or the age-appropriate Shalkov load).
s5807 Measure the pulse and BP immediately after the exercise, then repeat every minute for 3–5 minutes.
s5808 Calculate the percentage increase in pulse, the change in BP and the recovery time.
s5809 Assess the reaction as favorable or unfavorable and write the conclusion.
s5810 Skill 5. Analyzing an ECG
s5811 Check the voltage and paper speed.
s5812 Determine the rhythm source (is there a P before each QRS).
s5813 Calculate the HR from the RR interval.
s5814 Determine the electrical axis (from the QRS in standard leads I and III).
s5815 Measure the duration of P, PQ, QRS and QT; assess ST and T.
s5816 Compare the results with the age norm and write the conclusion (e.g., “Sinus rhythm, HR 92/min, respiratory arrhythmia, normal electrical axis — age norm”).

s6022 A 7-year-old child at a preventive check-up. No complaints, physical development appropriate for age. The cardiac borders are normal. On auscultation a short, soft systolic murmur of musical timbre is heard at the apex and at Botkin's point, not radiating anywhere; it is heard well lying down and becomes much weaker on standing and after 10 squats. The heart sounds are clear. ECG — age norm. CBC — Hb 128 g/l.
s6023 1) How do you assess the murmur? 2) Which signs confirm this conclusion? 3) With which investigation can it be definitively confirmed?
s6024 1) A functional (innocent, physiological) systolic murmur. 2) Only systolic, short, soft, of musical timbre, non-radiating, changing with body position and exertion (decreasing on standing and after exertion), the heart sounds and cardiac borders are unchanged, the ECG is normal, no complaints, no anemia. 3) Echocardiography — confirms the absence of organic changes (minor anomalies, e.g., a false chord, may be found).
s6025 An 11-year-old girl had tonsillitis 2 weeks ago. She now complains of weakness, dyspnea and unpleasant sensations in the heart area. On examination: pale skin, HR 118/min (temperature 37.2 °C), soft pulse. The apex beat is weakened, diffuse and displaced to the left. The cardiac border is enlarged 1.5 cm to the left. The heart sounds are muffled, S1 weakened at the apex, “gallop” rhythm, a soft systolic murmur at the apex. ECG: PQ 0.22 s, ST depression, negative T in V5–6.
s6026 1) Which syndrome? 2) How do you assess the tachycardia? 3) What is the origin of the murmur? 4) Explain the ECG changes.
s6027 1) The syndrome of myocardial damage (myocarditis, probably rheumatic — after tonsillitis). 2) The tachycardia does not correspond to the temperature (at 37.2 °C the pulse should be only slightly raised) — typical of myocarditis. 3) An organic-functional (muscular) murmur — relative mitral insufficiency due to left ventricular dilatation and reduced tone of the papillary muscles. 4) 1st-degree AV block (PQ longer than normal for 11 years), ST depression and negative T — disturbed repolarization in the myocardium (inflammatory and dystrophic changes).
s6028 In a 14-year-old adolescent, BP of 142/88 mm Hg was found at a school medical check-up. On repeated measurement 3 times (on different days) the result was 138–145/85–90 mm. Height 168 cm, weight 82 kg. Complains of morning headaches. His father has hypertension. The pulses in the arms and legs are equal, BP in the legs is 155/90. Urinalysis is normal.
s6029 1) How do you assess the BP? 2) Which investigations are needed? 3) Is the hypertension likely to be primary or secondary? Justify.
s6030 1) The average BP for 14 years is ≈118/74 mm; the values obtained are above the 95th centile and confirmed 3 times — arterial hypertension. 2) ABPM (to exclude “white coat”), ECG and echocardiography (left ventricular hypertrophy), fundoscopy, ultrasound (Doppler) of the kidneys and renal arteries, blood creatinine, electrolytes, glucose, lipids, thyroid hormones. 3) Primary (essential) is likely: adolescence, obesity (BMI ≈29), hereditary predisposition, BP in the legs higher than in the arms (no coarctation), urine unchanged. But secondary causes (renal, endocrine) must be excluded by investigation.
