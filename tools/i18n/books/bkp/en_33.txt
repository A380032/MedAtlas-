s6729 In young children with iron deficiency, intestinal iron absorption **does not increase compensatorily** (unlike adults) — this is why they are the group most prone to iron deficiency anemia.
s6730 The crossovers: **“5 days — 5 years”**. Between the two crossovers (**from 5 days to 5 years**) **lymphocytes predominate** in the differential — this is normal for children and is not regarded as “lymphocytosis”.
s6731 The laboratory triad of iron deficiency anemia: **hypochromia** (color index <0.85, MCH ↓) + **microcytosis** (MCV <80 fl) + **reduced ferritin and serum iron** (raised TIBC). In the blood smear — **the “PHA” syndrome**: poikilocytosis, hypochromia, anisocytosis.

s6733 Acute leukemia must be distinguished from **a leukemoid reaction**: in a leukemoid reaction there is no “leukemic hiatus”, intermediate and mature forms are present, there are no specific clinical signs (bone pain, leukemids), and the blood picture recovers after the underlying disease is treated. If in doubt — **bone marrow aspiration**.
s6734 11.1. Development of hematopoiesis
s6735 **Blood** is a liquid tissue; it consists of plasma and formed elements — **erythrocytes, leukocytes and platelets**. It supplies the tissues with oxygen and nutrients and performs metabolic and protective functions.
s6736 The three periods of intrauterine hematopoiesis:
s6737 **The embryonic (megaloblastic, yolk sac) period** — from **the 2nd–3rd week** of pregnancy; in the blood islands of the yolk sac, large nucleated cells — **megaloblasts** (primary erythroblasts) with embryonic hemoglobin — are formed.
s6738 **The hepatic period** — begins **from week 6–7** and reaches its maximum **at 5 months**; erythroblasts and erythrocytes are formed in the liver. **From 3–4 months the spleen** also joins in (erythro-, granulo-, megakaryocytopoiesis); active lymphopoiesis **from week 20**.
s6739 **The bone marrow (medullary) period** — forms **from 4–5 months** and gradually becomes the main hematopoietic organ.
s6740 In line with these periods there are **three types of hemoglobin**: **embryonic (HbP)**, **fetal (HbF)** and **adult (HbA)**. HbP and HbF have **a high affinity for oxygen** — this facilitates the transfer of oxygen to the fetus. At birth HbF makes up **60–85%** of the total hemoglobin and is gradually replaced by HbA: **≈15% at 1 year**, **≤2% at 3 years**.

s6745 **After birth** hematopoiesis in the liver stops; in the spleen only lymphocytes and monocytes are formed, and it destroys aged erythrocytes and platelets. The main organ forming all the formed elements except lymphocytes is **the red bone marrow**.
s6746 In the newborn **all the flat and tubular bones** are filled with red bone marrow; from the first months of life the red marrow is gradually replaced by **yellow (fatty)** marrow; **at 12–15 years** hematopoiesis persists only in **the flat bones** and the epiphyses of the tubular bones.
s6747 In the newborn and young child the bone marrow is **functionally labile**: under adverse influences (severe infection, anemia) it is quickly exhausted or **hematopoiesis may revert to the embryonic type** (extramedullary foci — enlargement of the liver and spleen, young cells in the peripheral blood).
s6748 **Sites of bone marrow aspiration:** in the newborn — **the calcaneus**; up to 1 year — **the (proximal) epiphysis of the tibia**; in older children — **the iliac crest**. Sternal puncture is now rarely used in children. **Trephine biopsy** (from the ilium, under anesthesia) gives more accurate information on the structure of the bone marrow.
s6749 **Life span of blood cells:** erythrocytes — **≈120 days** (in the newborn, erythrocytes with HbF — 60–90 days); platelets — **9–11 days**; neutrophils — a few hours in the blood, ≈14 days in total; eosinophils — 8–12 days; monocytes circulate in the blood for ≈12 hours and become macrophages in the tissues; lymphocytes — from a few days to years.
s6750 11.2. Hematopoietic factors and iron metabolism
s6751 Hematopoietic factors:
s6752 **erythropoietin** — a glycoprotein hormone, **90% produced in the kidneys** in response to hypoxia; it stimulates the proliferation of erythroid cells and hemoglobin synthesis;
s6753 **thrombopoietin** — accelerates megakaryocytopoiesis; **colony-stimulating factors** and interleukins regulate leukopoiesis;
s6754 **folic acid and vitamin B~12~** — necessary for DNA synthesis; vitamin B12 is absorbed in the small intestine with the help of **the gastric intrinsic factor of Castle**;
s6755 **iron** — part of heme, ensures oxygen transport, is a component of many enzymes, and is necessary for immune function.
s6756 **The iron store** accumulates in the fetus from the mother via the placenta, especially at **weeks 28–32** of pregnancy, unidirectionally against the concentration gradient. For this reason the store is small in **preterm babies**, twins and those born after a complicated pregnancy. After birth the main sources of iron are food (in the first half-year — **breast milk**, from which up to **50%** of the iron is absorbed) and endogenous stores. A child's daily requirement is **0.5–1.2 mg** (more in adolescents).
s6757 **Stages of iron metabolism:** Fe2+ is absorbed in the intestine → oxidized to Fe3+ → bound to **transferrin** and transported to the bone marrow, depots and enzymes → in the bone marrow **Fe + protoporphyrin = heme; heme + globin = hemoglobin**. The store — in the form of **ferritin** and **hemosiderin** in the liver, muscles and bone marrow macrophages.

s6770 11.3. The hemostatic system
s6771 **Hemostasis** — the system that keeps the blood in a liquid state and stops bleeding when a vessel is damaged. Three main components:
s6772 **The vascular component** — reflex **local angiospasm** on injury; the endothelium normally produces prostacyclin and prevents platelet adhesion.
s6773 **The platelet component** — platelet attachment to the damaged endothelium (**adhesion**) and to each other (**aggregation**) — the primary platelet plug; platelets contain more than 10 clotting factors (designated by Arabic numerals).
s6774 **The plasma (coagulation) component** — **13 plasma factors** (designated by Roman numerals) are activated in sequence and a **fibrin** clot forms.
s6775 Then **retraction** of the clot (normally 0.3–0.5, i.e., 60–75%) and **fibrinolysis** — dissolution of the clot and restoration of vascular patency — take place. The anticoagulant system — antithrombin III, proteins C and S, heparin.
s6776 **Features of hemostasis in newborns:** in the first days of life **the vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X)**, as well as factors XI and XII, the physiological anticoagulants and the components of fibrinolysis are **physiologically reduced**; the system is balanced, but at a low level. In preterm babies these parameters are even lower — hemorrhagic and thrombotic syndromes, especially **DIC syndrome**, develop quickly. Early cord clamping and late first breastfeeding further reduce the vitamin K-dependent factors.
s6777 **Hemorrhagic disease** of the newborn (vitamin K deficiency) presents on days 2–5 of life with melena, blood in vomit, and bleeding from the umbilicus and into the skin. To prevent it, all newborns are given **vitamin K (phytomenadione) intramuscularly** after birth.
s6778 11.4. Age-related features of peripheral blood
s6779 Blood of the newborn
s6780 **The blood volume** in the newborn is **≈80–90 ml/kg** (≈8–9% of body mass; 90–100 ml/kg in preterm babies), in adults **≈65–70 ml/kg** (≈7%). Some older textbooks give 14–15% for the newborn.
s6781 In **the first days** of life hemoglobin (**180–240 g/l**), erythrocytes (**5–7×10^12^/l**), the color index (**up to 1.1**) and hematocrit (**≈54%**) are high — the result of intensive erythropoiesis in response to hypoxia in utero and during delivery.
s6782 After birth external respiration is established and hypoxia is replaced by hyperoxia → erythropoietin decreases → erythropoiesis slows; erythrocytes with HbF have a short life span and are easily hemolyzed → **physiological jaundice** (from day 2–3).
s6783 The erythrocytes are polychromatophilic, **anisocytosis** (macrocytes predominate); **normoblasts** may be present in the blood; **reticulocytes** are **2.2–4.2%** in the first days (later 0.6–0.8% — 1%).
s6784 **Leukocytes** are **10–30×10^9^/l** in the first days, 10–12×10^9^/l after 2 weeks; the differential shows **neutrophilia** and a left shift (down to myelocytes).
s6785 Platelets **150–400×10^9^/l**; **ESR low — 1–2 mm/h**; the bleeding time is as in adults; the clotting time is slightly faster or normal.
s6786 Physiological crossovers of the leukocyte differential
s6787 At birth neutrophils are **60–65%**, lymphocytes **20–30%**. Neutrophils decrease rapidly and lymphocytes increase, and on **days 4–6** they **become equal** (40–45%) — **the first physiological crossover**. Then lymphocytes predominate (up to 60–65% until 1 year, 50–60% until 5 years). From 3–4 years neutrophils gradually increase and **at 4–6 years (on average at 5 years)** they become equal again — **the second physiological crossover**; then neutrophils predominate, and at 12–14 years the differential approaches that of adults (neutrophils 60–65%, lymphocytes 25–30%).

s6790 Blood of children under 1 year and older children
s6791 In the 1st year erythrocytes fall to **3.5–4.5×10^12^/l**, hemoglobin to **110–120 g/l**, and the color index below 1.0; **at 3–6 months** the parameters may fall to their lowest values — **physiological (early) anemia** (rapid growth, increase in blood volume, relative immaturity of erythropoiesis, low iron in the diet).
s6792 **Preterm babies** develop **early anemia** (at 1.5–2 months; low erythropoietin, rapidly increasing blood volume) and **late anemia** (at 4–5 months; iron deficiency — hypochromic); late anemia can be prevented with iron preparations.
s6793 In the 1st year leukocytes are **8–12×10^9^/l**, with lymphocytes predominating in the differential; ESR **4–7 (5–8) mm/h**.
s6794 **From 2 years to adolescence** the composition of peripheral blood approaches that of adults: hemoglobin **120–140 g/l**, erythrocytes **4.0–4.5×10^12^/l**.

s6822 11.5. Methods of examining the blood system
s6823 **Specific complaints:** bleeding (from the nose, gums, gastrointestinal tract, menstrual), hemorrhages (“bruises” on the skin, hematomas, hemarthroses), enlarged lymph nodes, pallor of the skin and mucous membranes. **Nonspecific complaints:** weakness, rapid fatigue, dizziness, headache, dyspnea on exertion, reduced appetite, **distorted taste and smell** (eating chalk, soil, ice; liking the smell of gasoline, paint), hair loss, brittle nails, bone and joint pain, fever, sweating, weight loss.
s6824 **History:** the neonatal period (term birth, weight, neonatal jaundice — onset, degree, duration); feeding (breast milk, timing of complementary foods, early feeding with cow's milk, meat intake); past illnesses, medication; **family history** — blood diseases, jaundice, gallstone disease, splenectomy, hemorrhagic syndrome in relatives (hemophilia — in the mother's male relatives), early (before 50 years) infarction and stroke (thrombophilia).
