s1945 An 8-month-old child was brought to hospital with fever (39.4 °C) for 2 days and repeated vomiting. On examination the child is lethargic and gives a high-pitched, monotonous cry when stimulated. The anterior fontanelle is bulging and tense, with increased pulsation. When lifted under the arms, he draws the legs up to the abdomen and holds them there. The neck muscles are stiff.
s1946 1) Which syndrome should be considered? 2) Indicate the symptoms. 3) What investigation is needed to establish the diagnosis, and what result is expected?
s1947 1) The meningeal syndrome — acute meningitis is suspected. 2) General cerebral signs (fever, repeated vomiting, lethargy, monotonous cry) and meningeal signs: a bulging anterior fontanelle, a positive Lesage's sign, neck stiffness. 3) Lumbar puncture and analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid: the pressure is increased; in purulent meningitis the fluid is turbid, the cytosis is in the thousands with predominance of neutrophils, and the protein is sharply increased; in serous meningitis the fluid is clear with lymphocytic cytosis. A blood count and CSF culture; the child is treated urgently.
s1948 A 2-year-old child has ARVI. Temperature 39.2 °C. The child is lethargic, the skin is pale and mottled, the hands and feet are cold, the nails bluish, HR 160 per minute. According to the mother, he had a seizure against the background of fever at 1 year of age.
s1949 1) Determine the degree and clinical type of fever. 2) Why should the child be included in the risk group? 3) What is the physician's management?
s1950 1) High fever (39.1–41.0 °C); clinical type — “white” (pale) fever: spasm of the peripheral vessels (mottled skin, cold limbs, acrocyanosis, tachycardia). 2) There is a history of a febrile seizure (within the age range of 6 months – 5 years) and “white” fever — the risk of a recurrent seizure is high. 3) Lower the temperature immediately (paracetamol or ibuprofen at the age-appropriate dose) and at the same time warm the limbs and relieve the vascular spasm (vasodilator drugs prescribed by the physician); monitor the condition and decide on hospital admission.

s2021 A child's skin is **thin, delicate, rich in vessels, highly absorptive and weakly protective**. That is why it needs strict hygiene and a proper temperature regimen, and ointments are used with caution (for example, ointments containing boric acid, hormones or phenol may cause poisoning).
s2022 Remember the solid elements in order of depth: **“Macule — Papule — Tubercle — Node”** — from the skin surface downward. The cavitary elements by size and contents: **vesicle < 5 mm < bulla**, **pustule — pus**.
s2023 **An erosion heals without a trace, whereas an ulcer heals with a scar** — this is the main criterion for distinguishing them. Small scars remain after chickenpox, irregular deep scars after tuberculosis, and star-shaped scars after a syphilitic gumma.
s2024 **Physiological** jaundice of the newborn appears **on days 2–3** and disappears **by days 7–10** (up to 2–3 weeks in preterm babies). Jaundice appearing **on the 1st day** or persisting **longer than 2 weeks** is always **pathological** (hemolytic disease, infection, biliary atresia).
s2025 **Carotene jaundice** (from eating large amounts of carrots, pumpkin or tangerines) — only **the palms and soles** turn yellow; **the sclerae and mucous membranes never turn yellow**. This is the main difference from true jaundice.
s2026 **Physiological acrocyanosis in the newborn** (bluish palms and soles) is normal in the first 1–2 days and in the cold. If **cyanosis around the mouth** (perioral) increases with crying, sucking and coughing, cardiac or pulmonary pathology should be excluded.
s2027 Fat **is deposited: face → limbs → trunk → abdomen**, and **disappears in the reverse order: abdomen → trunk → limbs → face**. That is why in hypotrophy the face loses weight last (“Bichat's fat pads” persist for a long time).
s2028 Skin **elasticity** concerns only the skin fold (rapid smoothing); **turgor** is the resistance of all soft tissues; **the thickness of the skin fold** reflects the development of the fat layer. Do not confuse these three concepts.
s2029 In a healthy child **no more than 3 groups** are palpable — usually among the **submandibular, cervical, axillary and inguinal** groups: single, no larger than a pea, soft-elastic, mobile, painless. **The submental, supraclavicular and infraclavicular, thoracic, cubital and popliteal** nodes are normally **not palpable**.
s2030 The groups not palpable normally: **“S-S-I-T-C-P”** — **S**ubmental, **S**upraclavicular, **I**nfraclavicular, **T**horacic, **C**ubital, **P**opliteal.
s2031 In newborns and infants the pole of the spleen may be palpable **up to 1 cm** below the costal margin (the abdominal muscles are lax, and the organ is relatively large). In older children a palpable spleen is always **pathological**.
s2032 The rule of percussion: percussion proceeds **from the resonant (clear) note toward the dull note**, and the border is marked along the edge of the finger **facing the resonant note**. The spleen — **quiet percussion**, because it is surrounded by hollow organs that give tympany.
s2033 Causes of splenomegaly — **“HILTA”**: **H**emolysis, **I**nfection, **L**iver (portal hypertension), **T**umor (leukemia, lymphoma), **A**utoimmune and storage diseases.
s2034 4.1. Anatomical and physiological features of the skin
s2035 The skin is the organ that covers the body from the outside and performs the functions of a barrier between the internal and external environment, metabolism, thermoregulation, respiration and sensation. It consists of three layers: **the epidermis**, **the dermis** (papillary and reticular layers) and **the subcutaneous fat** (hypodermis).
s2036 Figure 4.1
s2037 Structure of the skin. I — epidermis: 1 — horny, 2 — lucid, 3 — granular, 4 — spinous, 5 — basal layer; II — dermis: 6 — papillary, 7 — reticular layer; III — subcutaneous fat; 8 — sweat glands, 9 — hair follicle, 10 — sebaceous glands (Navruzova, Geppe)
s2038 Structural features:
s2039 In children under 3 years the skin layers are **1.5–3 times thinner** than in adults; they approach those of adults only **at 7 years**.
s2040 The epidermal cells lie far apart, and the structure is loose; in the newborn the horny layer consists of 2–3 rows and is easily shed.
s2041 The granular layer is poorly developed — the newborn's skin is **translucent and pale pink**.
s2042 **The connection between the epidermis and the dermis is very delicate** (the basement membrane and connective fibers are weak) — in various diseases the epidermis is easily detached and **blisters** form.
s2043 The pH of the skin surface in the newborn is close to neutral and falls by the end of the 1st month; the bactericidal activity of the secretion is weak.
s2044 The capillary network is extensive and well developed — the skin is pink, and absorption and heat loss are high.
s2045 The nerve endings are morphologically immature but functionally mature — pain, tactile and temperature sensation are complete.

s2063 Skin appendages:
s2064 **The sebaceous glands** work already in the fetal period and produce **the cheese-like coating** (vernix caseosa) — it protects the skin from the amniotic fluid and eases passage through the birth canal. The glands work intensively in the 1st year, then their activity decreases and increases again at puberty (acne).
s2065 **The sweat glands** (eccrine) are not fully formed at birth, and their ducts are closed by epithelium. Sweating begins **from weeks 3–4** and is incomplete until 3–4 months; until 3 years sweating occurs only at high temperatures. The sweat glands and the thermoregulatory center mature fully **at 5–7 years**. The apocrine glands begin to work at puberty.
s2066 **Hair.** The newborn has downy hair — **lanugo** — on the shoulders and back (abundant in preterm babies). The hair follicles are poorly developed — furuncles hardly occur in newborns (pseudofurunculosis occurs instead). The eyebrows and eyelashes are poorly developed.
s2067 **The nails** reach the fingertips in a term newborn (a sign of maturity); in the first days of life their growth slows and a “physiological line” forms.
s2068 4.2. Methods of examining the skin
s2069 The examination is carried out by **questioning, inspection, palpation and special tests**.
s2070 Questioning
s2071 The time of appearance of changes in color, a rash, itching, sweating or edema; their relation to food, drugs, infections and chemicals; whether similar episodes have occurred before; allergic and skin diseases in the family are established.
s2072 Inspection
s2073 Inspection is carried out, as far as possible, **in natural daylight** in a warm room. The skin is inspected in sequence **from top to bottom**: the hairy part of the head, face, neck, trunk, **natural folds**, inguinal and femoral regions, palms and soles, the spaces between the fingers.
s2074 Inspection assesses:
s2075 the **color** of the skin and its evenness;
s2076 **moisture**;
s2077 **cleanness** (absence of rash, scaling, scratch marks, hemorrhages);
s2078 the state of the **skin vessels** (venous network);
s2079 the **integrity** of the skin;
s2080 the skin **appendages** — hair and nails.
s2082 Primary morphological elements of the skin
s2083 **Primary elements** appear on unchanged skin. They are **solid** (macule, papule, tubercle, node, wheal) and **cavitary** — filled with serous, hemorrhagic or purulent fluid (vesicle, bulla, pustule).
