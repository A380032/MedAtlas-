s0005 The student should know:

s0007 the structure of the case history and the content of each of its sections;
s0008 the concept of emergency assessment (triage) and the danger signs;
s0009 the order of taking the history of present illness and the life history, and the features of talking with parents and with children of different ages;
s0010 the clinical criteria for assessing the severity of a sick child's condition;
s0011 the order of general inspection: general condition, consciousness, position, body build, stigmas of dysembryogenesis.
s0012 The student should be able to:
s0013 establish contact with the child and the parents and take the history independently;
s0014 take the history of present illness and the life history according to the scheme and draw a conclusion;
s0015 assess the child rapidly (emergency assessment) and identify the danger signs;
s0016 perform a general inspection and assess the severity of the patient's condition;
s0017 record the data obtained correctly in the case history.
s0018 Remember
s0019 A child is not a scaled-down copy of an adult. The child's body is constantly growing and developing, so each age period has its own anatomical and physiological features, its own normal values and its own specific course of disease. A pulse, respiratory rate or blood formula that is normal for an infant is pathological for an older child.
s0020 Memory aid
s0021 The order of questioning and examining a child can be remembered with the English acronym **HELP**: **H** (history) — the history; **E** (examination) — objective examination; **L** (logical deduction) — logical conclusion (syndromal diagnosis); **P** (plan of management) — the plan of investigation and treatment.
s0022 If questioning reveals that an organ is affected, the physician pays more attention to it, but this **does not release him from the obligation to examine all systems fully**.
s0023 The emergency signs are easy to check in the **ABCD** order: **A** (airway) — patency of the airway; **B** (breathing) — breathing (present or absent, severe respiratory distress, cyanosis); **C** (circulation; coma; convulsions) — circulation (shock), coma, convulsions; **D** (dehydration) — severe dehydration.
s0024 The specific feature of the history in pediatrics: the information is obtained mainly **from the parents** (or the child's caregiver) and is supplemented by questions addressed to the child only according to the child's age. In a child under 5 years of age the history is taken almost entirely from the parents.
s0025 **A short history** (from a few hours to 1–2 weeks) indicates an acute process, and **a long history** (weeks, months, years) a chronic disease. In a chronic disease the parents often confuse the duration of the disease with the duration of the last exacerbation — that is why the medical records should be reviewed.
s0026 **Two main criteria** are used to assess the general condition: 1. **The degree of intoxication (toxicosis)** — the main criterion in acute diseases. 2. **The degree of functional impairment of organs and systems** (respiratory, cardiac, renal, hepatic failure) — the main criterion in chronic, long-lasting diseases. In blood diseases the blood count and myelogram, and in kidney diseases the urinalysis and functional tests, are also taken into account.
s0027 The general condition and well-being **are not the same thing**. The general condition is assessed objectively by the physician, and well-being subjectively by the patient. Even with many complaints the general condition may be satisfactory, and vice versa: for example, at the onset of severe poisoning a child may feel well.
s0028 **Up to 5–6** stigmas in one child are considered borderline. If there are more stigmas (**the stigmatization threshold is exceeded**), malformations of the internal organs (heart, genitourinary system) and hereditary syndromes should be sought. The degree of stigmatization is assessed by their total number regardless of location.
s0029 1.1. The subject, aim and tasks of the discipline
s0030 **Propaedeutics** (Greek propaideuo — “I teach beforehand”) is an introduction to the study of a discipline, the part that gives its foundations. **The propaedeutics of childhood diseases** is the introductory branch of pediatrics; it teaches the age-specific features of the child's body, the methods of examining healthy and sick children, and the main signs of diseases.
s0031 The discipline consists of the following parts:
s0032 The anatomical and physiological features of the child's body and the laws of physical and neuropsychological development.
s0033 Methods of questioning the child (taking the history) and of objective examination.
s0034 The basic semiotics of childhood diseases — symptoms and syndromes.
s0035 The rules of feeding a healthy child and drawing up a menu.
s0036 Methods of child care and the basic medical procedures.
s0037 **The aim of the discipline** is to teach the student the features of the child's body according to age and the technique of examining it, and to develop the skill of evaluating the data obtained to identify the affected system and the main syndrome.
s0038 Tasks of the discipline:
s0039 to teach the anatomical and physiological features of children of different ages;
s0040 to master the technique of a complete objective examination of the child (inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation);
s0041 to teach the evaluation of laboratory and instrumental findings according to age;
s0042 to teach the identification of the main symptoms and syndromes and their combination into a syndromal diagnosis;
s0043 to develop the skills of feeding and caring for a healthy child and of writing a case history.
s0044 A brief history of pediatrics
s0045 The first information on children's medicine is found in Hippocrates' (4th century BC) work “On the Nature of the Child”. In the Middle Ages, scholars of the East made a great contribution to this field. **Abu Bakr al-Razi** (865–925) wrote “A Treatise on Smallpox and Measles” and “Diseases of Children”, and was the first to introduce keeping a daily record of the patient's condition in hospital — the basis of today's case history. **Abu Ali ibn Sina** (980–1037) devoted special chapters of the “Canon of Medicine” to the care of the newborn, breastfeeding, choosing a wet nurse and the treatment of childhood diseases.
s0046 Pediatrics took shape as an independent discipline in the 19th century. The founders of the Russian school of pediatrics are N.F. Filatov, N.P. Gundobin, A.A. Kisel, G.N. Speransky, M.S. Maslov and A.F. Tur. N.P. Gundobin created the classification of the periods of childhood that is still used today.
s0047 Professors R.S. Gershenovich, K.G. Titov, S.Sh. Shamsiyev, S.N. Yo‘ldosheva, M.A. Mirzamuhamedov, M.R. Nuritdinov, F.X. Nazarmuhamedov, O.S. Mahmudov, X.A. Yunusova and T.A. Daminov made a great contribution to the development of the school of pediatrics in Uzbekistan. Professor **S.Sh. Shamsiyev** headed departments of the propaedeutics of childhood diseases in 1951–1989 and is the author of works such as “Acute Pneumonia in Children” and “Clinical Electrocardiography in Children”. In 1972 the Central Asian Medical Pediatric Institute was opened.

s0078 1.2. Medical ethics and deontology in pediatrics
s0079 The pediatrician works with two parties at the same time — the child and the parents. That is why the physician's communication skills directly affect the accuracy of the diagnosis.
s0080 Before the examination, **contact should be established** with the child: ask an older child about his name, school and interests; distract a small child with a toy.
s0081 A young child is **not asked for permission** to be examined (he usually says “no”) — the physician acts confidently but gently.
s0082 Words that convey a poor prognosis or that the treatment was wrong are not used in front of the child and the parents.
s0083 The child is addressed by name and the parents respectfully; accusatory phrases such as “Why didn't you bring him earlier?” are not used.
s0084 **Confidentiality** is maintained in conversations with adolescents; it is possible to talk first with the parents and then separately with the adolescent.
s0085 Before an examination with a group of students, the consent of the child (if older) or of the parents is obtained.
s0086 **The physician's appearance and hands:** a clean gown, hair under a cap, hands washed before examining each child, warm, with short nails. The physician should be patient, calm and kind.
s0087 1.3. Methods of examining the child
s0088 The examination of a child is divided into two groups of methods:
s0089 **Main methods:** subjective — questioning (history); objective (physical) — inspection (inspectio), palpation (palpatio), percussion (percussio), auscultation (auscultatio).
s0090 **Additional methods:** laboratory (blood, urine and stool tests and others) and instrumental (radiography, ultrasound, ECG, endoscopy and others).
s0091 The order of questioning and examining a child can be remembered with the English acronym **HELP**:
s0092 **H** (history) — the history;
s0093 **E** (examination) — objective examination;
s0094 **L** (logical deduction) — logical conclusion (syndromal diagnosis);
s0095 **P** (plan of management) — the plan of investigation and treatment.
s0096 The objective examination is carried out in the following sequence: general inspection → skin and subcutaneous fat → lymph nodes → musculoskeletal system → respiratory system → cardiovascular system → digestive system → urinary system → nervous system and sense organs → endocrine system. Each system is examined in the order **inspection → palpation → percussion → auscultation**.
s0097 1.4. The concept of emergency assessment (triage)
s0098 Every sick child arriving at a hospital or an admission room is first assessed **rapidly — within 1–2 minutes**. The aim here is not a detailed diagnosis but the detection of a life-threatening condition and the setting of the order of care. This process is called **triage** (French trier — to sort). The World Health Organization (WHO) has developed the **ETAT** system for children (Emergency Triage Assessment and Treatment).

s0113 The emergency signs are easy to check in the **ABCD** order:
s0114 **A** (airway) — patency of the airway;
s0115 **B** (breathing) — breathing (present or absent, severe respiratory distress, cyanosis);
s0116 **C** (circulation; coma; convulsions) — circulation (shock), coma, convulsions;
s0117 **D** (dehydration) — severe dehydration.
s0118 **Rapid assessment of consciousness — the AVPU scale.** In triage, consciousness is assessed with a simple four-level scale:
s0119 **A** (alert) — the child is alert and responds appropriately to the surroundings;
s0120 **V** (voice) — responds only to voice;
s0121 **P** (pain) — responds only to pain;
s0122 **U** (unresponsive) — does not respond to anything.
s0123 Responding only to pain (P) or not responding (U) is a sign of coma, that is, an emergency.
s0124 **General danger signs.** In the WHO and UNICEF Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) program, the following **general danger signs** are checked in every sick child aged 2 months to 5 years:
s0125 The child is unable to drink or breastfeed.
s0126 The child vomits everything.
s0127 The child has had convulsions during this illness.
s0128 The child is lethargic or unconscious.
s0129 The child is convulsing now.
s0130 A child with at least one general danger sign is considered **severely ill**: he is given immediate care and urgently admitted to hospital. For example, if a 1-year-old child has fever and cough and refuses to breastfeed, this is not a simple cold but may be a sign of severe pneumonia or sepsis.
s0131 1.5. Taking the history
s0132 **The history (anamnesis)** (Greek anamnesis — recollection) is the body of information about the patient and the disease obtained by questioning the patient or a person who knows the patient well.
s0133 Components of the history:
s0134 Personal data.
s0135 Complaints — the main reason for seeking care.
s0136 History of present illness (anamnesis morbi).
s0137 Life history (anamnesis vitae): obstetric, neonatal, feeding, developmental, past illnesses, immunizations, allergic, hereditary (family), social and living conditions, and epidemiological history.
s0138 Personal data
s0139 Surname, first name and patronymic; date of birth (exact age — years, months, and days for a newborn); home address; the parents' names, ages, occupations and places of work; whether the child attends a kindergarten or school; date of admission; the diagnosis of the referring institution. The home address gives information about the epidemiological environment (water supply, sewerage), and the parents' occupation about the family's social level and occupational hazards.
s0140 Complaints
s0141 Complaints are the subjective signs of the disease. First the **main** and then the **general** complaints are recorded concisely; the complaints on admission and at the time of examination are indicated separately. Each complaint is clarified in detail: site, character, intensity, duration, when it appears, what makes it worse and what relieves it.
s0142 Difficulties in obtaining complaints from children:
s0143 a young child cannot express complaints because of age or impaired consciousness, and the parents only report that signs have appeared (for example, “he is crying and pulling his legs up to his tummy”);
s0144 adolescents may exaggerate their condition (aggravation), play it down (dissimulation) or feign a nonexistent disease (simulation).
s0145 Questions are asked in language the child and the parents understand; a question should not contain a ready answer (not “Does your tummy hurt on the right?” but “Where does it hurt? Can you show me?”).
s0146 History of present illness (anamnesis morbi)
s0147 The history of present illness establishes:
s0148 What the child was like before the illness.
s0149 When and how the illness began: **acutely** (within hours, 1–2 days) or **gradually**.
s0150 What the illness is thought to be associated with: chilling, contact with a sick person, a new food, taking a medicine, an injury.
