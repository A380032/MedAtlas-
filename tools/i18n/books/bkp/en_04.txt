s0586 1) What general danger signs does the child have? 2) To which triage group does the child belong? 3) Assess his general condition.
s0587 1) There are two general danger signs: the child is unable to breastfeed and vomits everything; in addition, lethargy is also a general danger sign. 2) Chest indrawing and tachypnea indicate severe respiratory distress, that is, an emergency (E) sign; lethargy is a priority sign. The child belongs to the emergency group, and treatment is started immediately. 3) The general condition is severe: obvious intoxication (lethargy, reduced consciousness — weak response to voice, V on AVPU) and signs of respiratory failure. s0588 Case 2
s0589 A 10-year-old girl came with the complaint of “tummy ache”. Her mother is very anxious: her daughter says that her tummy hurts every morning before going to school. There is no pain at weekends. Objective examination revealed no changes; the condition is satisfactory.
s0590 1) Which part of the history gives an important direction for the diagnosis? 2) How should the conversation with the child be organized? 3) Explain the difference between the condition and well-being.
s0591 1) The appearance of pain only on school mornings and its absence at weekends suggest psychogenic (functional) pain; the social history (the school environment, performance, relations with classmates, the family situation) should be taken in detail. At the same time, organic causes (digestive diseases) are excluded. 2) The conversation begins with an interesting, non-frightening topic; it is also useful to talk with the child alone, without the mother; open questions are asked (“How does your day at school go? What upsets you?”); the child is asked to show where it hurts. 3) The condition (the physician's objective assessment) is satisfactory; the well-being (the girl's subjective sensation) may be unsatisfactory because of the pain. It is natural for these two concepts not to coincide.
s0592 Case 3
s0593 On assessing a newborn at 1 minute: the body is pink and the limbs bluish; heart rate 110 per minute; grimaces when a nasal catheter is inserted; the limbs are slightly flexed; breathing is slow and irregular, and the cry is weak.
s0594 1) Score by the Apgar scale. 2) Draw a conclusion. 3) When is the reassessment performed?
s0595 1) Skin color — 1 point, heart rate (more than 100) — 2 points, reflex irritability (grimace) — 1 point, muscle tone (slightly flexed) — 1 point, respiration (slow, irregular) — 1 point. Total: 6 points. 2) Mild asphyxia (6–7 points). 3) The reassessment is performed at 5 minutes; if the score is below 7, the assessment is continued every 5 minutes up to 20 minutes, and resuscitation measures are taken.

s0658 All anthropometric measurements are made **in the morning, on an empty stomach**, after voiding urine and stool, with the same instruments. The measurements are repeated regularly: **under 1 year — every month; 1–3 years — every quarter; 3–7 years — twice a year; 7–17 years — once a year**.
s0659 For height, “**50 – 75 – 100 – 130 – 150**”: 50 cm at birth, 75 cm at 1 year, 100 cm at 4 years (“one meter”), 130 cm at 8 years, 150 cm at 12 years. Height **doubles by 4 years** and **triples by 12 years**.
s0660 For weight, the “**3 – 6 – 12**” rule: at 1 year the weight is **3 times** the birth weight; at 6–7 years it is **2 times** the weight at 1 year; at 12 years it is **3 times** the weight at 1 year.
s0661 Order of centile assessment: 1) determine into which corridor each of height, weight and chest circumference falls; 2) **the level of physical development is determined by the height corridor**; 3) **harmony** is assessed by the difference between the corridors: a difference of **0–1** corridor — harmonious; **2** corridors — disharmonious; **3 or more** — sharply disharmonious development. Corridors 2 and 6 are borderline (require follow-up); in corridors 1 and 7 the probability of pathology is high, and an in-depth examination is needed.
s0662 Main definitions: **Live born** — a baby that has breathed or shows other signs of life (heartbeat, pulsation of the umbilical vessels, voluntary muscle movement) (WHO). **Term** — born from **37 completed weeks to 42 weeks** of gestation (37^0^–41^6^ weeks). **Post-term** — born at **42 completed weeks or later**. **Preterm (premature)** — born **before 37 completed weeks**. **Gestational age** — the number of completed weeks from conception to birth.
s0663 Most transitional states follow the “**3–7 rule**”: they begin around **day 3** and pass by **days 7–10** (weight loss, jaundice, sexual crisis, uric acid infarction). An earlier onset or longer duration is a sign of pathology.
s0664 On **the Apgar** score, the higher the score, the better the condition (10 — ideal). On **the Silverman** score it is the opposite: the higher the score, the more severe the respiratory distress (0 — ideal).
s0665 2.1. The concept of physical development and the factors affecting it
s0666 **Physical development** is the set of morphological and functional features reflecting the process of growth and maturation of the body. In clinical pediatrics it is understood as the child's growth in length, weight gain, development of body parts and biological maturation in the different periods of childhood. Physical development is one of the most important criteria of a child's health.
s0667 Four groups of signs of physical development are distinguished:
s0668 **Anthropometric** — length (height), weight, and the circumferences of the head, chest, arm, thigh and calf.
s0669 **Functional** — vital capacity of the lungs, hand grip and back strength, physical working capacity.
s0670 **Somatoscopic** — shape of the spine, degree of fat deposition, muscle development, posture, shape of the legs and feet.
s0671 **Biological** — dental formula, body proportions, ossification centers, secondary sexual characteristics.

s0689 Acceleration and retardation
s0690 **Acceleration** (Latin acceleratio — speeding up) is the speeding up of children's growth and biological maturation compared with previous generations. The term was introduced in 1935 by the German scientist E. Koch. In the 20th century the length of newborns increased by 1–2 cm, that of 1-year-old children by 4–5 cm, and that of 15-year-old adolescents by an average of 20 cm over 100 years. Early eruption of permanent teeth, early sexual maturation and early menarche in girls are also signs of acceleration.
s0691 **Harmonious acceleration** — growth in height and biological maturation speed up together.
s0692 **Disharmonious acceleration** — only one indicator speeds up (for example, height increases rapidly, while sexual maturation occurs on time).
s0693 **Retardation** is a slowing of the rate of physical development. Causes: reduced physical activity (sitting for long periods at a computer), the parents' smoking, alcohol and drug use (intrauterine growth restriction), children's harmful habits. The alternation of periods of acceleration and retardation is called **the secular trend**.
s0694 2.2. Methods of assessing physical development and the technique of anthropometry
s0695 Physical development is assessed by three methods:
s0696 **Somatoscopic** — visual assessment of body build and proportions.
s0697 **Somatometric (anthropometric)** — measuring body weight, length (height), and head and chest circumferences.
s0698 **Physiometric** — measuring functional indicators with instruments (spirometry, dynamometry).
s0699 Measuring length (height)
s0700 The length of children **under 2 years** is measured **lying down with a horizontal length board** (a board 80 cm long and 40 cm wide). The child is placed on the back with the head touching the fixed headboard. The head is held so that the lower margin of the orbit and the upper margin of the tragus are **in one vertical plane**. An assistant or the mother holds the head. With the right hand the physician straightens the legs by pressing lightly on the knees, and with the left hand moves the sliding footboard against the heels (the feet flexed at a right angle). The distance between the two boards equals the child's length.
s0701 The height of children **over 2 years** is measured **standing with a vertical stadiometer**. The child stands with the back to the stadiometer, touching it with **the heels, buttocks, the area between the shoulder blades and the back of the head**; the arms hang along the body, the heels are together and the toes apart. The lower margin of the orbit and the upper margin of the tragus should be **in one horizontal plane**. The sliding headpiece is lowered without pressing on the head.

s0707 Children under 3 years are weighed on **electronic baby scales** with a maximum load of up to 15 kg. s0708 Measuring the head and chest circumferences
s0709 **Head circumference** — the tape measure is passed at the back over **the occipital protuberance** and at the front over **the brow ridges**.
s0710 **Chest circumference** — the tape is passed at the back below **the lower angles of the shoulder blades** and at the front at the level of **the nipples**. In older children the chest circumference is measured three times — during quiet breathing, at maximal inspiration and at maximal expiration; the difference shows the chest excursion.
