s0151 The first signs and their subsequent course (dynamics).
s0152 What investigations were done and their results (the outpatient card and discharge summaries should be reviewed).
s0153 What treatment was received (including self-treatment) and its effect.
s0154 The present condition.
s0155 After the history of present illness has been taken, a preliminary assumption is made about which organ or system is affected and about the course of the disease (acute or chronic).
s0156 Life history (anamnesis vitae)
s0157 The life history is taken in two ways depending on the child's age: **in young children (under 3 years)** in detail, starting from the mother's pregnancy and delivery, and in older children more briefly.
s0158 1. Obstetric (antenatal and intranatal) history:
s0159 from which pregnancy and which delivery the child was born; how previous pregnancies ended (abortion, miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm delivery);
s0160 how the first and second halves of pregnancy proceeded: toxicosis, gestosis, anemia, threatened miscarriage, the mother's illnesses (especially infections), medicines taken, harmful habits (smoking, alcohol), occupational hazards, nutrition;
s0161 the delivery: at term or preterm, its duration and complications, whether a cesarean section, obstetric forceps or a vacuum extractor was used, the duration of the period after rupture of the membranes.
s0162 Spontaneous miscarriages in the mother and threatened abortion may indicate hereditary pathology or intrauterine infection. Gestosis, abnormal labor and a low Apgar score lead to cerebral hypoxia, which later becomes the basis for diseases of the nervous system.
s0163 **2. Neonatal period:** whether the child cried at once and what the cry was like (loud or weak); whether resuscitation was needed; the Apgar score; the birth weight, length and head and chest circumferences; when the baby was put to the breast and how he sucked; when the umbilical stump fell off and how the umbilical wound healed; the degree and duration of physiological jaundice; the physiological weight loss and its recovery; illnesses in the newborn period; on which day and with what weight the baby was discharged from the maternity hospital.
s0164 **3. Feeding history:** breast, mixed or formula feeding; for how many months the baby was exclusively breastfed; when he was weaned; the type and amount of formula; when and in what order complementary foods were introduced; whether vitamin D was given to prevent rickets; when the child moved to the family table; present diet, appetite.
s0165 Early formula feeding, non-adapted formulas and failure to introduce complementary foods on time lead to allergic diseases, digestive diseases, anemia, rickets and hypotrophy. For example, in celiac disease the arrest of weight gain begins a few weeks (usually 1–2 months) after gluten-containing cereals are introduced.
s0166 **4. Physical and neuropsychological development:** when the birth weight doubled; the weight and length at 1 year; when the first teeth erupted and the number of teeth at 1 year; when the child began to hold up his head, smile, babble, roll over, sit, crawl, stand and walk; the first words and phrases; in an older child, school performance and behavior in a group.
s0167 **5. Past illnesses:** what illnesses the child had before and after 1 year of age, their severity and complications; rickets, anemia, diatheses; infectious diseases; operations, injuries, blood transfusions (risk of hepatitis B and C and HIV infection).
s0168 **6. Immunizations:** whether the child has been immunized according to the national immunization schedule; reactions and complications after vaccination; medical contraindications; the results of the Mantoux test.
s0169 **7. Allergic history:** reactions to drugs, foods, household and other allergens (rash, swelling, bronchospasm). This information is necessary for choosing drugs and preventing complications.
s0170 **8. Hereditary (family) history:** the parents' ages and health; chronic, hereditary, allergic, endocrine and mental diseases, tuberculosis, syphilis and alcoholism in the family; consanguineous marriage; the other children in the family and their health, children who have died and the causes of death. The hereditary history is drawn up as **a pedigree (family tree)** covering at least three generations.
s0171 **9. Social and living conditions:** the family's financial situation; housing conditions (area, lighting, dampness, heating, ventilation, number of occupants); who looks after the child; whether the child has a separate bed; daily routine, sleep, walks; bathing; smoking in the family.
s0172 **10. Epidemiological history:** contact with an infectious patient (influenza, ARVI, intestinal infections, tuberculosis, hepatitis) during the last 3 weeks; the quality of food products and water; whether the child has traveled recently. The epidemiological history completes the life history and decides to which ward the patient will be admitted.
s0173 Features of talking with the parents, the child and the adolescent
s0174 **Talking with the parents.** Parents describe signs mixed with their own emotions, so the information should be assessed objectively. For example, parents associate a chronic cough with “frequent colds” and do not think of the possibility of bronchial asthma. The conversation takes place in a friendly atmosphere; helping (open) questions are more effective. It is useful to ask at the end of the conversation, “Is there anything else you would like to tell me about your child?” — sometimes the real reason for the visit comes out at this moment.
s0175 **Talking with the child (5 years and older).** A child often describes symptoms more accurately than the parents. The conversation begins with an interesting, non-frightening topic (games, school, clothes). Then questions the child can understand are asked: “Show me where it hurts. Is the pain strong, or like a needle prick? Does the pain stay in one place or move somewhere else?”
s0176 **Talking with the adolescent.** An adolescent opens up when addressed respectfully, like an adult. The conversation should begin with his interests, friends and school. Confidentiality is guaranteed. Confrontation and long silences make the adolescent close up.
s0177 Conclusion from the history
s0178 After the history has been taken, a general conclusion is drawn: the presumed pathology, its nature (**hereditary, congenital or acquired**), its course (**acute or chronic**) and the **risk factors** that contributed to the development of the disease are indicated.

s0205 1.7. General rules of objective examination
s0206 The following conditions are met for examining a child:
s0207 the room is warm — air temperature **20–22 °C**; if the temperature is below 20 °C, the child is examined partly undressed;
s0208 the lighting is sufficient, preferably **natural daylight**; the child's face is turned toward the light source; in direct light the skin color is seen well, and in side lighting the movements on the body surface (pulsation, peristalsis);
s0209 the child is **fully undressed**: under 3 years — completely; school-age children (especially girls during puberty) are undressed gradually during the examination;
s0210 young children are examined **on a changing table**, older children lying or sitting on a couch or bed; having the parents nearby calms the child;
s0211 the physician stands **on the patient's right side**;
s0212 **a sleeping child is not woken**: during sleep the respiratory rate and pulse are counted and the abdomen is palpated;
s0213 procedures unpleasant for the child (inspection of the throat and ears, examination of a painful area, instrumental investigations) are performed **at the end of the examination**;
s0214 in a preschool child the examination is conducted not according to a strict scheme but according to the child's mood; a restless child is distracted with play.

s0236 The general condition and the criteria for assessing it
s0237 **The general condition** is the physician's **objective conclusion** based on the history and the findings of objective and additional investigations. It determines the scope and order of treatment, the need for additional investigations and where the patient is admitted (intensive care, isolation box, ordinary ward). The condition may change during the day, even within hours.
s0238 Grades of the general condition: **satisfactory, moderately severe, severe** and **extremely severe (terminal)**.

s0299 **Posture and gait.** A healthy child holds the body upright, walks briskly, and moves freely. Stooping, scoliosis, limping, a “waddling” gait (hip dislocation, muscular dystrophy) and an ataxic gait (cerebellar damage) are noted.
s0300 Facial expression
