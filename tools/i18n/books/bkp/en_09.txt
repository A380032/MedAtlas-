s1684 3.7. Body temperature and thermoregulation
s1685 Features of thermoregulation in children
s1686 Thermoregulation is the balance between heat production (**chemical thermoregulation**) and heat loss (**physical thermoregulation**). Its center is **the hypothalamus**.
s1687 The hypothalamic centers are immature — thermoregulation is **imperfect**, especially in newborns and preterm babies; the child **cools down and overheats quickly**.
s1688 The body surface area is large relative to weight (2–3 times that of adults) — heat loss is high.
s1689 Heat is produced mainly by **brown adipose tissue** (non-shivering thermogenesis); the newborn cannot produce heat by shivering.
s1690 The sweat glands work poorly (full function at 5–7 years) — heat loss is difficult in hot weather, and the newborn overheats easily.
s1691 The skin is rich in blood vessels, and the subcutaneous fat is thin (almost absent in preterm babies).
s1692 Newborns cannot produce a typical febrile reaction — in a severe infection (sepsis) the temperature may not rise or may even fall.

s1714 3.8. Fever
s1715 **Fever (febris)** is a rise in body temperature resulting from an **upward shift of the “set point”** of the hypothalamic thermoregulatory center under the action of pyrogens. It is a protective-adaptive reaction of the body: the production of interferon and antibodies increases, and the multiplication of many microbes slows.
s1716 **Hyperthermia (overheating)** differs from fever: the set point does not change, and the temperature rises because of impaired heat loss (a hot room, heavy wrapping, dehydration, heatstroke). Antipyretic drugs are ineffective here — the child must be cooled.

s1776 Antipyretic treatment is not necessary in every fever. s1777 3.9. Clinical part
s1778 Delayed development — the first sign of disease
s1779 Regular assessment of NPD makes it possible to detect many diseases early:
s1780 If a 3-month-old child **does not hold up the head** and **does not smile** — perinatal CNS damage, hypothyroidism, a visual or hearing defect should be considered.
s1781 If **the grasp and Moro reflexes persist** in a 6-month-old child and the muscle tone is increased — risk of cerebral palsy.
s1782 If a 1-year-old child **does not respond to sound**, does not coo or babble — a hearing defect; an audiological examination is needed.
s1783 If a 2-year-old child does not speak, does not make eye contact and does not respond to his name — autism spectrum disorder, a hearing defect or intellectual delay.
s1784 **Loss of acquired skills (regression)** is always a serious sign: a neurodegenerative disease, severe hypotrophy, encephalitis.
s1785 Fever and danger signs
s1786 In a febrile child the following **“red flag”** signs require urgent admission: age under 3 months; lethargy, barely opening the eyes; incessant vomiting; convulsions; a bulging fontanelle or neck stiffness; a hemorrhagic rash that does not fade on pressure (meningococcal infection); “white” fever; respiratory distress; signs of dehydration.
