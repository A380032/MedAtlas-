s2912 A 60-year-old patient has a reduced appetite, aversion to meat, weight loss and a feeling of heaviness in the epigastrium. Gastric juice analysis: total acidity 10 t.u., no free HCl, the Uffelmann reaction positive. Blood hemoglobin is 95 g/l.
s2913 Evaluate the gastric juice analysis. Why has lactic acid appeared? Which disease is suspected?

s2915 A 40-year-old man complains of pain in the upper abdomen on an empty stomach and at night; the pain decreases after food or after taking soda. Gastric juice: total acidity 90 t.u., free HCl 70 t.u. The urea breath test is positive.
s2916 Evaluate the condition. Explain the biochemical basis of treatment.
s2917 A hyperacid state — duodenal ulcer (associated with Helicobacter pylori). With the help of urease H. pylori breaks urea down into NH3 and CO2 (the labeled CO2 is detected in the breath test), surrounds itself with an alkaline “cloud”, damages the mucosa and increases gastrin secretion. Treatment: a proton pump inhibitor (it blocks H+,K+-ATPase and sharply reduces HCl secretion) + antibiotics (eradication); antacids are symptomatic. Soda (NaHCO3) neutralizes HCl and relieves the pain, but it forms CO2 and causes acid rebound — it is not recommended.

s2961 1. The fate of amino acids
s2962 The amino acid pool of the cell is used in the following ways:
s2963 **Protein synthesis** — the main route.
s2964 **Synthesis of nitrogenous compounds**: purine and pyrimidine bases, heme, creatine, carnitine, biogenic amines, hormones (thyroxine, adrenaline), neurotransmitters, glutathione, nicotinamide.
s2965 **Catabolism** — removal of the amino group (transamination, deamination) and oxidation of the carbon skeleton or its conversion into glucose and ketone bodies. The catabolism of amino acids increases especially when the diet is rich in protein, in starvation and in diabetes mellitus. The **ammonia** formed is detoxified (topic 13).
s2966 2. Transamination
s2967 **Transamination** is the transfer of the α-amino group of an amino acid to an α-keto acid; a new amino acid and a new α-keto acid are formed. The reaction is **reversible** and no ammonia is released. It was discovered by A.E. Braunstein and M.G. Kritzman (1937).
s2968 L-amino acid + α-ketoglutarate ⇄ α-keto acid + L-glutamate
s2969 **Aminotransferases (transaminases)** belong to the class of transferases; they are found in almost all tissues, in the cytosol and the mitochondria. Their coenzyme is **pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)**, a derivative of vitamin B6. PLP accepts the amino group from the amino acid and is converted into **pyridoxamine phosphate**, then passes it to the keto acid (a “ping-pong” mechanism).
s2970 Most aminotransferases use **α-ketoglutarate** as the acceptor of the amino group — as a result the amino groups are collected in **glutamate**.

s2994 3. Deamination
s2995 **Deamination** is the removal of the amino group from an amino acid in the form of **ammonia**. Its types: oxidative, reductive, hydrolytic and intramolecular. In humans the main type is **oxidative deamination**.
s2996 Direct oxidative deamination
s2997 Only **glutamate** is actively deaminated — by **glutamate dehydrogenase** (GDH; in the mitochondria, especially active in the liver):
s2998 L-Glutamate + NAD+ (NADP+) + H2O ⇄ α-Ketoglutarate + NH3 + NADH + H+
s2999 The reaction is reversible: in the reverse direction it binds ammonia and synthesizes glutamate. GDH is an **allosteric** enzyme: **ADP and GDP** (low energy) activate it — the catabolism of amino acids increases; **ATP, GTP and NADH** inhibit it.
s3000 The direct deamination of other amino acids is of little importance: **L-amino acid oxidase** (FMN; very low activity), D-amino acid oxidase (FAD; for D-amino acids coming from bacteria), serine and threonine — through dehydratases (PLP), histidine — through histidase.
s3001 Indirect deamination (transdeamination)
s3002 Most amino acids are deaminated in **two steps**:
s3003 **Transamination**: the amino acid passes its amino group to α-ketoglutarate → glutamate;
s3004 **Oxidative deamination of glutamate** (GDH): glutamate → α-ketoglutarate + NH3.

s3008 4. The fate of the carbon skeleton of amino acids
s3009 After the amino group has been removed, the carbon skeletons of the 20 amino acids are converted into 7 common products: pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, acetoacetyl-CoA, α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, fumarate and oxaloacetate. They are oxidized in the Krebs cycle (energy) or:
s3010 **glucogenic amino acids** (those converted into pyruvate or Krebs cycle metabolites) — give glucose through gluconeogenesis: alanine, serine, glycine, cysteine, aspartate, asparagine, glutamate, glutamine, arginine, histidine, proline, methionine, valine, threonine;
s3011 **ketogenic amino acids** (those that give acetyl-CoA or acetoacetyl-CoA) — only **leucine** and **lysine**;
s3012 **mixed** (gluco-ketogenic): isoleucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan.
s3013 5. Decarboxylation of amino acids. The biogenic amines
s3014 **Decarboxylation** is the removal of the carboxyl group from an amino acid as CO2; the product is a **biogenic amine**. The reaction is catalyzed by **decarboxylases** (lyases); their coenzyme is **pyridoxal phosphate** (B6). The reaction is irreversible.
