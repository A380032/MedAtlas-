s0917 1. Energy metabolism. High-energy compounds
s0918 Reactions in the living cell are of two types: **exergonic** (free energy is released, ΔG < 0, proceeds spontaneously) and **endergonic** (energy is consumed, ΔG > 0). Endergonic reactions proceed **coupled** with exergonic reactions; the universal mediator of this coupling is **ATP**.
s0919 **High-energy compounds** are substances that release a large amount of free energy on hydrolysis (ΔG°’ < −30 kJ/mol): ATP and other nucleoside triphosphates (GTP, UTP, CTP), creatine phosphate, phosphoenolpyruvate, 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA.

s0929 The intermediate value of ATP allows it to accept energy from compounds with a higher potential and pass it on to those with a lower potential. In the human body an amount of ATP close to body weight (≈ 40–60 kg) is formed and broken down per day, yet at any moment the body contains only ≈ 50–100 g of ATP — the **ATP–ADP cycle** turns over very rapidly.
s0930 Routes of ATP formation:
s0931 **Oxidative phosphorylation** — in the mitochondria at the expense of the respiratory chain energy (≈ 90%).
s0932 **Substrate-level phosphorylation** — direct transfer of a phosphate group from a high-energy substrate to ADP: in glycolysis (1,3-bisphosphoglycerate → 3-phosphoglycerate, phosphoenolpyruvate → pyruvate) and in the Krebs cycle (succinyl-CoA → succinate, GTP is formed).
s0933 2. Biological oxidation. The respiratory chain
s0934 **Biological oxidation** is the sum of the oxidation-reduction reactions that take place in living cells. Oxidation is the removal of electrons (and often of hydrogen atoms), reduction is their addition. Hydrogen atoms removed from substrates (2H+ + 2e−) pass to **NAD^+^** or **FAD**, are then delivered to oxygen through the **respiratory (electron transport) chain**, and water is formed. This process is called **tissue respiration**.
s0935 The respiratory chain is located in the **inner mitochondrial membrane**. Electrons move along the carriers from the side with a low (negative) **redox potential** to the side with a high (positive) one: NAD+/NADH (−0.32 V) → ... → ½O2/H2O (+0.82 V). The total potential difference is ≈ 1.14 V and the free energy released is ≈ 220 kJ/mol (from NADH).

s0971 3. Oxidative phosphorylation
s0972 **Oxidative phosphorylation** is ATP synthesis coupled to electron transport in the respiratory chain. Its mechanism is explained by **P. Mitchell’s chemiosmotic theory** (1961, Nobel Prize 1978):
s0973 Complexes I, III and IV pump protons from the matrix into the **intermembrane space** at the expense of the energy of electron transport.
s0974 The inner membrane is impermeable to protons — an **electrochemical proton gradient** (ΔμH+) is formed: a chemical part (ΔpH ≈ 0.5–1) and an electrical part (Δψ ≈ 150–180 mV).
s0975 Protons return to the matrix only through the **F~o~ channel of ATP synthase**; this flow drives the rotation of F1 (rotational catalysis, P. Boyer) and the synthesis of ATP from ADP + Pi. The **P/O ratio** is the number of ATP molecules formed per 1 atom of oxygen (2 electrons). According to current data, oxidation of NADH yields **≈ 2.5 ATP** and oxidation of FADH2 (succinate) **≈ 1.5 ATP** (older textbooks give whole numbers — 3 and 2).
s0976 **Respiratory control** is the dependence of the rate of respiration (O2 consumption) on the ADP concentration. When ATP is used and ADP increases, ATP synthase speeds up, the proton gradient decreases and the respiratory chain speeds up. When ADP is low, the gradient is high and the electron flow slows down. Thanks to this, ATP formation is matched to the cell’s needs.
s0977 ATP is exported to the cytosol by the **ADP/ATP translocase** (antiport); phosphate enters by symport with H+.
s0978 Transfer of cytosolic NADH into the mitochondria
s0979 The inner membrane is impermeable to NADH; the hydrogen of NADH formed in the cytosol in glycolysis enters through **shuttle mechanisms**:
s0980 **The malate-aspartate shuttle** (liver, heart, kidney) — hydrogen passes to NAD+ in the mitochondria (≈ 2.5 ATP).
s0981 **The glycerol phosphate shuttle** (skeletal muscle, brain) — hydrogen passes to FAD (≈ 1.5 ATP).

s1005 **Uncouplers** are substances that uncouple oxidation from phosphorylation. In **brown adipose tissue** the protein thermogenin (UCP-1) is a physiological uncoupler: it returns protons to the matrix and the energy is released as heat — **non-shivering thermogenesis** in newborns and hibernating animals.
s1006 **Cyanide poisoning**: cytochrome oxidase is blocked, the tissues cannot use oxygen (venous blood is as red as arterial blood, O2 is not used), anaerobic glycolysis increases and lactic acidosis develops. Treatment: sodium nitrite (converts hemoglobin to methemoglobin, which binds cyanide — cyanmethemoglobin), sodium thiosulfate (converts cyanide into the less toxic thiocyanate with the participation of rhodanese), hydroxocobalamin. **In CO poisoning** hemoglobin is converted to carboxyhemoglobin and cytochrome oxidase is also inhibited; treatment — oxygen (hyperbaric oxygenation).

s1017 6. Mitochondrial diseases
s1018 The mitochondria have their own DNA (mtDNA); it encodes 13 proteins of the respiratory chain and is inherited only **through the mother**. Mutations of mtDNA or of nuclear genes encoding respiratory chain proteins cause **mitochondrial diseases**. They mainly affect tissues with high energy needs — the brain, skeletal and heart muscle, the optic nerve: Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, MELAS syndrome (myopathy, encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, stroke-like episodes), Kearns–Sayre syndrome. Their common biochemical sign is increased blood lactate and pyruvate.
s1019 7. Laboratory work
s1020 Experiment 1: determination of succinate dehydrogenase activity by the Thunberg method
s1021 **Principle of the method:** succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) removes hydrogen from succinate and passes it to an artificial acceptor — methylene blue; methylene blue is reduced and becomes colorless (the leuco form).
s1022 Mince muscle tissue and wash it with water (to remove endogenous substrates).
s1023 Put equal amounts of tissue into two test tubes. Add 2 ml of 2% sodium succinate to tube 1 and 2 ml of water to tube 2 (control).
s1024 Add 2 drops of 0.01% methylene blue to each and cover with a layer of petroleum jelly oil to keep air out.
s1025 Place the tubes in a 37 °C water bath and observe the time of decolorization. **Result and analysis:** in the tube with succinate methylene blue decolorizes faster — SDH oxidizes succinate to fumarate. In the control decolorization is very slow. If **malonate** is added to a 3rd tube together with succinate, decolorization slows down — competitive inhibition.
s1026 Experiment 2: determination of catalase activity
s1027 Pour 1 ml of 3% hydrogen peroxide solution into a test tube.
s1028 Add 1 drop of blood (or liver homogenate).
s1029 Repeat in another tube with boiled blood. **Result and analysis:** when fresh blood is added, vigorous foaming (O2 bubbles) occurs — catalase breaks down H2O2 into water and oxygen. With boiled blood there is no reaction — the enzyme is denatured. Catalase activity is determined quantitatively by the Bach–Zubkova method: the undecomposed H2O2 is titrated with potassium permanganate.
s1030 Experiment 3: determination of peroxidase activity (benzidine or guaiac test)
s1031 Pour 1 ml of diluted blood (or potato juice) and 5 drops of benzidine (or guaiac resin) solution into a test tube.
s1032 Add 3–5 drops of 3% H2O2.
s1033 **Result and analysis:** a blue (or blue-green) color appears — peroxidase (in blood, the pseudoperoxidase activity of hemoglobin) oxidizes the substrate at the expense of the oxygen of H2O2. This reaction underlies the detection of occult blood in stool and urine.
