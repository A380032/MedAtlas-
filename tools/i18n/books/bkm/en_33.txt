s6387 A 45-year-old man presented after eating a large fatty meal and drinking alcohol with severe girdle-like pain in the upper abdomen and repeated vomiting. Serum lipase 1250 U/l (normal < 60), amylase 820 U/l, glucose 9.8 mmol/l, calcium 1.9 mmol/l.
s6388 What is the diagnosis? Explain the biochemical mechanism of the disease. Why is calcium decreased and glucose increased?
s6389 Acute pancreatitis. Alcohol and fatty food enhance secretion, raise the pressure in the ducts and damage the acinar cells; trypsinogen is converted to trypsin prematurely inside the gland and activates the other proenzymes (chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, prophospholipase A2) — the gland digests itself (autolysis): elastase destroys the vessels (hemorrhage), phospholipase A2 destroys membranes, and lipase breaks down adipose tissue. The enzymes are released into the blood (increased lipase and amylase). In foci of fat necrosis, free fatty acids form soaps with calcium, leading to hypocalcemia (a sign of a severe course). Damage to the islets of Langerhans and stress hormones cause hyperglycemia.
s6390 A 25-year-old woman complains of bloating, rumbling and loose stools 1–2 hours after drinking milk or eating dairy products. After 50 g of lactose, blood glucose did not rise (+ 0.6 mmol/l), and breath hydrogen rose sharply.
s6391 What is the diagnosis? Explain the biochemical mechanism of the symptoms. What recommendations would you give?
s6392 Lactase deficiency (lactose intolerance). Without lactase, lactose is not broken down into glucose and galactose in the small intestine — blood glucose does not rise. Lactose passes into the colon, raises the osmotic pressure and retains water (osmotic diarrhea); bacteria ferment it to H2, CO2, methane and short-chain acids (bloating, rumbling; H2 is absorbed into the blood and exhaled). Recommendations: limit fresh milk; fermented dairy products (yogurt, cheese — little lactose, contain bacterial β-galactosidase), lactose-free milk or lactase preparations; prevention of calcium deficiency.
s6393 A 7-year-old child is growth-retarded and thin, with a large abdomen and frequent, large, fatty, foul-smelling stools. Blood Hb 88 g/l, ferritin 5 µg/l, albumin 30 g/l. IgA antibodies against tissue transglutaminase are sharply increased. Intestinal biopsy shows villous atrophy.
s6394 What is the diagnosis? Explain the mechanism of the disease. Why did iron deficiency and hypoalbuminemia develop? What is the treatment?
s6395 Celiac disease (gluten enteropathy). The gliadin peptides of wheat, barley and rye are resistant to proteases; tissue transglutaminase deamidates them, and the deamidated peptides are presented to T lymphocytes with HLA-DQ2/DQ8 — inflammation; cytotoxic lymphocytes destroy the enterocytes, and the villi become atrophic. The absorptive surface and membrane enzymes decrease: the absorption of fats (steatorrhea), iron (in the duodenum — iron deficiency anemia), amino acids (hypoalbuminemia), calcium and vitamin D is impaired — growth retardation. Treatment — a lifelong strict gluten-free diet (rice, corn and buckwheat are allowed); iron and vitamin supplementation; a decrease in antibodies shows that the diet is being followed.

s6445 2. Heme catabolism and bilirubin metabolism
s6446 ≈ 250–300 mg of bilirubin is formed per day: ≈ 80% from the hemoglobin of senescent erythrocytes, the rest from myoglobin, cytochromes and ineffective erythropoiesis.
s6447 Figure 25.1
s6448 Bilirubin metabolism (drawn after Severin E.S.)
s6449 **In macrophages** (spleen, Kupffer cells of the liver, bone marrow): **heme oxygenase** (microsomal, requires O2 and NADPH) cleaves the α-methene bridge of heme to form **biliverdin**, **CO** and Fe2+; **biliverdin reductase** (NADPH) reduces biliverdin to **bilirubin** (from green to yellow — the color change of a bruise).
s6450 **Transport in the blood:** bilirubin is insoluble in water (lipophilic) and is carried to the liver bound to **albumin** — **unconjugated (indirect, free)** bilirubin. It does not pass the renal filter — it is not found in urine. Its free (non-albumin-bound) fraction crosses the blood–brain barrier and is **neurotoxic**. Sulfonamides, salicylates and free fatty acids displace it from albumin.
s6451 **In the liver:** hepatocytes take up bilirubin with membrane transporters (OATP) and bind it inside the cell to ligandin (glutathione S-transferase); in the endoplasmic reticulum, **UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1)** attaches two glucuronic acid residues from UDP-glucuronic acid — **bilirubin diglucuronide** (**conjugated, direct** bilirubin) — which is water-soluble.
s6452 **Secretion into bile** — active transport (ATP) through the **MRP2** transporter; this is the **slowest (rate-limiting) step** of the process.
s6453 **In the intestine:** bacterial β-glucuronidase removes the glucuronic acid; bacteria reduce bilirubin to **urobilinogens** (mesobilinogen, stercobilinogen). Most of it is oxidized to **stercobilin**, which colors the feces (200–300 mg per day). Part of the urobilinogen is reabsorbed and returns to the liver (enterohepatic circulation), and a small part is excreted in the urine and oxidized to **urobilin** (the color of urine).

s6503 4. Neonatal jaundice. Hereditary hyperbilirubinemias
s6504 **Physiological jaundice** is observed in ≈ 60% of full-term and ≈ 80% of preterm newborns. Causes: the large number and short lifespan (≈ 80 days) of fetal erythrocytes (HbF) — bilirubin production is 2–3 times higher than in adults; the low activity of **UDP-glucuronosyltransferase** (≈ 1% of the adult level, matures by 1–3 months); little ligandin; the intestine is sterile — urobilinogen is not formed, and bilirubin is reabsorbed under the action of β-glucuronidase. Features: it begins on **days 2–3**, peaks on days 3–5 (< 205–220 µmol/l) and resolves by days 10–14; only unconjugated bilirubin increases; the general condition is good.

s6506 Dynamics of bilirubin in newborns (drawn after Harper’s Illustrated Biochemistry)
s6507 **Signs of pathological jaundice:** appearance on the first day, a rapid rise in bilirubin (> 85 µmol/l/day), duration of more than 2 weeks in full-term and more than 3 weeks in preterm infants, an increase in conjugated bilirubin (> 25–34 µmol/l), an undulating course. Causes: **hemolytic disease** (Rh or AB0 incompatibility), G6PD deficiency, spherocytosis, infections, hypothyroidism, biliary atresia, hereditary hyperbilirubinemias.
s6508 **Bilirubin encephalopathy (kernicterus)** — unconjugated bilirubin (> 340 µmol/l in full-term infants, lower in preterm infants) crosses the blood–brain barrier and accumulates in the basal ganglia: it uncouples oxidative phosphorylation in the mitochondria — neurons die (deafness, cerebral palsy, intellectual disability). **Phototherapy** (blue light, 460–490 nm) converts bilirubin into water-soluble photoisomers (lumirubin), which are excreted in bile and urine without conjugation. In severe cases — exchange transfusion. Phenobarbital induces UGT1A1.

s6550 6. Biochemical mechanisms of hepatic coma
s6551 **Hepatic encephalopathy and coma** is a disturbance of central nervous system function in acute or chronic liver failure.
s6552 **The ammonia hypothesis (the main one):** urea synthesis in the liver decreases, and ammonia from the intestine reaches the brain directly through portocaval shunts. In the brain, astrocytes convert NH3 into **glutamine** (glutamine synthetase) — glutamine accumulates and the astrocytes swell (osmotically), brain edema; **α-ketoglutarate** is consumed for glutamate — the Krebs cycle and ATP formation decrease; inhibitory and excitatory neurotransmission are disturbed (topic 13).
