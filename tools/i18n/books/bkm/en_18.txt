s3421 1. Functions of nucleotides. Digestion of nucleoproteins
s3422 **Nucleotides** are compounds consisting of a nitrogenous base (purine: adenine, guanine; pyrimidine: cytosine, uracil, thymine), a pentose (ribose or deoxyribose) and 1–3 phosphate residues.
s3423 monomers of the nucleic acids (DNA, RNA);
s3424 **energy** — ATP is the universal energy carrier; GTP (protein synthesis, gluconeogenesis), UTP (glycogen synthesis), CTP (phospholipid synthesis);
s3425 components of **coenzymes** — NAD+, NADP+, FAD, CoA;
s3426 **second messengers** — cAMP, cGMP;
s3427 **activated intermediates** — UDP-glucose, CDP-choline, S-adenosylmethionine, PAPS;
s3428 **allosteric effectors** — they regulate the activity of many enzymes. **Digestion of nucleoproteins:** in the stomach nucleoproteins are split into the protein part (histones) and the nucleic acids; in the intestine the pancreatic **nucleases** (DNase, RNase) break the nucleic acids down into oligonucleotides, **phosphodiesterases** into mononucleotides, **nucleotidases** into nucleosides, and **nucleosidases (phosphorylases)** into the nitrogenous base and the pentose. The purine and pyrimidine bases that come with food are almost not used for the synthesis of nucleic acids in the body — they are broken down. Therefore nucleotides are **not an essential factor**: they are synthesized from amino acids and other simple substances.
s3429 2. Synthesis of purine nucleotides
s3430 De novo synthesis
s3431 Purine nucleotides are synthesized mainly in the **liver**, in the cytosol, on the basis of **phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP)** — the purine ring is “assembled” step by step on ribose-5-phosphate.
s3432 **Sources of the atoms of the purine ring:** glycine (C4, C5, N7), the amide nitrogen of glutamine (N3, N9), aspartate (N1), CO2 (C6), N10-formyl-THF (C2, C8).
s3433 Figure 14.1
s3434 De novo synthesis of purine nucleotides and the salvage pathway (drawn after Severin E.S.)
s3435 Ribose-5-phosphate + ATP → **PRPP** (PRPP synthetase).
s3436 PRPP + glutamine → 5-phosphoribosylamine — **amidophosphoribosyltransferase** (the **key enzyme**).
s3437 In 9 reactions **inosine monophosphate (IMP)** is formed (6 ATP are used).
s3438 From IMP: **AMP** (the amino group of aspartate, the energy of GTP) and **GMP** (the amide group of glutamine, the energy of ATP). **Regulation:** PRPP synthetase and amidophosphoribosyltransferase are inhibited by the end products — **AMP, GMP and IMP** (negative feedback), while PRPP activates them. In addition there is **reciprocal regulation**: GTP is needed for the synthesis of AMP and ATP for the synthesis of GMP — this keeps the balance of adenine and guanine nucleotides.
s3439 The salvage (reutilization) pathway
s3440 The free purine bases formed by the breakdown of nucleotides combine with PRPP and are converted back into nucleotides — energy is saved:
s3441 **hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT)**: hypoxanthine + PRPP → IMP; guanine + PRPP → GMP;
s3442 **adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (APRT)**: adenine + PRPP → AMP. The salvage pathway is especially important in the **brain** and erythrocytes (where de novo synthesis is low).
s3443 **Lesch–Nyhan syndrome** — complete congenital deficiency of **HGPRT** (X-linked recessive, in boys). The salvage pathway does not work: PRPP accumulates and sharply increases de novo synthesis, so purines
s3444 are formed in excess and broken down — **severe hyperuricemia**, uric acid stones, gouty arthritis; neurological disorders (choreoathetosis, mental retardation) and **self-injury** (biting the lips and fingers). Allopurinol reduces the hyperuricemia but does not correct the neurological signs.
s3445 3. Breakdown of purine nucleotides. Uric acid
s3446 Purine nucleotides are converted by nucleotidases, adenosine deaminase, purine nucleoside phosphorylase and guanase into **hypoxanthine** and **xanthine**, and then by **xanthine oxidase** (which contains molybdenum, FAD and Fe; active in the liver and intestine) into **uric acid**.
s3447 Figure 14.2
s3448 Breakdown of the purine bases to uric acid and the xanthine oxidase inhibitors (drawn after Severin E.S.)
s3449 In humans and other primates uric acid is the **end product** of purine metabolism (in most mammals it is converted by **uricase** into the readily soluble allantoin; humans do not have this enzyme). Uric acid is a weak acid and at physiological pH is in the form of **urate** (sodium urate); it is poorly soluble in water. About 2/3 of it is excreted by the kidneys and 1/3 through the intestine. Uric acid is also a strong **antioxidant**.

s3459 4. Hyperuricemia and gout
s3460 **Hyperuricemia** is a rise of uric acid in the blood (> 420 µmol/l in men, > 360 in women).
s3461 **Primary hyperuricemia** (in ≈ 90% of cases — a hereditary reduction of urate excretion by the kidney; less often — increased synthesis: raised PRPP synthetase activity, partial HGPRT deficiency).
s3462 Secondary hyperuricemia:
s3463 increased formation — extensive breakdown of cells: leukemias, lymphomas, chemotherapy of tumors (**tumor lysis syndrome**), hemolytic anemia, psoriasis; food rich in purines (meat, liver, kidney, fish, beer), excess fructose, type I glycogenosis;
s3464 reduced excretion — renal failure, thiazide diuretics, low-dose aspirin, lactic acidosis and ketoacidosis (lactate and ketone bodies compete with urate for secretion), alcohol, lead poisoning. **Gout** is a disease in which urate crystals (monosodium urate) are deposited in joints and tissues. **Acute gouty arthritis** — most often the 1st metatarsophalangeal joint of the big toe; the crystals are taken up by neutrophils and inflammatory mediators are released (severe pain, swelling, redness). In chronic gout **tophi** (deposits of urate under the skin), **urate stones** in the kidney and urate nephropathy develop.

s3480 5. Synthesis and breakdown of pyrimidine nucleotides
s3481 The pyrimidine ring is first assembled free and then attached to PRPP:
s3482 Glutamine + CO2 + 2ATP → carbamoyl phosphate — **carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II** (in the cytosol; the **key enzyme**; inhibited by UTP, activated by PRPP and ATP). (Unlike carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I of the ornithine cycle: that one is in the mitochondria and uses NH3.)
s3483 Carbamoyl phosphate + aspartate → carbamoyl aspartate (aspartate carbamoyltransferase) → dihydroorotate → **orotic acid** (dihydroorotate dehydrogenase — in the mitochondria).
s3484 Orotate + PRPP → orotidine monophosphate → **UMP** (a bifunctional enzyme — **UMP synthase**: orotate phosphoribosyltransferase and OMP decarboxylase).
s3485 UMP → UDP → **UTP** → **CTP** (CTP synthetase, the amide group of glutamine). In humans the first three enzymes are combined in one multifunctional protein (CAD).
s3486 **Breakdown:** cytosine and uracil → β-alanine, NH3, CO2; thymine → β-aminoisobutyric acid. The products are readily soluble in water and do not cause diseases like hyperuricemia.
s3487 **Hereditary orotic aciduria** — UMP synthase deficiency: a lot of **orotic acid** in the urine (crystals), megaloblastic anemia (not enough pyrimidine nucleotides and DNA synthesis; it is not cured by folate or B12),
s3488 growth retardation. Treatment — **uridine**: it is converted into UMP and makes up the shortage of nucleotides, and through UTP it inhibits carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II and reduces the formation of orotic acid. Orotic aciduria is also seen in ornithine carbamoyltransferase deficiency (topic 13), but there is hyperammonemia there and no anemia.
s3489 6. Synthesis of deoxyribonucleotides and thymidylate nucleotides
s3490 **Ribonucleotide reductase** reduces ribonucleoside **diphosphates** (ADP, GDP, CDP, UDP) to deoxyribonucleoside diphosphates. The reductant is **thioredoxin** (which is regenerated at the expense of thioredoxin reductase and NADPH). The enzyme is regulated allosterically in a complex way: **dATP** is an inhibitor for all substrates and ATP an activator; the various dNTPs change the substrate specificity and keep the balance of deoxyribonucleotides. The activity of the enzyme is highest in the S phase of the cell cycle.
s3491 **Thymidylate nucleotides:** dUMP + N5,N10-methylene-THF → **dTMP** + dihydrofolate — **thymidylate synthase**. Dihydrofolate is converted back into THF by **dihydrofolate reductase** (NADPH). These reactions are vital for rapidly dividing cells (tumors, bone marrow, intestinal epithelium).

s3517 7. Age-related features of nucleotide metabolism
s3518 In the first days of life the blood uric acid of newborns is high (a lot of cells, especially erythrocytes, are broken down) and urate crystals may be deposited in the renal tubules — a **uric acid infarct** (a physiological state; the urine is brick-red and it passes within 1–2 weeks). During growth the synthesis of nucleotides is intense. Hyperuricemia is more common in old age and, in women, after the menopause (estrogens increase the excretion of urate).
s3519 Experiment 1: determination of uric acid in serum by the uricase method
s3520 **Principle of the method: uricase** oxidizes uric acid to allantoin, CO2 and **H~2~O~2~**; peroxidase, at the expense of H2O2, oxidizes a chromogen and forms a colored compound (the Trinder reaction, 520 nm). The intensity of the color is proportional to the amount of uric acid.
s3521 Pour 1 ml of working reagent (uricase, peroxidase, chromogen) into each of three test tubes.
s3522 Add 25 µl of serum to tube 1, 25 µl of standard (for example, 357 µmol/l) to tube 2 and 25 µl of water to tube 3.
s3523 Keep at 37 °C for 10 minutes and measure against the control at 520 nm. **Calculation:** C (µmol/l) = (Esample / Estandard) × Cstandard
s3524 **Evaluation:** 200–420 µmol/l in men, 140–360 in women. Ascorbic acid (a strong reductant) can use up the H2O2 and falsely lower the result.
s3525 Experiment 2: the murexide test for uric acid
s3526 Put a small amount of uric acid (or scrapings from a urate stone) into a porcelain dish and add 2–3 drops of concentrated nitric acid.
s3527 Evaporate carefully on a water bath (a red-yellow residue).
s3528 After cooling, add 1 drop of 10% ammonia solution. **Result and analysis: violet-red** color appears (murexide — the ammonium salt of purpuric acid); with NaOH it is blue-violet. This reaction is characteristic of purines and uric acid; it is used to determine the chemical composition of kidney stones.
s3529 Experiment 3: microscopic examination of uric acid crystals in urine (demonstration)
s3530 Place a drop of the urine sediment (after centrifugation) on a slide.
s3531 Examine under the microscope. **Result and analysis:** in acidic urine, uric acid crystals are yellow-brown and rhomboid, barrel-shaped or in rosettes; amorphous urates form a brick-red sediment. Large amounts indicate a urate diathesis or a tendency to urate stones.
