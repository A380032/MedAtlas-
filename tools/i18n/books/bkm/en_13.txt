s2486 3. Transport and removal of cholesterol
s2487 Cholesterol is transported in the blood in lipoproteins (topic 8); ≈ 2/3 of total cholesterol is in the form of **cholesterol esters**.
s2488 **Transport to the tissues.** The VLDL synthesized in the liver are converted in the blood by LPL into IDL and then into **LDL** — ≈ 70% of blood cholesterol is in LDL. LDL enter cells by endocytosis through **LDL receptors** (which recognize apo B-100 and apo E) (M. Brown and J. Goldstein, Nobel Prize 1985).

s2490 Entry of cholesterol into the cell through the LDL receptor and regulation of the intracellular cholesterol level (drawn after Severin E.S.)
s2491 When free cholesterol increases inside the cell: the synthesis of HMG-CoA reductase and of LDL receptors falls, and **ACAT** (acyl-CoA:cholesterol acyltransferase) is activated, so the excess cholesterol is stored as esters.
s2492 **Reverse transport. HDL** (apo A-I) are formed in the liver and intestine as “flat discs”; they take free cholesterol from peripheral cells through the ABCA1 transporter. **Lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT)** (activated by apo A-I) transfers a fatty acid from phosphatidylcholine to cholesterol and forms cholesterol esters; these move into the core of the HDL and the particle becomes spherical. HDL deliver cholesterol to the **liver** (SR-B1 receptors) or pass the cholesterol esters to other lipoproteins. For this reason HDL are **antiatherogenic** lipoproteins.
s2493 **Removal.** Cholesterol is not broken down to CO2 and H2O in the body. It:
s2494 is converted in the liver into **bile acids** (≈ 0.5 g per day; the key enzyme is 7α-hydroxylase) and leaves with the bile;
s2495 passes unchanged with the bile into the intestine; part of it is reabsorbed, and the rest is converted by bacteria into **coprostanol** and excreted with the feces;
s2496 a small amount leaves with sebum, and the metabolites of steroid hormones with the urine.
s2497 4. Hypercholesterolemia and atherosclerosis
s2498 **Atherosclerosis** is a chronic disease in which lipids (mainly cholesterol and its esters) accumulate in the intima of large and medium arteries and fibrous plaques form. Its complications — ischemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, stroke — are among the main causes of death in the world.
s2499 The biochemical stages of atherogenesis:
s2500 Damage to the endothelium (hypercholesterolemia, arterial hypertension, smoking, hyperglycemia, homocysteine).
s2501 LDL enter the intima and are **oxidized** or glycated there (modified LDL).
s2502 Monocytes move into the intima and become macrophages; they take up modified LDL without limit through **scavenger receptors** (these receptors are not regulated by cholesterol) — **foam cells** are formed.
s2503 Under the action of inflammatory cytokines smooth muscle cells proliferate and collagen accumulates — a plaque with a fibrous cap forms; rupture of the plaque causes thrombosis. **Risk factors:** raised LDL cholesterol, low HDL cholesterol, high lipoprotein(a), arterial hypertension, smoking, diabetes mellitus, obesity, physical inactivity, heredity, age and male sex.
s2504 **The atherogenic index (A.N. Klimov):** AI = (total CH − HDL-CH) / HDL-CH; normal < 3.0; > 4 — high risk.
s2505 **Familial hypercholesterolemia** (type IIa hyperlipoproteinemia) — a mutation of the LDL receptor gene (autosomal dominant). In heterozygotes (≈ 1:250) total cholesterol is 8–12 mmol/l and ischemic heart disease appears at 30–50 years; in homozygotes (≈ 1:1 000 000) cholesterol is 15–25 mmol/l, with tendon xanthomas and myocardial infarction in childhood. LDL do not enter the cells, and the cell, sensing cholesterol as “low”, does not reduce its synthesis. Treatment: statins, ezetimibe (reduces absorption in the intestine), PCSK9 inhibitors and, in severe cases, LDL apheresis.

s2534 5. Gallstone disease
s2535 In bile, cholesterol is kept dissolved by forming micelles with bile acids and phosphatidylcholine. When their ratio is disturbed (too much cholesterol or too few bile acids and phospholipids), the bile becomes **supersaturated with cholesterol (“lithogenic”)**; cholesterol crystals precipitate in the gallbladder and **cholesterol stones** form (≈ 80% of all stones). Risk factors: female sex, pregnancy, estrogens, obesity, rapid weight loss, a high-calorie diet, reduced motility of the gallbladder, diseases of the ileum (bile acids are not reabsorbed).
s2536 **The biochemical basis of treatment: ursodeoxycholic** (and chenodeoxycholic) acid preparations — they increase the bile acid pool, improve the solubility of cholesterol in the bile and reduce cholesterol synthesis in the liver (they dissolve small cholesterol stones); surgery (cholecystectomy). **Pigment stones** (calcium bilirubinate) form in hemolytic anemias and infection of the bile ducts.
s2537 6. Regulation and disorders of fat metabolism
s2538 **In the fed state** (insulin ↑): the synthesis of fatty acids and TAG from glucose increases (in the liver) and VLDL are formed; in adipose tissue LPL is activated and fat is stored; lipolysis is inhibited.
s2539 **In starvation** (glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol ↑): lipolysis in adipose tissue increases, fatty acids are oxidized in muscle and liver, and ketone bodies are formed in the liver.
s2540 **Obesity** is an increase of adipose tissue resulting from a prolonged excess of energy intake over expenditure (body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m^2^). Adipose tissue is an endocrine organ: it produces **leptin** (a satiety signal acting on the hypothalamus), adiponectin and cytokines. In obesity, leptin resistance, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia and arterial hypertension develop — the **metabolic syndrome** (topic 15). **Obesity in children** is usually related to overeating and physical inactivity; rarely it is caused by a defect of the leptin gene or of its receptor.
s2541 **Sphingolipidoses** are congenital deficiencies of the enzymes that break down sphingolipids in the lysosomes; the lipids accumulate mainly in nervous tissue, the liver and the spleen.

s2564 Experiment 1: determination of total cholesterol in serum by the enzymatic method (on a biochemical analyzer)
s2565 Principle of the method (CHOD-PAP):
s2566 **Cholesterol esterase**: cholesterol esters + H2O → cholesterol + fatty acids;
s2567 **Cholesterol oxidase**: cholesterol + O2 → cholest-4-en-3-one + H2O2;
s2568 **Peroxidase**: 2H2O2 + 4-aminoantipyrine + phenol → pink quinoneimine + 4H2O. The intensity of the color is proportional to the amount of total cholesterol (500 nm).
s2569 Pour 1 ml of working reagent into each of three test tubes.
s2570 Add 10 µl of serum to tube 1, 10 µl of standard (5.17 mmol/l) to tube 2 and 10 µl of water to tube 3.
s2571 Mix and keep at 37 °C for 10 minutes.
s2572 Measure the optical density against the control at 500 nm (automatically on the analyzer). **Calculation:** C (mmol/l) = (Esample / Estandard) × 5.17
s2573 **Evaluation:** < 5.2 mmol/l — desirable; 5.2–6.2 — borderline high; ≥ 6.2 — high. The sample is taken after 12–14 hours of fasting.
s2574 Experiment 2: qualitative reaction for cholesterol (Liebermann–Burchard reaction)
s2575 Pour 1 ml of a solution of cholesterol in chloroform into a dry test tube.
s2576 Add 10 drops of acetic anhydride and 1–2 drops of concentrated sulfuric acid (take care!).
s2577 Observe the change of color. **Result and analysis:** first a pink, then a blue and finally a **green** color appears — cholesterol is dehydrated by sulfuric acid and forms colored compounds. This reaction used to be the basis of the quantitative determination of cholesterol (the Ilk method).

s2662 A 12-year-old child was found to have hard nodules over the Achilles tendons (tendon xanthomas) and yellow patches on the eyelids (xanthelasmas). Total cholesterol is 16 mmol/l and triglycerides are normal. His father died of myocardial infarction at 38, and his mother’s cholesterol is 9 mmol/l.
s2663 What is the diagnosis? Explain its biochemical mechanism. Why may the effect of statins be limited?
s2664 Homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (type IIa) — a mutation in both alleles of the LDL receptor gene (both parents are heterozygotes). LDL do not enter cells and accumulate in the blood; the cells sense a “shortage” of cholesterol and do not reduce HMG-CoA reductase and synthesis. The excess LDL is taken up by macrophages through scavenger receptors — xanthomas and early atherosclerosis. Statins act mainly by increasing the number of LDL receptors; if the receptors do not work at all, the effect is small — LDL apheresis, PCSK9 inhibitors and other methods are needed.
s2665 A 45-year-old obese woman (BMI 34 kg/m^2^) complains of attacks of severe pain under the right costal margin after fatty food. Ultrasound revealed small radiolucent stones in the gallbladder.
s2666 What is the likely chemical composition of the stones? Explain the mechanism of their formation.
s2667 Cholesterol stones (radiolucent). Obesity, female sex and estrogens increase cholesterol synthesis in the liver and its secretion into the bile, while the synthesis of bile acids falls relatively; the bile becomes supersaturated with cholesterol (lithogenic). Reduced motility of the gallbladder helps crystals to form. Small cholesterol stones can be dissolved with ursodeoxycholic acid; with frequent attacks — cholecystectomy.
s2668 A 55-year-old man smokes and his blood pressure is 160/95 mm Hg. Tests: total cholesterol 6.8 mmol/l, HDL-CH 0.9 mmol/l, triglycerides 2.2 mmol/l.
s2669 Calculate the atherogenic index and LDL-CH. Assess the risk.
s2670 AI = (6.8 − 0.9) / 0.9 ≈ 6.6 (normal < 3.0). Friedewald formula: LDL-CH = 6.8 − 0.9 − 2.2 / 2.2 = 6.8 − 0.9 − 1.0 = 4.9 mmol/l (desirable < 3.0). HDL is low, LDL and TG are high — atherogenic dyslipidemia. Together with smoking and arterial hypertension the risk of cardiovascular disease is very high. Recommendations: stopping smoking, diet, physical activity, statins, control of blood pressure.
