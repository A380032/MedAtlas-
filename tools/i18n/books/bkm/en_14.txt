s2706 1. The significance of proteins. Nitrogen balance
s2707 Proteins carry out many functions in the body: **plastic** (the structure of cells and tissues), **catalytic** (enzymes), **transport** (hemoglobin, albumin, transferrin), **protective** (immunoglobulins, clotting factors), **regulatory** (peptide hormones), **contractile** (actin, myosin), **energy** (1 g → 4 kcal; in starvation). Proteins are the source of **nitrogen** and of the **essential amino acids** for the body.
s2708 **Nitrogen balance** is the difference between the nitrogen taken in with food and the nitrogen excreted from the body (mainly as urea). 1 g of nitrogen corresponds to ≈ 6.25 g of protein.

s2720 **The daily requirement for protein** in adults is 0.8–1.0 g/kg (≈ 70–100 g), at least half of it animal protein; in children 1.5–4 g/kg (most in newborns), and more in pregnant and breastfeeding women, in athletes and during recovery. The **protein minimum** (the smallest amount needed to maintain nitrogen equilibrium) is ≈ 30–50 g.
s2721 **The biological value of proteins** depends on their content of essential amino acids and on their digestibility: egg and milk proteins are high; plant proteins are lower (the limiting amino acids: wheat — lysine, legumes — methionine, maize — tryptophan and lysine). Eating different plant proteins together (for example, grain + legumes) increases the biological value.
s2722 **Protein-energy malnutrition** is one of the main causes of child mortality in developing countries. **Kwashiorkor** — energy in the diet is relatively adequate but protein is sharply reduced: edema because of hypoalbuminemia, fatty degeneration of the liver, changes of the skin and hair, reduced immunity. **Marasmus** — both protein and energy are lacking: severe wasting, loss of muscle and adipose tissue, no edema.
s2723 **The dynamic state of body proteins.** The body’s proteins are constantly broken down and resynthesized: ≈ 300–400 g of protein is renewed per day (more than the amount that comes with food) — ≈ 75% of the amino acids released by breakdown are reused. The half-life of proteins differs: pancreatic enzymes and insulin — minutes to hours; liver enzymes — hours to days; hemoglobin — 120 days (the life of the erythrocyte); collagen — months to years. Intracellular proteins are broken down in the **lysosomes** (cathepsins) and in the **proteasomes** (the ubiquitin-dependent pathway).
s2724 **The species and tissue specificity of proteins**: the protein composition of every species and tissue is genetically determined; therefore, if foreign proteins enter the body undigested (for example, parenterally), they cause an immune response. Dietary proteins are broken down to amino acids and lose their “species specificity”.
s2725 2. Digestion of proteins in the stomach
s2726 Proteins are not digested in the oral cavity. Gastric juice (1.5–2.5 l per day) contains **hydrochloric acid**, **pepsinogens**, mucus (mucin) and Castle’s intrinsic factor.
s2727 **The formation of HCl** — in the **parietal cells** of the fundic glands: by carbonic anhydrase CO~2~ + H~2~O → H~2~CO~3~ → H^+^ + HCO~3~
s2728 −; H^+^ is pumped into the gastric lumen in exchange for K^+^ by **H^+^,K^+^-ATPase** (the “proton pump”), and Cl^-^ leaves through channels; HCO~3~
s2729 − passes into the blood in exchange for Cl^-^ (the “alkaline tide” after a meal).
s2730 **The functions of HCl:** it denatures and swells proteins (opening the peptide bonds to enzymes); converts pepsinogen into pepsin and creates the optimal pH for pepsin (1.5–2); has a bactericidal action; regulates gastric emptying and the release of secretin in the duodenum; improves the absorption of iron (Fe^3+^
s2731 → Fe^2+^).
s2732 **Pepsin** is synthesized in the chief cells as inactive **pepsinogen**; under the action of HCl and by **autocatalysis** a fragment of 42 amino acid residues is removed from the N-terminus and active pepsin is formed. Pepsin is an **endopeptidase**: it cleaves the peptide bonds formed by aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan) and leucine; proteins are broken down into large polypeptides. **Gastricsin** (pH 3–3.5) is important in the newborn’s stomach. Newborns also have **chymosin (rennin)**, the enzyme that curdles milk casein.
s2733 **Regulation of gastric juice secretion:** nervous (the vagus — acetylcholine), humoral (**gastrin** — released by the G cells of the antrum under the action of protein products and distension of the stomach; **histamine** — through H~2~ receptors), and inhibitors — somatostatin, secretin, a low pH.

s2740 **Pathological gastric juice: a hyperacid state** (gastritis, peptic ulcer, Zollinger–Ellison syndrome — gastrinoma); **hyposecretion and achylia** (atrophic gastritis, gastric cancer, pernicious anemia — no HCl and no intrinsic factor). s2765 4. Absorption and distribution of amino acids
s2766 Free amino acids and di- and tripeptides are absorbed in the small intestine:
s2767 free L-amino acids — by **symport with Na^+^** (secondary active transport) — with separate carriers for neutral, basic and acidic amino acids and imino acids;
s2768 di- and tripeptides — by **symport with H^+^** (PepT1), and they are broken down inside the enterocyte;
s2769 **the γ-glutamyl cycle (Meister cycle)** — entry of amino acids into the cell with the participation of glutathione (renal tubules, intestine, brain). The absorbed amino acids reach the liver through the **portal vein**; the liver retains part of them (synthesis of plasma proteins, gluconeogenesis, urea) and passes the rest into the general circulation. **The amino acid pool** is the free amino acids in the blood and cells (≈ 35 g). Its sources: dietary proteins, breakdown of body proteins, synthesis of nonessential amino acids. Its uses: protein synthesis, synthesis of nitrogenous compounds (heme, creatine, nucleotides, biogenic amines), production of energy and glucose.
s2770 **Distribution among tissues**: in starvation muscle proteins are a source of amino acids (they are released mainly as alanine and glutamine); the liver is the center of amino acid metabolism; the kidney uses glutamine and excretes ammonium ions.
s2771 **Congenital absorption disorders: Hartnup disease** (a defect of the carrier of neutral amino acids, especially tryptophan — pellagra-like signs), **cystinuria** (a defect of the carrier of cystine, ornithine, lysine and arginine — cystine stones in the kidney).
s2772 5. Features of protein digestion in children
s2773 The acidity of gastric juice is low (pH 3–5) and pepsin activity is low; **gastricsin** and **chymosin** play an important role.
s2774 In the first months of life the permeability of the intestinal mucosa is high — some proteins (**IgA** and IgG of the colostrum) are absorbed undigested by **pinocytosis** (passive immunity).
s2775 For this reason foreign dietary proteins (cow’s milk proteins, egg, wheat gluten) may also be absorbed partly undigested and cause **food allergy**. This is why complementary food should be introduced at the right time and gradually.
s2776 **Celiac disease** — an immune reaction to the gluten (gliadin) of wheat, barley and rye: atrophy of the villi of the small intestine, malabsorption; treatment — a gluten-free diet.

s2797 Experiment 1: determination of the acidity of gastric juice by titration (Töpfer method)
s2798 **Principle of the method:** the acids of gastric juice are titrated with 0.1 N NaOH in the presence of two indicators. **Dimethylaminoazobenzene** (which changes from red to yellow at pH 2.9–4.0) shows free HCl; **phenolphthalein** (pink at pH 8.2–10) shows the total acidity.
s2799 Measure 5 ml of filtered gastric juice into a flask and add 1–2 drops of dimethylaminoazobenzene and 1–2 drops of phenolphthalein (a red color means free HCl is present).
s2800 Titrate from a burette with 0.1 N NaOH until the red color turns **orange** — mark 1 (free HCl).
s2801 Continue the titration until a **yellow** color appears — mark 2.
s2802 Titrate until a **pink** color appears — mark 3 (total acidity). **Calculation:** the result is expressed in titration units (t.u.) — the number of ml of 0.1 N NaOH needed to neutralize 100 ml of gastric juice: X = NaOH used (ml) × 20 (for 5 ml of juice).
s2803 Free HCl = NaOH used up to mark 1 × 20;
s2804 Total acidity = NaOH used up to mark 3 × 20;
s2805 Total HCl (free + bound) = the mean of marks 2 and 3 × 20; bound HCl = total HCl − free HCl. **Evaluation:** total acidity 40–60, free HCl 20–40, bound HCl 10–20 t.u. If there is no free HCl — **achlorhydria**; if there is neither HCl nor pepsin — **achylia**.
