s3032 Detoxification of the biogenic amines
s3033 So that the action of the biogenic amines is short, they are broken down quickly:
s3034 **Monoamine oxidases (MAO)** — on the outer mitochondrial membrane, containing FAD; oxidative deamination: R-CH2-NH2 + O2 + H2O → R-CHO + NH3 + H2O2. MAO-A breaks down serotonin and noradrenaline, MAO-B dopamine. The aldehydes formed are oxidized to acids (serotonin → 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid; catecholamines → vanillylmandelic acid) and excreted in the urine.
s3035 **Diamine oxidase (histaminase)** — oxidizes histamine, putrescine and cadaverine.
s3036 **Methylation** — **catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT)** methylates catecholamines at the expense of S-adenosylmethionine; histamine is also methylated.
s3037 **MAO inhibitors** (moclobemide — MAO-A, selegiline — MAO-B) and **selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors** (fluoxetine) are antidepressants: serotonin and noradrenaline increase in the synapse. In Parkinson disease (a lack of dopamine in the substantia nigra) **L-DOPA** (levodopa) is used — it crosses the blood-brain barrier, which dopamine cannot, and is converted into dopamine in the brain; it is given together with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor (carbidopa). If a patient taking MAO inhibitors eats food rich in tyramine (hard cheese, wine), a hypertensive crisis (the “cheese reaction”) may develop. **Pheochromocytoma** (a catecholamine-producing tumor) is diagnosed by a rise in vanillylmandelic acid and metanephrines in the urine; a **carcinoid tumor** by a rise in 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid in the urine.
s3038 Experiment 1: determination of ALT and AST activity in serum on a biochemical analyzer (kinetic method)
s3039 Principle of the method (IFCC):
s3040 **ALT**: L-alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + L-glutamate; pyruvate + NADH + H+ → lactate + NAD+ (the indicator enzyme is **lactate dehydrogenase**);
s3041 **AST**: L-aspartate + α-ketoglutarate → oxaloacetate + L-glutamate; oxaloacetate + NADH + H+
s3042 → malate + NAD+ (the indicator enzyme is **malate dehydrogenase**). NADH absorbs light at 340 nm while NAD+ does not; the **rate of fall** of the optical density is proportional to the enzyme activity. Pyridoxal phosphate is added to the reagent (to avoid a falsely low result in B6 deficiency).
s3043 Pour 1 ml of working reagent (substrates, NADH, LDH or MDH, PLP) into a cuvette and warm it at 37 °C for 5 minutes.
s3044 Add 100 µl of serum and mix.
s3045 After 1 minute measure the optical density at 340 nm and record it every minute for 3 minutes (automatically on the analyzer).
s3046 Calculate the mean ΔE/min. **Calculation:** activity (IU/l) = ΔE/min × F (F is the factor for the reagent and cuvette, derived from the molar absorption coefficient of NADH; for example, 1746).
s3047 **Evaluation:** compare with the normal values in the table and calculate the De Ritis ratio. Hemolyzed serum falsely raises AST (erythrocytes contain a lot of AST).
s3048 Experiment 2: determination of the aminotransferases by the Reitman–Frankel method (colorimetric) (a historical method)
s3049 **Principle of the method:** the pyruvate formed in transamination (or the pyruvate formed spontaneously from oxaloacetate) forms a hydrazone with **2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine**; in an alkaline medium it gives a red-brown color. The intensity of the color is proportional to the amount of pyruvate and therefore to the enzyme activity. The result is expressed in µmol pyruvate / (hour · ml); normal values: ALT 0.1–0.68, AST 0.1–0.45 mmol/(hour · l).
s3050 Experiment 3: the ninhydrin reaction for amino acids
s3051 Add to a test tube 1 ml of 1% glycine solution (or a protein hydrolysate) and 5 drops of 0.1% ninhydrin solution.
s3052 Boil for 1–2 minutes. **Result and analysis:** α-amino acids give a **blue-violet** color with ninhydrin (Ruhemann’s purple): the amino acid undergoes oxidative deamination and decarboxylation, and the ammonia released forms a colored compound with ninhydrin. Proline and hydroxyproline give a yellow color. This reaction underlies the detection of amino acids in chromatography.

s3125 The enzyme that is falsely increased in hemolyzed serum:
s3126 A 25-year-old patient has weakness, loss of appetite, nausea and heaviness under the right costal margin; jaundice appeared 3 days later. Tests: ALT 1200 IU/l, AST 680 IU/l, bilirubin 85 µmol/l (mainly conjugated and free), HBsAg positive.
s3127 Calculate the De Ritis ratio and interpret the result. Why has ALT risen more?
s3128 De Ritis = 680 / 1200 ≈ 0.57 (< 1) — cytolysis of hepatocytes (acute viral hepatitis B). ALT is located mainly in the cytosol of hepatocytes and is most abundant in the liver; when the membrane is damaged it is the first to be released into the blood. A large part of AST is in the mitochondria — it is released in quantity only in deep (necrotic) damage. The rise of ALT begins before the jaundice and is a sensitive sign of the activity of hepatitis.
s3129 A 58-year-old man says he had severe pain behind the sternum 8 hours ago. Tests: AST 180 IU/l, ALT 45 IU/l, CK-MB and troponin I sharply increased.
s3130 Calculate the De Ritis ratio. Which organ is damaged?
s3131 De Ritis = 180 / 45 = 4 (> 2) — damage to the myocardium. The heart muscle contains a lot of AST and little ALT; in myocardial infarction the cardiomyocytes undergo necrosis and AST, CK-MB, LDH1 and troponins are released into the blood. AST rises after 6–12 hours, peaks at 24–48 hours and normalizes in 4–7 days; today the troponins are considered the most specific marker.
s3132 A patient taking a MAO inhibitor for depression ate hard cheese and drank red wine at a party, after which he developed a severe headache, sweating and palpitations; his blood pressure is 210/120 mm Hg.
s3133 Explain the biochemical mechanism of this condition.
s3134 The “cheese reaction” — a hypertensive crisis. Cheese and wine contain a lot of tyramine (the decarboxylation product of tyrosine). Normally it is broken down by MAO in the intestinal wall and the liver. When MAO is inhibited, tyramine passes into the blood and displaces noradrenaline from the sympathetic nerve endings (an indirect sympathomimetic); because noradrenaline is also not broken down by MAO, its effect is stronger — the vessels constrict and blood pressure rises sharply. People taking MAO inhibitors are forbidden foods rich in tyramine.

s3170 1. Formation and toxicity of ammonia
s3171 Sources of ammonia:
s3172 deamination of amino acids (mainly the oxidative deamination of glutamate);
s3173 hydrolysis of amides (glutamine, asparagine);
s3174 oxidation of biogenic amines by MAO;
s3175 deamination of purine and pyrimidine bases (adenine, cytosine);
s3176 putrefaction of proteins and breakdown of urea by bacteria in the intestine (it reaches the liver through the portal vein). About 15–20 g of ammonia is formed per day in the human body, but its level in the blood is very low: **11–35 µmol/l** (normally < 50 µmol/l).
s3177 **Its toxic action** (especially on the brain):
s3178 in the brain ammonia forms glutamate with α-ketoglutarate (reductive amination) — **α-ketoglutarate falls**, the Krebs cycle slows down and ATP formation decreases;
s3179 glutamate and glutamine increase — **osmotic swelling** of the astrocytes (glutamine) and a disturbed balance of neurotransmitters (glutamate ↓ or ↑, GABA ↑);
s3180 NH4
s3181 + disrupts K+ channels and Na+,K+-ATPase, and the membrane potential changes;
s3182 the blood pH shifts to the alkaline side. Clinical signs: nausea, vomiting, lethargy, tremor, seizures, coma.
s3183 2. Temporary detoxification and transport of ammonia
s3184 The ammonia formed in the tissues is bound at once and transported to the liver and kidney in nontoxic forms:
s3185 **Synthesis of glutamine** (the main route; brain, muscle and other tissues): glutamate + NH3 + ATP → **glutamine** + ADP + Pi — **glutamine synthetase**. Glutamine is the main transport form of ammonia (the most abundant amino acid in the blood).
s3186 **Formation of alanine** (muscles): pyruvate is transaminated into alanine and goes to the liver — the **glucose-alanine cycle** (topic 7).
s3187 **Synthesis of asparagine** (less important). **In the kidney** glutamine is broken down by **glutaminase** into glutamate and NH3; NH3 passes into the tubular lumen, binds H+ and is excreted in the urine as **ammonium salts** (NH4
s3188 +) (≈ 0.5–1 g per day). This process **increases sharply in acidosis** — an important mechanism by which the kidney maintains the acid-base balance (topic 29).
s3189 3. Synthesis of urea (the ornithine cycle)
s3190 The **final detoxification** of ammonia is the synthesis of urea in the **liver**. The cycle was discovered by H. Krebs and K. Henseleit (1932). Urea is a slightly toxic, highly water-soluble, neutral substance; ≈ 80–90% of the body’s nitrogen is excreted in this form.
