s0434 **Activation of proenzymes (zymogens)** — partial proteolysis: trypsinogen → trypsin (enteropeptidase), pepsinogen → pepsin (HCl), blood clotting factors, the complement system.
s0435 **Covalent modification** — **phosphorylation–dephosphorylation**: protein kinases phosphorylate serine, threonine or tyrosine residues at the expense of ATP, and phosphoprotein phosphatases remove the phosphate. For example, glycogen phosphorylase is activated when phosphorylated, whereas glycogen synthase becomes inactive.
s0436 **Allosteric regulation** — binding of an effector. Often the end product of a metabolic pathway inhibits the first (key) enzyme — **negative feedback** (feedback inhibition).
s0437 **Protein–protein interaction** — binding or dissociation of regulatory subunits (activation of protein kinase A by cAMP: the regulatory subunits dissociate).
s0438 **Compartmentalization** — the location of enzymes in different compartments of the cell.

s0474 Experiment 1: determination of salivary amylase activity by the Wohlgemuth method
s0475 **Principle of the method:** amylase breaks down starch to dextrins and maltose. Starch gives a blue color with iodine, dextrins — violet-red, maltose — no color. Saliva is serially diluted and the smallest amount that completely breaks down the starch is found.
s0476 Rinse the mouth with water, collect 1 ml of saliva and dilute it with 9 ml of distilled water (1 : 10).
s0477 Pour 1 ml of saline into each of 10 test tubes. Add 1 ml of diluted saliva to tube 1, mix and transfer 1 ml of it to tube 2; serially dilute in this way up to tube 10 (1 ml is discarded from the last one). Degree of dilution: 1 : 20, 1 : 40 ... 1 : 10 240.
s0478 Add 2 ml of 0.1% starch solution to each tube, mix and place in a 38 °C thermostat for 30 minutes.
s0479 Cool the tubes (to stop the reaction) and add 1 drop of 0.1% iodine solution to each.
s0480 Identify the most diluted tube in which the starch has been completely broken down (yellow color, no blue). **Calculation:** amylase activity (A) is the number of ml of 0.1% starch solution broken down by 1 ml of undiluted saliva at 38 °C in 30 minutes. For example, if the yellow color is in the tube diluted 1 : 160: A = 2 ml × 160 = 320.
s0481 **Evaluation:** normal salivary amylase activity is **160–320 units** (Wohlgemuth). Urinary amylase (diastase) is normally 16–64 units; it rises sharply in acute pancreatitis.
s0482 Experiment 2: demonstrating the specificity of enzymes
s0483 Pour 1 ml of diluted saliva into each of two test tubes.
s0484 Add 1 ml of 1% starch to tube 1 and 1 ml of 1% sucrose solution to tube 2; keep at 38 °C for 15 minutes.
s0485 Perform the Trommer (or Fehling) reaction in both tubes: add 1 ml of 10% NaOH and 3–5 drops of 1% CuSO~4~ and heat. **Result and analysis:** in tube 1 a red precipitate (Cu~2~O) — starch has been broken down to maltose, a reducing sugar. In tube 2 the reaction is negative — amylase does not break down sucrose (sucrose is acted on by sucrase). This shows the relative (group) specificity of the enzyme.
s0486 Experiment 3: effect of temperature, activators and inhibitors on amylase activity
s0487 Keep a saliva + starch mixture in three tubes at 0 °C (ice), 38 °C and 100 °C (previously boiled saliva) for 10 minutes; test with iodine.
s0488 To three other tubes add, respectively, 1% NaCl (activator), 1% CuSO~4~ (inhibitor) and water, and keep with saliva + starch at 38 °C; test with iodine from time to time. **Result and analysis:** at 38 °C starch is broken down quickly; on ice — very slowly (reversible inactivation); with boiled saliva — not at all (denaturation). Cl^-^ ions activate amylase (breakdown speeds up), Cu^2+^ ions inhibit it.
s0489 Skill: determining the type of inhibition from a Lineweaver–Burk plot

s0491 If the lines cross on the y-axis (1/V~max~) — V~max~ is unchanged and K~m~ has increased: **competitive inhibition**.
s0492 If the lines cross on the x-axis (−1/K~m~) — K~m~ is unchanged and V~max~ has decreased: **noncompetitive inhibition**.

s0592 After working in a newly renovated warehouse, a patient was brought to hospital with profuse salivation, sweating, constricted pupils, bronchospasm and muscle twitching. Serum cholinesterase activity is sharply reduced. An organophosphorus pesticide had been used in the warehouse.
s0593 Explain the biochemical mechanism of the condition. On what principle is treatment based?
s0594 Organophosphorus compounds phosphorylate the serine residue in the active site of acetylcholinesterase and inhibit the enzyme irreversibly. Acetylcholine is not broken down and accumulates in the synapses — muscarinic (salivation, sweating, miosis, bronchospasm, bradycardia) and nicotinic (fasciculations, paralysis) signs develop. Treatment: atropine (blocks muscarinic receptors) and enzyme reactivators — oximes (pralidoxime): they remove the phosphate group from the serine and reactivate the enzyme (effective in the first hours, before the phosphorylated enzyme “ages”).
s0595 A patient drank methyl alcohol by mistake. He was prescribed intravenous ethyl alcohol solution and fomepizole.
s0596 On what biochemical principle is this treatment based?
s0597 Methanol is converted by alcohol dehydrogenase into formaldehyde and then into formic acid — they damage the optic nerve and cause metabolic acidosis. Ethanol is a substrate of the same enzyme and competes with methanol (it has a higher affinity for the enzyme); fomepizole is a competitive inhibitor of alcohol dehydrogenase. As a result the conversion of methanol into toxic products slows down, and it is excreted unchanged through the kidneys and lungs (hemodialysis is also used).

s0640 1. Structure and chemical composition of biological membranes
s0641 **Biological membranes** are lipid-protein structures 6–10 nm thick that surround the cell and its organelles. The cell has a plasma membrane and internal membranes (the membranes of the nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, mitochondria and lysosomes).
s0642 Membranes contain **lipids** (≈ 40%), **proteins** (≈ 50–60%) and **carbohydrates** (2–10%, as part of glycolipids and glycoproteins). In the myelin sheath lipids make up 75–80%, in the inner mitochondrial membrane proteins ≈ 75%.
s0643 Membrane lipids:
s0644 **Phospholipids** — glycerophospholipids (phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol) and sphingomyelin. They are **amphiphilic**: a hydrophilic “head” and two hydrophobic “tails” (fatty acid residues). In water they form a **lipid bilayer**.
s0645 **Glycolipids** — cerebrosides, gangliosides (mainly in the outer layer; receptor and antigen function).
s0646 **Cholesterol** — regulates the rigidity and fluidity of the membrane (a “buffer”): at high temperature it reduces fluidity, at low temperature it prevents solidification. **Membrane proteins:**
s0647 **Integral (transmembrane)** proteins — span the bilayer (channels, carriers, pumps, receptors).
s0648 **Peripheral** proteins — bound to the membrane surface by ionic and hydrogen bonds.
s0649 **Anchored** proteins — attached through a lipid “anchor” (a fatty acid, prenyl, glycosylphosphatidylinositol). **Fluid mosaic model** (S. Singer and G. Nicolson, 1972): the membrane is a fluid lipid bilayer in which proteins “float” and can move laterally.
