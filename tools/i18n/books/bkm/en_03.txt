s0433 **Activation of proenzymes (zymogens)** — partial proteolysis: trypsinogen → trypsin (enteropeptidase), pepsinogen → pepsin (HCl), blood clotting factors, the complement system.
s0434 **Covalent modification** — **phosphorylation–dephosphorylation**: protein kinases phosphorylate serine, threonine or tyrosine residues at the expense of ATP, and phosphoprotein phosphatases remove the phosphate. For example, glycogen phosphorylase is activated when phosphorylated, whereas glycogen synthase becomes inactive.
s0435 **Allosteric regulation** — binding of an effector. Often the end product of a metabolic pathway inhibits the first (key) enzyme — **negative feedback** (feedback inhibition).
s0436 **Protein–protein interaction** — binding or dissociation of regulatory subunits (activation of protein kinase A by cAMP: the regulatory subunits dissociate).
s0437 **Compartmentalization** — the location of enzymes in different compartments of the cell.

s0473 Experiment 1: determination of salivary amylase activity by the Wohlgemuth method
s0474 **Principle of the method:** amylase breaks down starch to dextrins and maltose. Starch gives a blue color with iodine, dextrins — violet-red, maltose — no color. Saliva is serially diluted and the smallest amount that completely breaks down the starch is found.
s0475 Rinse the mouth with water, collect 1 ml of saliva and dilute it with 9 ml of distilled water (1 : 10).
s0476 Pour 1 ml of saline into each of 10 test tubes. Add 1 ml of diluted saliva to tube 1, mix and transfer 1 ml of it to tube 2; serially dilute in this way up to tube 10 (1 ml is discarded from the last one). Degree of dilution: 1 : 20, 1 : 40 ... 1 : 10 240.
s0477 Add 2 ml of 0.1% starch solution to each tube, mix and place in a 38 °C thermostat for 30 minutes.
s0478 Cool the tubes (to stop the reaction) and add 1 drop of 0.1% iodine solution to each.
s0479 Identify the most diluted tube in which the starch has been completely broken down (yellow color, no blue). **Calculation:** amylase activity (A) is the number of ml of 0.1% starch solution broken down by 1 ml of undiluted saliva at 38 °C in 30 minutes. For example, if the yellow color is in the tube diluted 1 : 160: A = 2 ml × 160 = 320.
s0480 **Evaluation:** normal salivary amylase activity is **160–320 units** (Wohlgemuth). Urinary amylase (diastase) is normally 16–64 units; it rises sharply in acute pancreatitis.
s0481 Experiment 2: demonstrating the specificity of enzymes
s0482 Pour 1 ml of diluted saliva into each of two test tubes.
s0483 Add 1 ml of 1% starch to tube 1 and 1 ml of 1% sucrose solution to tube 2; keep at 38 °C for 15 minutes.
s0484 Perform the Trommer (or Fehling) reaction in both tubes: add 1 ml of 10% NaOH and 3–5 drops of 1% CuSO4 and heat. **Result and analysis:** in tube 1 a red precipitate (Cu2O) — starch has been broken down to maltose, a reducing sugar. In tube 2 the reaction is negative — amylase does not break down sucrose (sucrose is acted on by sucrase). This shows the relative (group) specificity of the enzyme.
s0485 Experiment 3: effect of temperature, activators and inhibitors on amylase activity
s0486 Keep a saliva + starch mixture in three tubes at 0 °C (ice), 38 °C and 100 °C (previously boiled saliva) for 10 minutes; test with iodine.
s0487 To three other tubes add, respectively, 1% NaCl (activator), 1% CuSO4 (inhibitor) and water, and keep with saliva + starch at 38 °C; test with iodine from time to time. **Result and analysis:** at 38 °C starch is broken down quickly; on ice — very slowly (reversible inactivation); with boiled saliva — not at all (denaturation). Cl- ions activate amylase (breakdown speeds up), Cu2+ ions inhibit it.
s0488 Skill: determining the type of inhibition from a Lineweaver–Burk plot
s0489 Draw the straight lines of 1/v against 1/[S] without inhibitor and with inhibitor.
s0490 If the lines cross on the y-axis (1/Vmax) — Vmax is unchanged and Km has increased: **competitive inhibition**.
s0491 If the lines cross on the x-axis (−1/Km) — Km is unchanged and Vmax has decreased: **noncompetitive inhibition**.

s0591 After working in a newly renovated warehouse, a patient was brought to hospital with profuse salivation, sweating, constricted pupils, bronchospasm and muscle twitching. Serum cholinesterase activity is sharply reduced. An organophosphorus pesticide had been used in the warehouse.
s0592 Explain the biochemical mechanism of the condition. On what principle is treatment based?
s0593 Organophosphorus compounds phosphorylate the serine residue in the active site of acetylcholinesterase and inhibit the enzyme irreversibly. Acetylcholine is not broken down and accumulates in the synapses — muscarinic (salivation, sweating, miosis, bronchospasm, bradycardia) and nicotinic (fasciculations, paralysis) signs develop. Treatment: atropine (blocks muscarinic receptors) and enzyme reactivators — oximes (pralidoxime): they remove the phosphate group from the serine and reactivate the enzyme (effective in the first hours, before the phosphorylated enzyme “ages”).
s0594 A patient drank methyl alcohol by mistake. He was prescribed intravenous ethyl alcohol solution and fomepizole.
s0595 On what biochemical principle is this treatment based?
s0596 Methanol is converted by alcohol dehydrogenase into formaldehyde and then into formic acid — they damage the optic nerve and cause metabolic acidosis. Ethanol is a substrate of the same enzyme and competes with methanol (it has a higher affinity for the enzyme); fomepizole is a competitive inhibitor of alcohol dehydrogenase. As a result the conversion of methanol into toxic products slows down, and it is excreted unchanged through the kidneys and lungs (hemodialysis is also used).
s0597 A 35-year-old patient suddenly developed severe girdle-like pain in the upper abdomen. Urinary diastase is 1024 units, and serum lipase and α-amylase are sharply increased.
s0598 Which organ is damaged? Why has enzyme activity increased?
s0599 Acute pancreatitis. Lipase is more specific for pancreatitis and persists longer in the blood.

s0639 1. Structure and chemical composition of biological membranes
s0640 **Biological membranes** are lipid-protein structures 6–10 nm thick that surround the cell and its organelles. The cell has a plasma membrane and internal membranes (the membranes of the nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, mitochondria and lysosomes).
s0641 Membranes contain **lipids** (≈ 40%), **proteins** (≈ 50–60%) and **carbohydrates** (2–10%, as part of glycolipids and glycoproteins). In the myelin sheath lipids make up 75–80%, in the inner mitochondrial membrane proteins ≈ 75%.
s0642 Membrane lipids:
s0643 **Phospholipids** — glycerophospholipids (phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol) and sphingomyelin. They are **amphiphilic**: a hydrophilic “head” and two hydrophobic “tails” (fatty acid residues). In water they form a **lipid bilayer**.
s0644 **Glycolipids** — cerebrosides, gangliosides (mainly in the outer layer; receptor and antigen function).
s0645 **Cholesterol** — regulates the rigidity and fluidity of the membrane (a “buffer”): at high temperature it reduces fluidity, at low temperature it prevents solidification. **Membrane proteins:**
s0646 **Integral (transmembrane)** proteins — span the bilayer (channels, carriers, pumps, receptors).
s0647 **Peripheral** proteins — bound to the membrane surface by ionic and hydrogen bonds.
s0648 **Anchored** proteins — attached through a lipid “anchor” (a fatty acid, prenyl, glycosylphosphatidylinositol). **Fluid mosaic model** (S. Singer and G. Nicolson, 1972): the membrane is a fluid lipid bilayer in which proteins “float” and can move laterally.
