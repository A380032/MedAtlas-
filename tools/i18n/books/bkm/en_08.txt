s1511 Transfer of the hydrogen of cytosolic NADH into the mitochondria
s1512 Under aerobic conditions the NADH formed in glycolysis must be oxidized in the respiratory chain, but the inner mitochondrial membrane is impermeable to NADH. The hydrogen passes through **shuttle mechanisms**:
s1513 **The malate-aspartate shuttle** (liver, kidney, heart): in the cytosol oxaloacetate is reduced to malate at the expense of NADH, malate enters the mitochondria, is oxidized back to oxaloacetate and NADH is formed in the matrix (≈ 2.5 ATP). Oxaloacetate returns in the form of aspartate.
s1514 **The glycerol phosphate shuttle** (skeletal muscle, brain): dihydroxyacetone phosphate is reduced to glycerol-3-phosphate at the expense of NADH; the FAD-dependent glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase of the mitochondrial membrane reoxidizes it and the electrons pass to CoQ (≈ 1.5 ATP).

s1549 Experiment 1: iodine reaction for starch and glycogen
s1550 Pour 1 ml of 1% starch and 1 ml of 1% glycogen solution into two test tubes.
s1551 Add 1 drop of Lugol’s solution to each; then heat and cool. **Result and analysis:** starch gives a blue color and glycogen a red-brown one (iodine enters the spiral of the polysaccharide and forms a complex; because the branches of glycogen are short, the color is different). On heating the spiral is destroyed and the color disappears; on cooling it returns.
s1552 Experiment 2: the Trommer reaction for reducing carbohydrates
s1553 Pour 1 ml each of 1% glucose, sucrose and lactose solutions into test tubes.
s1554 Add to each 1 ml of 10% NaOH and 3–5 drops of 1% CuSO~4~ (the blue Cu(OH)2 precipitate dissolves).
s1555 Heat the upper part to boiling. **Result and analysis:** with glucose and lactose a yellow (CuOH) and then a red-brick (Cu~2~O) precipitate forms — they are reducing sugars (they have a free hemiacetal hydroxyl). Sucrose gives no reaction (both hemiacetal hydroxyls are used in the glycosidic bond), but after hydrolysis with acid it gives a positive reaction.
s1556 Experiment 3: the Seliwanoff reaction for fructose
s1557 Add to a test tube 1 ml of 1% fructose solution and 1 ml of Seliwanoff reagent (a solution of resorcinol in concentrated HCl); heat to boiling.
s1558 Compare with glucose. **Result and analysis:** fructose (a ketose) quickly gives a red color — in acid it is converted into hydroxymethylfurfural, which forms a colored compound with resorcinol. With glucose (an aldose) the color develops very slowly and is weak. Sucrose is also positive (it contains fructose). This reaction is used to detect fructose in urine (fructosuria).

s1658 Von Gierke disease (glycogenosis type I) — glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency. Glycogen accumulates in the liver (hepatomegaly); because the last reaction of glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis is blocked, glucose is not released into the blood — severe fasting hypoglycemia; glucagon increases glycogenolysis, but glucose-6-phosphate goes into glycolysis and lactate rises. The excess of glucose-6-phosphate increases purine synthesis through the pentose phosphate pathway (uric acid ↑), and through glycolysis more acetyl-CoA and glycerol are formed and fat synthesis increases (hyperlipidemia). Low insulin and high glucagon also raise lipids.
s1659 A 22-year-old man feels severe pain and cramps in his muscles during the first minutes of a gym session; after a rest he can continue exercising (the “second wind” phenomenon). After heavy exercise his urine was brown. In the ischemic exercise test blood lactate did not rise.
s1660 Which enzyme is deficient? Why does lactate not rise? Explain the “second wind” phenomenon.
s1661 McArdle disease (glycogenosis type V) — deficiency of muscle glycogen phosphorylase. Muscle glycogen is not broken down, and at the start of exercise no glucose-6-phosphate is formed for glycolysis — an ATP deficit, muscle cramps, damage to muscle cells and release of myoglobin into the urine (brown urine). Because there is no glycolysis, lactate does not rise. The “second wind” — after a few minutes blood flow increases and more glucose and fatty acids reach the muscle from the blood, so the energy supply improves.

s1701 **Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate** is the “key” to glycolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver: insulin increases its formation and thereby glycolysis, while glucagon (through cAMP) decreases it and increases gluconeogenesis.
s1702 1. Anaerobic glycolysis
s1703 When oxygen is lacking (intense muscle work, hypoxia) or in cells without mitochondria (**erythrocytes**), pyruvate is reduced to lactate by **lactate dehydrogenase**:
s1704 Pyruvate + NADH + H^+^ → Lactate + NAD^+^
s1705 This reaction regenerates NAD^+^ for glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and allows glycolysis to continue without oxygen.
s1706 **The overall equation of anaerobic glycolysis:** Glucose + 2ADP + 2Pi → 2 Lactate + 2ATP + 2H~2~O
s1707 The energy yield is **2 ATP** (≈ 15 times less than in aerobic oxidation), but it is fast and needs no oxygen. Anaerobic glycolysis is characteristic mainly of erythrocytes, the retina and lens of the eye, the renal medulla, white muscle fibers and tumor cells.
s1708 **Alcoholic fermentation** (in yeast): pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO~2~ (pyruvate decarboxylase, TDP) → ethanol (alcohol dehydrogenase, NADH). Fermentation is used in baking bread and in making wine and beer.
s1709 **Lactic acidosis** — blood lactate > 5 mmol/l and pH < 7.35 (normal lactate 0.5–2.2 mmol/l). Causes: tissue hypoxia (shock, heart failure, sepsis, severe anemia — type A), liver failure (lactate is not used in gluconeogenesis), metformin, thiamine deficiency, mitochondrial diseases (type B). Tumor cells break glucose down to lactate even in the presence of oxygen
