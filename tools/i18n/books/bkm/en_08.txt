s1510 Transfer of the hydrogen of cytosolic NADH into the mitochondria
s1511 Under aerobic conditions the NADH formed in glycolysis must be oxidized in the respiratory chain, but the inner mitochondrial membrane is impermeable to NADH. The hydrogen passes through **shuttle mechanisms**:
s1512 **The malate-aspartate shuttle** (liver, kidney, heart): in the cytosol oxaloacetate is reduced to malate at the expense of NADH, malate enters the mitochondria, is oxidized back to oxaloacetate and NADH is formed in the matrix (≈ 2.5 ATP). Oxaloacetate returns in the form of aspartate.
s1513 **The glycerol phosphate shuttle** (skeletal muscle, brain): dihydroxyacetone phosphate is reduced to glycerol-3-phosphate at the expense of NADH; the FAD-dependent glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase of the mitochondrial membrane reoxidizes it and the electrons pass to CoQ (≈ 1.5 ATP).

s1548 Experiment 1: iodine reaction for starch and glycogen
s1549 Pour 1 ml of 1% starch and 1 ml of 1% glycogen solution into two test tubes.
s1550 Add 1 drop of Lugol’s solution to each; then heat and cool. **Result and analysis:** starch gives a blue color and glycogen a red-brown one (iodine enters the spiral of the polysaccharide and forms a complex; because the branches of glycogen are short, the color is different). On heating the spiral is destroyed and the color disappears; on cooling it returns.
s1551 Experiment 2: the Trommer reaction for reducing carbohydrates
s1552 Pour 1 ml each of 1% glucose, sucrose and lactose solutions into test tubes.
s1553 Add to each 1 ml of 10% NaOH and 3–5 drops of 1% CuSO4 (the blue Cu(OH)2 precipitate dissolves).
s1554 Heat the upper part to boiling. **Result and analysis:** with glucose and lactose a yellow (CuOH) and then a red-brick (Cu2O) precipitate forms — they are reducing sugars (they have a free hemiacetal hydroxyl). Sucrose gives no reaction (both hemiacetal hydroxyls are used in the glycosidic bond), but after hydrolysis with acid it gives a positive reaction.
s1555 Experiment 3: the Seliwanoff reaction for fructose
s1556 Add to a test tube 1 ml of 1% fructose solution and 1 ml of Seliwanoff reagent (a solution of resorcinol in concentrated HCl); heat to boiling.
s1557 Compare with glucose. **Result and analysis:** fructose (a ketose) quickly gives a red color — in acid it is converted into hydroxymethylfurfural, which forms a colored compound with resorcinol. With glucose (an aldose) the color develops very slowly and is weak. Sucrose is also positive (it contains fructose). This reaction is used to detect fructose in urine (fructosuria).

s1660 McArdle disease (glycogenosis type V) — deficiency of muscle glycogen phosphorylase. s1701 1. Anaerobic glycolysis
s1702 When oxygen is lacking (intense muscle work, hypoxia) or in cells without mitochondria (**erythrocytes**), pyruvate is reduced to lactate by **lactate dehydrogenase**:
s1703 Pyruvate + NADH + H+ → Lactate + NAD+
s1704 This reaction regenerates NAD+ for glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and allows glycolysis to continue without oxygen.
s1705 **The overall equation of anaerobic glycolysis:** Glucose + 2ADP + 2Pi → 2 Lactate + 2ATP + 2H2O
s1706 The energy yield is **2 ATP** (≈ 15 times less than in aerobic oxidation), but it is fast and needs no oxygen. Anaerobic glycolysis is characteristic mainly of erythrocytes, the retina and lens of the eye, the renal medulla, white muscle fibers and tumor cells.
s1707 **Alcoholic fermentation** (in yeast): pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO2 (pyruvate decarboxylase, TDP) → ethanol (alcohol dehydrogenase, NADH). Fermentation is used in baking bread and in making wine and beer.
s1708 **Lactic acidosis** — blood lactate > 5 mmol/l and pH < 7.35 (normal lactate 0.5–2.2 mmol/l). Causes: tissue hypoxia (shock, heart failure, sepsis, severe anemia — type A), liver failure (lactate is not used in gluconeogenesis), metformin, thiamine deficiency, mitochondrial diseases (type B). Tumor cells break glucose down to lactate even in the presence of oxygen
s1709 — the **Warburg effect**; this underlies the detection of tumors in PET scanning (^18^F-fluorodeoxyglucose).
s1710 2. Gluconeogenesis
s1711 **Gluconeogenesis** is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate substances. It takes place mainly in the **liver** (≈ 90%) and in the **renal cortex** (up to ≈ 40% in prolonged starvation). Its significance: maintaining blood glucose during starvation, when the glycogen store is exhausted (after 12–24 hours) — the brain (≈ 120 g per day), the erythrocytes and the renal medulla need glucose.
