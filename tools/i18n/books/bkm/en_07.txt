s1364 A 6-month-old child has had low muscle tone, developmental delay and seizures since birth. Blood lactate and pyruvate are sharply increased, the lactate/pyruvate ratio is normal, and alanine is also increased. The condition worsens after carbohydrate food.
s1365 Which enzyme deficiency is suspected? Why do carbohydrates worsen the condition? What diet is recommended?
s1366 Congenital deficiency of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. Pyruvate is not converted to acetyl-CoA and accumulates; it is converted to lactate (LDH) and to alanine (transamination). Carbohydrates form pyruvate through glycolysis and increase the acidosis. Because the brain obtains energy mainly from glucose oxidation, the neurological disorders are severe. Treatment: a ketogenic diet (low in carbohydrates, high in fats) — fatty acids and ketone bodies give acetyl-CoA bypassing the PDC; in some patients high-dose thiamine is effective.
s1367 After rats were given an inhibitor of one of the Krebs cycle enzymes, succinate accumulated in the tissues while the amounts of fumarate and malate decreased.
s1368 Which enzyme is inhibited? Name a possible inhibitor. How does this affect energy metabolism?
s1369 Succinate dehydrogenase (complex II) is inhibited; malonate is its classic competitive inhibitor. Succinate is not converted to fumarate and the cycle stops: the following metabolites and oxaloacetate decrease, acetyl-CoA cannot enter the cycle; less FADH2 and NADH are formed and ATP synthesis falls. Increasing the succinate concentration partly overcomes the inhibition.
s1370 In a patient after prolonged starvation, blood glucose is 3.0 mmol/l, ketone bodies are sharply increased and acetone is detected in the urine.
s1371 How does the activity of the Krebs cycle in the liver change in starvation? Why do ketone bodies increase?
s1372 In starvation, under the action of glucagon and cortisol, gluconeogenesis in the liver increases and oxaloacetate is used for it (it leaves for the cytosol as malate). At the same time the β-oxidation of fatty acids coming from adipose tissue increases and a lot of acetyl-CoA is formed. Because oxaloacetate is lacking, acetyl-CoA cannot form citrate and is not fully oxidized in the Krebs cycle — in the liver mitochondria it is converted into acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate (ketone bodies); these serve as an energy source for other tissues (brain, muscle).

s1408 1. Dietary carbohydrates and their functions
s1409 Carbohydrates provide 55–60% of the energy of food; the daily requirement of adults is 350–450 g. The main dietary carbohydrates are: **polysaccharides** — starch (amylose and amylopectin), glycogen (little in meat), cellulose and other dietary fiber; **disaccharides** — sucrose (glucose + fructose, α1→β2), lactose (galactose + glucose, β1→4), maltose (2 glucose, α1→4); **monosaccharides** — glucose, fructose.
s1410 **Functions of carbohydrates in the body:** energy (1 g → 4 kcal), storage (glycogen), plastic and structural (glycoproteins, proteoglycans, glycolipids, the ribose and deoxyribose of nucleotides), protective (mucous substances), receptor and recognition (glycocalyx, blood group antigens), and a carbon skeleton for the synthesis of other substances (amino acids, fats).
s1411 **Dietary fiber** (cellulose, pectin, hemicellulose) is not digested in humans, but it increases intestinal peristalsis, binds and removes cholesterol and bile acids, and serves as food for the beneficial microflora.

s1459 4. Glycogen metabolism
s1460 **Glycogen** is a branched polymer of glucose (α1→4 bonds, with an α1→6 branch every 8–12 residues). It is stored mainly in the **liver** (≈ 100–120 g, 5–6% of its mass; to maintain the blood glucose level) and in **skeletal muscle** (≈ 300–400 g, 1–2% of its mass; only for the muscle’s own energy).

s1464 Glucose → glucose-6-phosphate (hexokinase/glucokinase, ATP).

s1469 Breakdown of glycogen (glycogenolysis)
s1470 **Glycogen phosphorylase** (the key enzyme; coenzyme — pyridoxal phosphate) cleaves α1→4 bonds from the end of the chain with phosphate (phosphorolysis): glycogen(n) + Pi → glycogen(n−1) + glucose-1-phosphate.
s1471 The **debranching enzyme** (transferase + amylo-1,6-glucosidase) removes the branch point, and free glucose is released.
s1472 Glucose-1-phosphate → glucose-6-phosphate (phosphoglucomutase).
s1473 **In the liver**: glucose-6-phosphatase → free glucose → into the blood. **In muscle** there is no glucose-6-phosphatase — glucose-6-phosphate enters glycolysis (muscle glycogen does not serve to maintain blood glucose).
s1474 Regulation of glycogen metabolism
s1475 Glycogen synthase and glycogen phosphorylase are regulated in **opposite** directions — through **phosphorylation–dephosphorylation**:
s1476 **When phosphorylated**: phosphorylase is **active**, glycogen synthase is **inactive** → glycogen is broken down.
s1477 **When dephosphorylated**: phosphorylase is inactive, glycogen synthase is **active** → glycogen is synthesized.

s1493 5. Aerobic oxidation of glucose. Glycolysis
s1494 **Glycolysis** is the breakdown of glucose in the cytosol to **pyruvate** (10 reactions). Under aerobic conditions pyruvate is converted in the mitochondria into acetyl-CoA and oxidized in the Krebs cycle to CO2 and H2O — **aerobic oxidation**. Under anaerobic conditions pyruvate is reduced to lactate (topic 7).
