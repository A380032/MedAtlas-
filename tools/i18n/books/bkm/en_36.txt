s6900 When ethanol is oxidized by alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase, a lot of NADH is formed and the NADH/NAD+ ratio rises. As a result, pyruvate is reduced to lactate (lactic acidosis) and oxaloacetate to malate — substrates for gluconeogenesis are lacking; when the glycogen store is exhausted on an empty stomach, hypoglycemia develops (confusion). Lactate competes with urate secretion in the kidney — hyperuricemia. β-Oxidation slows because of the excess NADH, glycerol-3-phosphate increases and TAG synthesis is enhanced — hypertriglyceridemia and fatty liver. Acetyl-CoA formed from acetate is converted to ketone bodies when oxaloacetate is lacking, and NADH reduces acetoacetate to β-hydroxybutyrate — alcoholic ketoacidosis. The increase in GGT is due to the induction of CYP2E1 and microsomal enzymes and to liver damage; macrocytosis is due to the direct effect of alcohol on the bone marrow and folate deficiency. Treatment: thiamine before giving glucose (to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy).

s6995 4. Hierarchy of regulatory systems. The hypothalamic–pituitary system
s6996 **Hierarchy:** cerebral cortex → **hypothalamus** (releasing and inhibiting hormones) → **pituitary** (tropic hormones) → **peripheral endocrine glands** → target tissues. Regulation is based on the principle of **negative feedback**: the peripheral hormone inhibits the secretion of the hypothalamus and pituitary.

s7019 5. Interrelation of the endocrine and nervous systems. Ontogenesis
s7020 The hypothalamus is the center that links the nervous and endocrine systems: its neurons produce neurohormones (**neurosecretion**). The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion (acetylcholine from preganglionic fibers triggers epinephrine secretion). Hormones affect brain function (thyroid hormones — brain development, cortisol — mood and memory, sex hormones — behavior); in stress the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal system and the sympathoadrenal system are activated together. Circadian rhythms (cortisol high in the morning, melatonin at night) are controlled by the suprachiasmatic nucleus.
s7021 **Changes in ontogenesis:** in the fetal period thyroid hormones are crucial for brain and skeletal development (congenital hypothyroidism — cretinism; newborn screening — TSH); in childhood somatotropin and IGF-1 ensure growth; at puberty gonadoliberin pulsatility begins and sex hormones increase; in old age somatotropin (somatopause), sex hormones (menopause, andropause) and DHEA decrease, and insulin sensitivity declines.
s7022 6. Synthesis and catabolism of steroid hormones
s7023 All steroid hormones are synthesized **from cholesterol** (obtained mainly through LDL). The key (rate-limiting) step is the transport of cholesterol to the inner mitochondrial membrane by the **StAR protein** and its conversion to **pregnenolone** by **CYP11A1** (the side-chain cleavage enzyme); this step is stimulated through cAMP by ACTH (adrenal gland) and LH (gonads). The enzymes of synthesis belong mainly to the cytochrome P450 family.

s7031 **Congenital adrenal hyperplasia** — most often **21-hydroxylase** deficiency (autosomal recessive): cortisol and aldosterone are not synthesized; by feedback ACTH increases, the gland becomes hyperplastic and the accumulated 17α-hydroxyprogesterone is converted into androgens — virilization of the external genitalia in girls, salt-wasting crisis (hyponatremia, hyperkalemia). Newborn screening — blood 17-hydroxyprogesterone. Treatment — replacement with glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids.
s7032 **Transport:** in the blood most steroids are bound to proteins: cortisol — to **transcortin** (≈ 90%) and albumin; testosterone and estradiol — to **sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG)** and albumin; only the free fraction (1–3%) is active.
s7033 **Catabolism (in the liver):** reduction of ring A, hydroxylation, then conjugation with **glucuronic acid** or **sulfate** — water-soluble metabolites are excreted in the urine: **17-hydroxycorticosteroids** from cortisol, **17-ketosteroids** from androgens and part of cortisol, **pregnanediol** from progesterone, and estriol glucuronides from estrogens. The daily urinary free cortisol is a screening test for Cushing syndrome. In liver cirrhosis the inactivation of estrogens decreases (topic 26).
s7034 7. Mechanism of action of sex hormones
s7035 **Androgens** (testosterone, dihydrotestosterone) bind to the androgen receptor (nuclear): formation of the male genitalia in the fetal period, secondary sexual characteristics at puberty, spermatogenesis (together with FSH), **anabolic effect** (muscle protein synthesis, erythropoiesis, bone mass), libido. **Androgen receptor defect** — complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (genotype XY, female phenotype).
s7036 **Estrogens** (estradiol, estrone, estriol) — estrogen receptors (ERα, ERβ): development of the female genitalia and mammary glands, endometrial proliferation (the 1st phase of the menstrual cycle), reduction of bone resorption (osteoporosis after menopause), raising HDL and lowering LDL (atheroprotective), increasing the hepatic synthesis of transport proteins (SHBG, transcortin, thyroxine-binding globulin) and clotting factors (risk of thrombosis with oral contraceptives).
s7037 **Progesterone** — progesterone receptor: secretory phase of the endometrium, maintenance of pregnancy (reduces myometrial excitability), development of the alveoli of the mammary glands, thermogenic effect (rise in basal temperature).
s7038 **Pharmacological use:** oral contraceptives (estrogen + progestin — suppress gonadotropin secretion by feedback), tamoxifen (an estrogen antagonist in the breast), aromatase inhibitors (letrozole), 5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride — in prostatic adenoma), antiandrogens, anabolic steroids (abuse — liver, heart, infertility).
s7039 8. Chorionic gonadotropin
s7040 **Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)** is a glycoprotein produced by the **syncytiotrophoblast**; its α subunit is identical to that of LH, FSH and TSH, while the **β subunit** is specific (that is why tests detect β-hCG). Its function is to act like LH and maintain the **corpus luteum** and progesterone secretion during the first 8–10 weeks of pregnancy (after that the placenta produces progesterone itself). It is detectable in the blood 8–10 days after fertilization, doubles every 48–72 hours and peaks at 8–10 weeks. At high concentrations it stimulates TSH receptors (signs of hyperthyroidism in hyperemesis of pregnancy).
s7041 **Diagnostic importance:** early detection of pregnancy; ectopic (extrauterine) pregnancy — hCG rises slowly; threatened miscarriage; trophoblastic diseases (**hydatidiform mole**, **choriocarcinoma** — very high hCG; a tumor marker); embryonal tumors of the testis; prenatal screening (elevated in Down syndrome in the 2nd trimester).
