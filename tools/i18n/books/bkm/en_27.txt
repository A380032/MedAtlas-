s5348 A 6-year-old child has swelling of the face, legs and abdomen. Urine protein is 8 g/day. Blood total protein 42 g/l, albumin 15 g/l, cholesterol 11 mmol/l. The proteinogram shows α2-globulins 28%.
s5349 What syndrome is this? What is the mechanism of the edema? Why are α2-globulins and cholesterol increased?
s5350 Nephrotic syndrome. The glomerular filter is damaged, and albumin (a small protein) is lost in large amounts in the urine — hypoalbuminemia; the oncotic pressure falls and fluid leaves the vessels for the tissues (edema); the decrease in blood volume activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and enhances retention of Na+ and water. α2-Macroglobulin (a large molecule, 720 kDa) is not filtered, while the liver increases the synthesis of all proteins in response to the fall in oncotic pressure — a relative and absolute increase. For the same reason the synthesis of lipoproteins (LDL) in the liver increases and LPL activity decreases — hypercholesterolemia.
s5351 In winter a coal stove was burning in a closed room. In the morning the family members woke up with headache, nausea and weakness; one was unconscious. The skin was pinkish red. The pulse oximeter showed SpO2 98%; CO-oximetry showed HbCO 35%.
s5352 What is the diagnosis? Why did the pulse oximeter show a normal value? What are the mechanism of poisoning and the principles of treatment?
s5353 Carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning. CO binds to the iron of hemoglobin ≈ 200–250 times more strongly than O2, forming carboxyhemoglobin; the remaining hemes release O2 less readily (the dissociation curve shifts to the left) — tissue hypoxia. CO also inhibits cytochrome oxidase. An ordinary pulse oximeter cannot distinguish HbCO from HbO2 (both are red) — a falsely normal value; CO-oximetry is needed. Treatment: removal from the source of poisoning, 100% oxygen (the half-life of HbCO shortens from 4–5 hours to ≈ 1 hour), hyperbaric oxygenation in severe cases.

s5409 2. Absorption, transport and storage of iron
s5410 Figure 21.1
s5411 Iron metabolism and its regulation by hepcidin (drawn after Lippincott’s Illustrated Reviews: Biochemistry)
s5412 **Absorption:** the daily diet supplies 10–20 mg of iron, of which only **1–2 mg** (10%) is absorbed (3–5 times more in deficiency) — mainly in the **duodenum and the initial part of the jejunum**.
s5413 **Heme iron** (meat, liver, fish) is well absorbed (20–30%);
s5414 **nonheme iron** (plants) — Fe3+ is reduced to Fe2+ by **duodenal cytochrome b** (a ferrireductase) on the surface of the intestinal cell and by **ascorbic acid**, and enters through **DMT1**. Gastric HCl, ascorbate, fructose and amino acids improve absorption; phytates (grain), polyphenols (tea, coffee), phosphates, calcium and antacids reduce it. In the enterocyte iron is stored as **ferritin** (the intestinal cell is shed in 2–3 days — the iron is lost) or released into the blood through **ferroportin** in the basolateral membrane; **hephaestin** (a Cu-containing protein) oxidizes it to Fe3+.
s5415 **Transport: transferrin** (a β-globulin synthesized in the liver) binds 2 Fe3+ ions. Normally ≈ 1/3 of transferrin is saturated (**saturation coefficient** 20–45%). Cells take up iron by endocytosis through the **transferrin receptor (TfR1)**; in the acidic medium of the endosome the iron is released, and apotransferrin and the receptor return to the membrane.
s5416 **Storage: ferritin** — up to 4500 Fe3+ atoms (as hydroxyphosphate) inside a shell of 24 subunits; it is water-soluble and rapidly mobilized. Serum ferritin reflects storage iron (1 µg/l ≈ 8–10 mg of storage iron). **Hemosiderin** is an insoluble aggregate formed by the breakdown of ferritin in lysosomes; it accumulates in iron overload (stains with Prussian blue).
s5417 **Recycling:** erythropoiesis requires ≈ 20–25 mg of iron per day — almost all of it is obtained from the heme of senescent erythrocytes in the macrophages of the spleen and liver (**heme oxygenase**). The body has **no** mechanism for active iron excretion: the daily loss is ≈ 1 mg (intestinal and skin epithelium), in women an additional ≈ 0.5–1 mg with menstruation, and in pregnancy ≈ 3–4 mg/day.
s5418 3. Regulation of iron metabolism
s5419 **Systemic regulation — hepcidin:** a peptide hormone of 25 amino acids synthesized in the liver. It binds to ferroportin and causes its endocytosis and degradation — iron absorption from the intestine and iron release from macrophages decrease.
s5420 Hepcidin **increases** in iron overload and **in inflammation (IL-6)** — the cause of anemia of chronic disease;
s5421 hepcidin **decreases** in iron deficiency, hypoxia, enhanced erythropoiesis (erythroferrone) and hemochromatosis (HFE, hemojuvelin defect). **Intracellular regulation — the IRE/IRP system:** when iron is low, **iron regulatory proteins (IRP)** bind to the **iron-responsive elements (IRE)** in mRNA:
s5422 binding to the IRE at the 5’-end of **ferritin** mRNA, they **stop translation** (stores decrease);
s5423 binding to the IRE at the 3’-end of **transferrin receptor** mRNA, they **stabilize** the mRNA (iron uptake increases). When iron is abundant, IRP dissociates — the opposite result.

s5438 **Iron deficiency anemia** is the most common anemia: chronic blood loss (menstruation, gastrointestinal bleeding — in adults a tumor must be excluded), pregnancy, periods of growth, malabsorption (celiac disease, gastric resection), diet. A **microcytic, hypochromic** anemia (MCV, MCH ↓); sideropenic signs — dry skin, hair loss, brittle nails (koilonychia), altered taste (eating chalk or ice — pica). Stages: prelatent (stores ↓ — ferritin ↓), latent (transport iron ↓), overt anemia. Treatment — oral Fe2+ preparations (with ascorbic acid), intravenous if necessary.
s5439 **Anemia of chronic disease (inflammation)** — IL-6 → hepcidin ↑ → iron is “trapped” in macrophages (ferritin N or ↑) and does not reach erythropoiesis.
s5440 **Hemochromatosis: hereditary** — mutation of the HFE gene (C282Y, autosomal recessive): low hepcidin → persistently high iron absorption → liver (cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma), pancreas (“bronze diabetes”), heart (cardiomyopathy), skin pigmentation, joints, hypogonadism. **Secondary** — multiple blood transfusions (thalassemia), ineffective erythropoiesis. Treatment — phlebotomy (bloodletting), deferoxamine, deferasirox (chelators).
s5441 **Acute iron poisoning** (iron tablets in children): gastrointestinal injury, metabolic acidosis, liver necrosis; antidote — deferoxamine.
s5442 5. Hemostasis. The vascular–platelet component
s5443 **Hemostasis** is the system that stops bleeding when a vessel is damaged and keeps the blood in a fluid state. Its components: the vessel wall, platelets, coagulation factors, anticoagulants, fibrinolysis.
s5444 Vascular–platelet (primary) hemostasis:
s5445 **Vasoconstriction** (thromboxane A2, serotonin, endothelin).
s5446 **Adhesion** — platelets adhere to exposed subendothelial collagen through **von Willebrand factor** (vWF) and the GPIb-IX-V receptor, and directly to collagen through GPVI and integrin α2β1.
s5447 **Activation and secretion** — platelets change shape and release ADP, serotonin, Ca2+, vWF, fibrinogen and factor V from their granules; **thromboxane A~2~** is synthesized (from arachidonic acid by **cyclooxygenase-1**); phosphatidylserine of the inner leaflet of the membrane moves to the outer leaflet — a surface for the coagulation complexes.
s5448 **Aggregation** — the **GPIIb/IIIa** receptor (integrin αIIbβ3) is activated and links platelets to one another through **fibrinogen** bridges — the **platelet plug**. Normally the **endothelium** is antithrombotic: **prostacyclin (PGI~2~)** and **nitric oxide (NO)** stop aggregation and vasoconstriction (through cAMP and cGMP), ecto-ADPase breaks down ADP, and thrombomodulin, heparan sulfate, t-PA and tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) are produced.
