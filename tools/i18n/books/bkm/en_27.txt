s5326 A 6-year-old child has swelling of the face, legs and abdomen. Urine protein is 8 g/day. Blood total protein 42 g/l, albumin 15 g/l, cholesterol 11 mmol/l. The proteinogram shows α~2~-globulins 28%.
s5327 What syndrome is this? What is the mechanism of the edema? Why are α~2~-globulins and cholesterol increased?
s5328 Nephrotic syndrome. The glomerular filter is damaged, and albumin (a small protein) is lost in large amounts in the urine — hypoalbuminemia; the oncotic pressure falls and fluid leaves the vessels for the tissues (edema); the decrease in blood volume activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and enhances retention of Na^+^ and water. α~2~-Macroglobulin (a large molecule, 720 kDa) is not filtered, while the liver increases the synthesis of all proteins in response to the fall in oncotic pressure — a relative and absolute increase. For the same reason the synthesis of lipoproteins (LDL) in the liver increases and LPL activity decreases — hypercholesterolemia.
s5329 In winter a coal stove was burning in a closed room. In the morning the family members woke up with headache, nausea and weakness; one was unconscious. The skin was pinkish red. The pulse oximeter showed SpO~2~ 98%; CO-oximetry showed HbCO 35%.
s5330 What is the diagnosis? Why did the pulse oximeter show a normal value? What are the mechanism of poisoning and the principles of treatment?
s5331 Carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning. CO binds to the iron of hemoglobin ≈ 200–250 times more strongly than O~2~, forming carboxyhemoglobin; the remaining hemes release O~2~ less readily (the dissociation curve shifts to the left) — tissue hypoxia. CO also inhibits cytochrome oxidase. An ordinary pulse oximeter cannot distinguish HbCO from HbO~2~ (both are red) — a falsely normal value; CO-oximetry is needed. Treatment: removal from the source of poisoning, 100% oxygen (the half-life of HbCO shortens from 4–5 hours to ≈ 1 hour), hyperbaric oxygenation in severe cases.

Treatment: stopping the provoking drugs, intravenous hemin (inhibits ALA synthase by feedback) and glucose (reduces the induction of ALA synthase).

s5387 2. Absorption, transport and storage of iron
s5388 Figure 21.1
s5389 Iron metabolism and its regulation by hepcidin (drawn after Lippincott’s Illustrated Reviews: Biochemistry)
s5390 **Absorption:** the daily diet supplies 10–20 mg of iron, of which only **1–2 mg** (10%) is absorbed (3–5 times more in deficiency) — mainly in the **duodenum and the initial part of the jejunum**.
s5391 **Heme iron** (meat, liver, fish) is well absorbed (20–30%);
s5392 **nonheme iron** (plants) — Fe^3+^ is reduced to Fe^2+^ by **duodenal cytochrome b** (a ferrireductase) on the surface of the intestinal cell and by **ascorbic acid**, and enters through **DMT1**. Gastric HCl, ascorbate, fructose and amino acids improve absorption; phytates (grain), polyphenols (tea, coffee), phosphates, calcium and antacids reduce it. In the enterocyte iron is stored as **ferritin** (the intestinal cell is shed in 2–3 days — the iron is lost) or released into the blood through **ferroportin** in the basolateral membrane; **hephaestin** (a Cu-containing protein) oxidizes it to Fe^3+^.
s5393 **Transport: transferrin** (a β-globulin synthesized in the liver) binds 2 Fe^3+^ ions. Normally ≈ 1/3 of transferrin is saturated (**saturation coefficient** 20–45%). Cells take up iron by endocytosis through the **transferrin receptor (TfR1)**; in the acidic medium of the endosome the iron is released, and apotransferrin and the receptor return to the membrane.
s5394 **Storage: ferritin** — up to 4500 Fe^3+^ atoms (as hydroxyphosphate) inside a shell of 24 subunits; it is water-soluble and rapidly mobilized. Serum ferritin reflects storage iron (1 µg/l ≈ 8–10 mg of storage iron). **Hemosiderin** is an insoluble aggregate formed by the breakdown of ferritin in lysosomes; it accumulates in iron overload (stains with Prussian blue).
s5395 **Recycling:** erythropoiesis requires ≈ 20–25 mg of iron per day — almost all of it is obtained from the heme of senescent erythrocytes in the macrophages of the spleen and liver (**heme oxygenase**). The body has **no** mechanism for active iron excretion: the daily loss is ≈ 1 mg (intestinal and skin epithelium), in women an additional ≈ 0.5–1 mg with menstruation, and in pregnancy ≈ 3–4 mg/day.
s5396 3. Regulation of iron metabolism
s5397 **Systemic regulation — hepcidin:** a peptide hormone of 25 amino acids synthesized in the liver. It binds to ferroportin and causes its endocytosis and degradation — iron absorption from the intestine and iron release from macrophages decrease.
s5398 Hepcidin **increases** in iron overload and **in inflammation (IL-6)** — the cause of anemia of chronic disease;
s5399 hepcidin **decreases** in iron deficiency, hypoxia, enhanced erythropoiesis (erythroferrone) and hemochromatosis (HFE, hemojuvelin defect). **Intracellular regulation — the IRE/IRP system:** when iron is low, **iron regulatory proteins (IRP)** bind to the **iron-responsive elements (IRE)** in mRNA:
s5400 binding to the IRE at the 5’-end of **ferritin** mRNA, they **stop translation** (stores decrease);
s5401 binding to the IRE at the 3’-end of **transferrin receptor** mRNA, they **stabilize** the mRNA (iron uptake increases). When iron is abundant, IRP dissociates — the opposite result.

s5416 **Iron deficiency anemia** is the most common anemia: chronic blood loss (menstruation, gastrointestinal bleeding — in adults a tumor must be excluded), pregnancy, periods of growth, malabsorption (celiac disease, gastric resection), diet. A **microcytic, hypochromic** anemia (MCV, MCH ↓); sideropenic signs — dry skin, hair loss, brittle nails (koilonychia), altered taste (eating chalk or ice — pica). Stages: prelatent (stores ↓ — ferritin ↓), latent (transport iron ↓), overt anemia. Treatment — oral Fe^2+^ preparations (with ascorbic acid), intravenous if necessary.
s5417 **Anemia of chronic disease (inflammation)** — IL-6 → hepcidin ↑ → iron is “trapped” in macrophages (ferritin N or ↑) and does not reach erythropoiesis.
s5418 **Hemochromatosis: hereditary** — mutation of the HFE gene (C282Y, autosomal recessive): low hepcidin → persistently high iron absorption → liver (cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma), pancreas (“bronze diabetes”), heart (cardiomyopathy), skin pigmentation, joints, hypogonadism. **Secondary** — multiple blood transfusions (thalassemia), ineffective erythropoiesis. Treatment — phlebotomy (bloodletting), deferoxamine, deferasirox (chelators).
s5419 **Acute iron poisoning** (iron tablets in children): gastrointestinal injury, metabolic acidosis, liver necrosis; antidote — deferoxamine.
s5420 5. Hemostasis. The vascular–platelet component
s5421 **Hemostasis** is the system that stops bleeding when a vessel is damaged and keeps the blood in a fluid state. Its components: the vessel wall, platelets, coagulation factors, anticoagulants, fibrinolysis.
s5422 Vascular–platelet (primary) hemostasis:
s5423 **Vasoconstriction** (thromboxane A~2~, serotonin, endothelin).
s5424 **Adhesion** — platelets adhere to exposed subendothelial collagen through **von Willebrand factor** (vWF) and the GPIb-IX-V receptor, and directly to collagen through GPVI and integrin α~2~β~1~.
s5425 **Activation and secretion** — platelets change shape and release ADP, serotonin, Ca^2+^, vWF, fibrinogen and factor V from their granules; **thromboxane A~2~** is synthesized (from arachidonic acid by **cyclooxygenase-1**); phosphatidylserine of the inner leaflet of the membrane moves to the outer leaflet — a surface for the coagulation complexes.
s5426 **Aggregation** — the **GPIIb/IIIa** receptor (integrin αIIbβ~3~) is activated and links platelets to one another through **fibrinogen** bridges — the **platelet plug**. Normally the **endothelium** is antithrombotic: **prostacyclin (PGI~2~)** and **nitric oxide (NO)** stop aggregation and vasoconstriction (through cAMP and cGMP), ecto-ADPase breaks down ADP, and thrombomodulin, heparan sulfate, t-PA and tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) are produced.
