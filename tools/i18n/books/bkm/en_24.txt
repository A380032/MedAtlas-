s4728 An 8-year-old child has many freckles, dryness and ulcers on sun-exposed areas of the skin; a basal cell carcinoma was found on the face. The parents are related and healthy.
s4729 What is the disease? What is its molecular basis? What is the mode of inheritance?
s4730 Xeroderma pigmentosum. Ultraviolet rays form covalent bonds between adjacent pyrimidines in DNA — thymine dimers. They are repaired by the nucleotide excision repair (NER) system; in this disease the XP proteins (enzymes that recognize and excise the damage) are defective — the dimers accumulate and lead to mutations during replication, including in p53 and other genes, so skin cancer develops at an early age. The inheritance is autosomal recessive (consanguineous marriage increases the risk). Treatment and prevention — complete protection from sunlight and early removal of tumors.
s4731 A 35-year-old woman's mother and maternal aunt died of breast cancer before the age of 45. Genetic testing of the woman found a frameshift mutation in the BRCA1 gene (heterozygous).
s4732 What is the function of the BRCA1 protein? Why does even a single mutant allele increase the risk of cancer? Which group of drugs is used in treatment?
s4733 BRCA1 takes part in the accurate repair of DNA double-strand breaks by homologous recombination (a tumor suppressor). In an inherited mutation one allele is inactive in all cells; if the second allele is lost by a somatic mutation during life (Knudson's “two-hit” hypothesis), homologous recombination does not work, the genome becomes unstable and a tumor develops. Therefore the inheritance appears dominant (risk ≈ 60–70%). PARP inhibitors (olaparib) are used in treatment: by blocking the repair of single-strand breaks, PARP inhibition selectively kills tumor cells with defective homologous recombination (synthetic lethality).
s4734 In a patient with chronic lymphocytic leukemia, an excess of the Bcl-2 protein was found in the tumor cells. The effect of chemotherapy is poor. The drug venetoclax was recommended.
s4735 How does Bcl-2 excess contribute to tumor growth? Why is chemotherapy ineffective? What is the mechanism of action of venetoclax?
s4736 Bcl-2 is an antiapoptotic protein: it blocks Bax and Bak and prevents the increase in mitochondrial membrane permeability and the release of cytochrome c. As a result, damaged tumor cells do not undergo apoptosis and accumulate. Many chemotherapy drugs damage DNA and trigger the intrinsic apoptotic pathway through p53 — when Bcl-2 is in excess, this pathway is blocked. Venetoclax is a Bcl-2 inhibitor (a BH3 mimetic): it releases the proapoptotic proteins and restores apoptosis in tumor cells.

s4807 2. Chemical carcinogenesis
s4808 Most chemical carcinogens are **procarcinogens**: they are not active themselves but are converted in the body by the **cytochrome P450** system (topic 26) into electrophilic **ultimate carcinogens**, which form covalent **adducts** with nucleophilic centers of DNA (most often the N7 or N2 atom of guanine). Direct-acting carcinogens (alkylating agents) do not require activation.

s4811 **Cytochrome P450 polymorphism and carcinogenesis:** smokers with highly inducible variants of CYP1A1 have a higher risk of lung cancer; gene variants of detoxifying enzymes (glutathione S-transferase GSTM1, N-acetyltransferase NAT2) affect the risk: for example, the risk is higher in smokers who lack the GSTM1 gene (null genotype); in slow acetylators, bladder cancer caused by aromatic amines is more common.
s4812 Tobacco smoke contains more than 60 carcinogens. Benzo(a)pyrene diol epoxide causes G→T transversions at certain “hot spots” of the TP53 gene (codons 157, 248, 273) — the very same mutations are found in lung cancer in smokers. This is evidence of a direct molecular link between smoking and cancer.
s4813 3. Proto-oncogenes, oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes
s4814 **Proto-oncogenes** are normal genes that stimulate cell growth, division and differentiation. As a result of an activating mutation they become **oncogenes** (a **dominant** effect — one allele is enough; “a stuck gas pedal”).
s4815 Mechanisms of proto-oncogene activation:
s4816 **Point mutation** — the RAS protein loses its GTPase activity and remains permanently active (codons 12, 13, 61; in ≈ 30% of tumors);
s4817 **Gene amplification** — MYC (N-MYC in neuroblastoma), HER2/neu (≈ 20% of breast cancers);
s4818 **Chromosomal translocation** — the gene comes under a strong promoter (Burkitt lymphoma: t(8;14) — MYC next to an immunoglobulin gene) or a hybrid protein is formed (chronic myeloid leukemia: t(9;22) — **BCR-ABL**, a constitutively active tyrosine kinase);
s4819 **Integration next to a viral promoter** (insertional mutagenesis).

s4860 4. The multistage theory of carcinogenesis
s4861 A tumor develops not as a result of a single mutation but as a result of successive changes in several (usually 4–7) genes over years (in the colon: APC → KRAS → SMAD4 → TP53 — the **Vogelstein model**: normal epithelium → adenoma → carcinoma).
