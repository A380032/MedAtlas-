s6722 **Types of reactions:** aliphatic and aromatic hydroxylation, epoxidation, N-, O- and S-dealkylation, deamination, N- and S-oxidation, dehalogenation.
s6723 **Toxic activation (lethal synthesis):** some substances become more toxic under the action of P450: benzo(a)pyrene → diol epoxide (a carcinogen, topic 19), paracetamol → NAPQI, CCl~4~ → trichloromethyl radical (lipid peroxidation, liver necrosis), methanol → formaldehyde → formic acid (blindness, acidosis), ethylene glycol → oxalate (kidney).
s6724 **Difference from mitochondrial oxidation:** microsomal oxidation does not produce ATP (energy is consumed — NADPH), has low substrate specificity and is inducible.

s6764 5. Detoxification of normal metabolites and hormones
s6765 **Bilirubin** — glucuronidation (topic 25).
s6766 **Steroid hormones:** the cortisol ring is reduced and conjugated with glucuronic acid (17-hydroxycorticosteroids in the urine); androgens — 17-ketosteroids (sulfate and glucuronide conjugates); estrogens — hydroxylation, methylation, glucuronidation (enterohepatic circulation).
s6767 **Catecholamines** — catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) and monoamine oxidase (MAO) → vanillylmandelic acid (VMA).
s6768 **Peptide hormones** (insulin, glucagon, somatotropin) — proteolysis (insulinase); ≈ 50% of insulin is degraded in the liver on first pass.
s6769 **Thyroid hormones** — deiodination, glucuronidation, sulfation. **Clinical importance:** in liver cirrhosis the inactivation of estrogens decreases — gynecomastia in men, “spider angiomas”, palmar erythema; the inactivation of aldosterone decreases — retention of Na^+^ and water (ascites); the breakdown of insulin decreases.
s6770 **Age-related features:** in newborns P450 and UGT activity is low (chloramphenicol — the “gray baby syndrome”, physiological jaundice); in children some P450s are more active; in the elderly liver mass, blood flow and phase I reactions decrease — drug doses must be reduced.
s6771 6. Biotransformation of drugs and the factors that affect it
s6772 **Influencing factors:** age, sex (pregnancy), genetic polymorphism, liver and kidney diseases, nutrition (protein deficiency decreases P450; aromatic hydrocarbons in grilled meat induce CYP1A2), smoking (CYP1A2 induction), alcohol (CYP2E1 induction), other drugs (induction and inhibition), circadian rhythm.
s6773 **The “first-pass” effect:** when an orally administered drug passes through the intestinal wall and the liver, a large part of it is broken down (nitroglycerin, propranolol, morphine) — therefore it is given sublingually or parenterally.
s6774 **Prodrugs** are inactive drugs that are activated in the body: codeine → morphine (CYP2D6), clopidogrel → active thiol metabolite (CYP2C19), enalapril → enalaprilat (esterases), cyclophosphamide (CYP2B6).

s6785 7. P450 polymorphism and personalized treatment
s6786 Because of pharmacogenetic polymorphism, people are divided into **poor**, **intermediate**, **normal** and **ultrarapid** metabolizers of drugs.
s6787 **CYP2D6** — poor metabolizers (≈ 7% in Europe): toxicity of β-blockers and antidepressants, codeine is ineffective (not converted to morphine); in **ultrarapid** metabolizers (many gene copies), codeine is converted to large amounts of morphine and depresses the respiratory center (deaths in children).
s6788 **CYP2C19** — in poor metabolizers **clopidogrel** is not activated (risk of thrombosis); proton pump inhibitors act more strongly.
s6789 **CYP2C9** and **VKORC1** — the dose of **warfarin** is chosen according to the genotype.
s6790 **NAT2** — in slow acetylators, isoniazid polyneuropathy (protection with B~6~), and lupus-like syndrome with hydralazine and procainamide.
s6791 **TPMT** — in its deficiency 6-mercaptopurine and azathioprine cause severe bone marrow suppression — genotyping before treatment is recommended.
s6792 **UGT1A1*28** (Gilbert) — irinotecan toxicity.
s6793 **Pseudocholinesterase** — in the atypical variant suxamethonium causes prolonged apnea.
s6794 **G6PD** — hemolysis with oxidizing drugs (topic 7). **Personalized medicine** — choosing the drug and the dose on the basis of pharmacogenetic tests and preventing adverse effects.
s6795 8. Detoxification of ethanol in the liver
s6796 Figure 26.2
s6797 Pathways of ethanol metabolism and its metabolic consequences (drawn after Lippincott’s Illustrated Reviews: Biochemistry)
s6798 ≈ 90% of ethanol is oxidized in the liver (2–10% is excreted unchanged by the lungs and in the urine); the rate is ≈ 7–10 g/hour (0.1–0.15‰ per hour).
s6799 **Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH)** — cytosol, NAD^+^; the main pathway (also a small amount in the stomach; women have less gastric ADH).
s6800 **The microsomal ethanol-oxidizing system (MEOS) — CYP2E1** — NADPH, O~2~; it is induced 5–10 times by chronic drinking (“tolerance”); it generates free radicals and alters the metabolism of paracetamol and other drugs.
s6801 **Catalase** — in peroxisomes, with H~2~O~2~ (of little importance). **Acetaldehyde** is the most toxic intermediate: adducts with proteins, DNA and phospholipids, damage to microtubules, depletion of glutathione. **Aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH)** oxidizes it to acetate: **ALDH2** — mitochondrial, low K~m~, the main one; ALDH1 — cytosolic.
s6802 The **ALDH2*2** (Glu487Lys) variant occurs in ≈ 40% of the East Asian population: the enzyme is almost inactive, and after alcohol acetaldehyde accumulates and causes facial flushing, tachycardia and nausea (a protective factor, but the risk of esophageal cancer increases). **Disulfiram**, by inhibiting ALDH, causes the same reaction — it is used in the treatment of alcoholism; metronidazole and some cephalosporins also have a disulfiram-like effect.
s6803 **Acetate** is converted to acetyl-CoA in various tissues.
s6804 **The metabolic consequences of alcohol** are mainly due to an **increase in the NADH/NAD^+^ ratio** in the cytosol and mitochondria:
s6805 pyruvate is converted to lactate — **lactic acidosis**; lactate competes with urate secretion in the kidney — **hyperuricemia** (gout attack);
s6806 gluconeogenesis stops (pyruvate and oxaloacetate are reduced) — **hypoglycemia** when drinking on an empty stomach (dangerous, especially in children and diabetic patients);
s6807 β-oxidation and the Krebs cycle slow down, and the synthesis of glycerol-3-phosphate and fatty acids increases — triacylglycerols accumulate: **fatty liver**, hyperlipidemia;
s6808 a lot of acetyl-CoA and little oxaloacetate — **alcoholic ketoacidosis**;
s6809 in chronic drinking: CYP2E1 induction, oxidative stress, activation of stellate cells — **alcoholic hepatitis** and **cirrhosis**; deficiency of thiamine (Wernicke–Korsakoff), folate and B~6~; pancreatitis; in methanol and ethylene glycol poisoning ethanol acts as an **antidote** as a competitive substrate for ADH (fomepizole — an ADH inhibitor). **Markers of chronic alcoholism:** GGT ↑, AST/ALT > 2, MCV ↑ (macrocytosis), **carbohydrate-deficient transferrin** (CDT) ↑, triacylglycerols and uric acid ↑.
s6810 Experiment 1: determination of hippuric acid in urine (assessment of the detoxifying function of the liver — based on the Quick–Pytel test)
s6811 **Principle of the method:** the liver conjugates benzoic acid with glycine and converts it into **hippuric acid**. The amount of hippuric acid excreted in the urine after a sodium benzoate load reflects the conjugating activity of the liver. Hippuric acid crystallizes in an acidic medium.
s6812 The subject drinks 4 g of sodium benzoate dissolved in 30 ml of water on an empty stomach (for teaching purposes a prepared urine sample is used).
s6813 Urine is collected for 4 hours and its volume is measured.
s6814 Add 1 g of NaCl and acetic acid (or HCl) to 20 ml of urine and keep in an ice bath for 30 minutes — hippuric acid precipitates as needle-shaped crystals.
s6815 Filter the crystals, wash with a small amount of cold water, dry and weigh (or titrate with 0.1 M NaOH in the presence of phenolphthalein: 1 ml of 0.1 M NaOH = 17.9 mg of hippuric acid). **Interpretation:** in a healthy person ≈ 65–85% of the administered sodium benzoate (≈ 3–3.5 g calculated as benzoic acid) is excreted as hippuric acid within 4 hours. A decrease is seen with damage to the liver parenchyma (hepatitis, cirrhosis) or with glycine deficiency; kidney function must be preserved.
s6816 Experiment 2: analysis of paracetamol metabolism (case-based)
s6817 Write the metabolic pathways of paracetamol at a therapeutic dose: glucuronidation (≈ 55%), sulfation (≈ 30%), NAPQI via CYP2E1 (≈ 5–10%) → conjugation with glutathione → mercapturic acid.
s6818 Show which pathways become saturated at a toxic dose (> 150 mg/kg) and the fate of NAPQI.
s6819 State the mechanism of action of N-acetylcysteine.
s6820 **Results and analysis:** the conjugation pathways become saturated, a lot of NAPQI is formed and the glutathione store is exhausted (< 30%) — NAPQI binds covalently to the cysteine residues of hepatocyte proteins — centrilobular liver necrosis. N-acetylcysteine supplies cysteine for glutathione synthesis and binds NAPQI directly; it is very effective if given within 8–10 hours. Chronic alcoholism (CYP2E1 induction, decreased glutathione) and fasting increase the risk.

s6892 A 19-year-old girl in a depressed state took 20 tablets of paracetamol (10 g). On the first day she had no complaints other than nausea. On day 3 jaundice and pain under the right costal margin appeared: ALT 6800 U/l, AST 5200 U/l, bilirubin 85 µmol/l, INR 3.8.
s6893 What is the biochemical mechanism of the liver damage? Why did the symptoms appear late? How does the antidote act?
s6894 At a therapeutic dose paracetamol is detoxified mainly by glucuronidation and sulfation; a small part is converted by CYP2E1 into reactive NAPQI, which is immediately bound by glutathione. At a high dose the conjugation pathways become saturated, a lot of NAPQI is formed and the glutathione in hepatocytes is exhausted; NAPQI binds covalently to the cysteine residues of proteins, damages the mitochondria and causes oxidative stress — necrosis of the cells in the center of the liver lobules (where CYP2E1 is abundant). The necrosis and its signs (a sharp rise in ALT, impaired synthesis — INR) develop within 24–72 hours; the early period is almost asymptomatic, which makes it dangerous. The antidote is N-acetylcysteine: a source of cysteine for glutathione synthesis, and it binds NAPQI directly; it is most effective when given early (within 8–10 hours). The patient also needs psychiatric care.
