s1716 — the **Warburg effect**; this underlies the detection of tumors in PET scanning (18F-fluorodeoxyglucose).
s1717 2. Gluconeogenesis
s1718 **Gluconeogenesis** is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate substances. It takes place mainly in the **liver** (≈ 90%) and in the **renal cortex** (up to ≈ 40% in prolonged starvation). Its significance: maintaining blood glucose during starvation, when the glycogen store is exhausted (after 12–24 hours) — the brain (≈ 120 g per day), the erythrocytes and the renal medulla need glucose.
s1719 **Its substrates:** lactate (working muscle, erythrocytes), **glucogenic amino acids** (alanine, glutamine and others — from the breakdown of muscle proteins), **glycerol** (from lipolysis in adipose tissue), propionate (from odd-chain fatty acids). **Fatty acids and acetyl-CoA do not give glucose** (the pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction is irreversible).

s1761 **The Cori cycle** — lactate formed in working muscle and erythrocytes reaches the liver through the blood, is converted into glucose and returns to the muscle. The muscle’s “oxygen debt” is repaid at the expense of the liver’s energy.
s1762 **The glucose-alanine cycle** — in muscle, pyruvate takes the amino group from amino acids and is converted into alanine; in the liver alanine is converted back into pyruvate (gluconeogenesis), while the ammonia goes into urea synthesis. This cycle carries nitrogen from the muscle in a harmless form.
s1763 3. The pentose phosphate pathway
s1764 **The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP)** is an alternative route of glucose-6-phosphate oxidation; it takes place in the cytosol. It does not form ATP; its main products are **NADPH** and **ribose-5-phosphate**.
s1765 Figure 7.3
s1766 The oxidative and non-oxidative stages of the pentose phosphate pathway (drawn after Severin E.S.)
s1767 **The oxidative stage** (irreversible): glucose-6-phosphate → 6-phosphogluconolactone → 6-phosphogluconate → ribulose-5-phosphate + CO2. **2 NADPH** are formed in the two dehydrogenase reactions. The key enzyme is **glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)**; it is activated by NADP+ and inhibited by NADPH; insulin induces it.
s1768 **The non-oxidative stage** (reversible): with the participation of **transketolase** (coenzyme — TDP, B1) and **transaldolase**, pentoses are converted into fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (returning to glycolysis) or, conversely, ribose-5-phosphate is formed from glycolytic intermediates. **The significance of NADPH:** synthesis of fatty acids, cholesterol and steroid hormones (the liver, adipose tissue, mammary gland and adrenal cortex are the organs with the most active PPP); keeping glutathione in the reduced state (protection of erythrocytes from oxidative stress); microsomal oxidation (cytochrome P450); NADPH oxidase in phagocytes (the bactericidal “respiratory burst”).
s1769 **Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency** is the most common enzymopathy (X-linked; frequent in areas where malaria is widespread). Little NADPH is formed in the erythrocytes and glutathione is not reduced; when oxidizing drugs (primaquine, sulfonamides, nitrofurans), infections or **fava beans** are involved, hemoglobin is oxidized and precipitates (Heinz bodies), and an **acute hemolytic crisis** develops.
s1770 4. Fructose and galactose metabolism
s1771 **Fructose** (in fruit, honey and sucrose) is metabolized mainly in the liver:

s1773 Fructose “bypasses” phosphofructokinase-1, so it is broken down rapidly in the liver and its excess is directed to fatty acid synthesis (high fructose intake is a risk factor for fatty degeneration of the liver and hyperlipidemia).

s1819 Experiment 1: determination of blood glucose by the glucose oxidase method
s1820 **Principle of the method:** glucose oxidase oxidizes glucose to gluconolactone and H2O2; peroxidase, at the expense of H2O2, oxidizes a chromogen (4-aminoantipyrine + phenol) and forms a pink compound (the Trinder reaction). The intensity of the color is proportional to the glucose concentration.
s1821 Pour 2 ml of working reagent (glucose oxidase, peroxidase, chromogen) into each of three test tubes.
s1822 Add 20 µl of serum (plasma) to tube 1, 20 µl of standard glucose solution (5.55 mmol/l) to tube 2 and 20 µl of water to tube 3 (control).
s1823 Mix and keep at 37 °C for 15 minutes.
s1824 Measure the optical density against the control at 500–510 nm on a photometer (or a biochemical analyzer). **Calculation:** C (mmol/l) = (Esample / Estandard) × 5.55
s1825 **Evaluation:** in venous plasma the fasting norm is 3.9–6.1 mmol/l; 6.1–6.9 — impaired fasting glycemia; ≥ 7.0 (on two occasions) — diabetes mellitus; < 3.3 — hypoglycemia.
s1826 Experiment 2: determination of glucose in capillary blood with a glucometer
s1827 Wash and dry the hand, wipe the pad of the finger with alcohol and wait until the alcohol evaporates.
s1828 Insert the test strip into the glucometer (check that the code matches).
s1829 Puncture the side of the finger with a lancet and wipe away the first drop.
s1830 Touch the second drop to the application area of the test strip.
s1831 After 5–10 s read and record the result. **Analysis:** the glucose oxidase (or glucose dehydrogenase) immobilized on the test strip oxidizes glucose; the electrons pass to the electrode and an electric current is generated (an electrochemical biosensor). The glucometer is intended for self-monitoring; the diagnosis is made in the laboratory in venous plasma. Causes of error: alcohol left on the finger, the first drop, squeezing out the blood (tissue fluid), an expired test strip, a sharp change in hematocrit.
