s1754 **The Cori cycle** — lactate formed in working muscle and erythrocytes reaches the liver through the blood, is converted into glucose and returns to the muscle. The muscle’s “oxygen debt” is repaid at the expense of the liver’s energy.
s1755 **The glucose-alanine cycle** — in muscle, pyruvate takes the amino group from amino acids and is converted into alanine; in the liver alanine is converted back into pyruvate (gluconeogenesis), while the ammonia goes into urea synthesis. This cycle carries nitrogen from the muscle in a harmless form.
s1756 3. The pentose phosphate pathway
s1757 **The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP)** is an alternative route of glucose-6-phosphate oxidation; it takes place in the cytosol. It does not form ATP; its main products are **NADPH** and **ribose-5-phosphate**.
s1758 Figure 7.3
s1759 The oxidative and non-oxidative stages of the pentose phosphate pathway (drawn after Severin E.S.)
s1760 **The oxidative stage** (irreversible): glucose-6-phosphate → 6-phosphogluconolactone → 6-phosphogluconate → ribulose-5-phosphate + CO2. **2 NADPH** are formed in the two dehydrogenase reactions. The key enzyme is **glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)**; it is activated by NADP+ and inhibited by NADPH; insulin induces it.
s1761 **The non-oxidative stage** (reversible): with the participation of **transketolase** (coenzyme — TDP, B1) and **transaldolase**, pentoses are converted into fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (returning to glycolysis) or, conversely, ribose-5-phosphate is formed from glycolytic intermediates. **The significance of NADPH:** synthesis of fatty acids, cholesterol and steroid hormones (the liver, adipose tissue, mammary gland and adrenal cortex are the organs with the most active PPP); keeping glutathione in the reduced state (protection of erythrocytes from oxidative stress); microsomal oxidation (cytochrome P450); NADPH oxidase in phagocytes (the bactericidal “respiratory burst”).
s1762 **Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency** is the most common enzymopathy (X-linked; frequent in areas where malaria is widespread). Little NADPH is formed in the erythrocytes and glutathione is not reduced; when oxidizing drugs (primaquine, sulfonamides, nitrofurans), infections or **fava beans** are involved, hemoglobin is oxidized and precipitates (Heinz bodies), and an **acute hemolytic crisis** develops.

s1812 Experiment 1: determination of blood glucose by the glucose oxidase method
s1813 **Principle of the method:** glucose oxidase oxidizes glucose to gluconolactone and H2O2; peroxidase, at the expense of H2O2, oxidizes a chromogen (4-aminoantipyrine + phenol) and forms a pink compound (the Trinder reaction). The intensity of the color is proportional to the glucose concentration.
s1814 Pour 2 ml of working reagent (glucose oxidase, peroxidase, chromogen) into each of three test tubes.
s1815 Add 20 µl of serum (plasma) to tube 1, 20 µl of standard glucose solution (5.55 mmol/l) to tube 2 and 20 µl of water to tube 3 (control).
s1816 Mix and keep at 37 °C for 15 minutes.
s1817 Measure the optical density against the control at 500–510 nm on a photometer (or a biochemical analyzer). **Calculation:** C (mmol/l) = (Esample / Estandard) × 5.55
s1818 **Evaluation:** in venous plasma the fasting norm is 3.9–6.1 mmol/l; 6.1–6.9 — impaired fasting glycemia; ≥ 7.0 (on two occasions) — diabetes mellitus; < 3.3 — hypoglycemia.
s1819 Experiment 2: determination of glucose in capillary blood with a glucometer
s1820 Wash and dry the hand, wipe the pad of the finger with alcohol and wait until the alcohol evaporates.
s1821 Insert the test strip into the glucometer (check that the code matches).
s1822 Puncture the side of the finger with a lancet and wipe away the first drop.
s1823 Touch the second drop to the application area of the test strip.
s1824 After 5–10 s read and record the result. **Analysis:** the glucose oxidase (or glucose dehydrogenase) immobilized on the test strip oxidizes glucose; the electrons pass to the electrode and an electric current is generated (an electrochemical biosensor). The glucometer is intended for self-monitoring; the diagnosis is made in the laboratory in venous plasma. Causes of error: alcohol left on the finger, the first drop, squeezing out the blood (tissue fluid), an expired test strip, a sharp change in hematocrit.

s1923 Two days after taking a sulfonamide drug, a 30-year-old man developed weakness, jaundice and dark urine. Blood hemoglobin is 85 g/l, indirect bilirubin is increased, and Heinz bodies were found in the erythrocytes. He has had the same condition before after eating a bean dish.
s1924 Which enzyme deficiency is suspected? Explain the mechanism of the hemolysis.
s1925 Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency. In erythrocytes NADPH is formed only in the pentose phosphate pathway; through glutathione reductase it keeps glutathione in the reduced state. An oxidizing drug (sulfonamide) or the substances of beans generate H2O2 and free radicals; because glutathione is not regenerated, hemoglobin and membrane proteins are oxidized, hemoglobin precipitates and forms Heinz bodies, and the erythrocytes are destroyed — acute hemolytic anemia, a rise in indirect bilirubin (jaundice) and hemoglobinuria.
