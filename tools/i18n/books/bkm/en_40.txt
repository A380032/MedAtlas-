s7539 Experiment 2: estimation of GFR from creatinine (calculation)
s7540 Determine creatinine in blood (the Jaffe reaction — an orange color with picric acid in an alkaline medium, or an enzymatic method) and creatinine and diuresis in daily urine.
s7541 Calculate the clearance: C = (U × V) / P, V — ml/min.
s7542 The Cockcroft–Gault formula: CrCl (ml/min) = [(140 − age) × weight (kg)] / [72 × creatinine (mg/dl)]; × 0.85 for women. (If creatinine is in µmol/l: mg/dl = µmol/l / 88.4.) **Results and analysis:** GFR ≥ 90 — normal; 60–89 — mildly decreased; < 60 ml/min/1.73 m^2^ for more than 3 months — chronic kidney disease; < 15 — kidney failure (dialysis). Drug doses must be adjusted to the GFR.

s7635 A 58-year-old patient has had type 2 diabetes mellitus for 15 years. Edema of the face and legs. Blood glucose 11.2 mmol/l, HbA~1c~ 9.1%, creatinine 210 µmol/l, albumin 28 g/l, potassium 5.6 mmol/l, Hb 98 g/l. Urine protein 4.2 g/day, glucose positive.
s7636 What complication has developed? What is the biochemical mechanism of the kidney damage? Explain the cause of the anemia and hyperkalemia.
s7637 Diabetic nephropathy with nephrotic-range proteinuria and chronic kidney disease. Prolonged hyperglycemia nonenzymatically glycates the proteins of the glomerular basement membrane (advanced glycation end products), the mesangial matrix increases via TGF-β, and negatively charged heparan sulfate decreases; hyperfiltration and hypertension damage the glomeruli — first microalbuminuria, then heavy proteinuria (hypoalbuminemia, edema), then GFR decreases (creatinine increased). Anemia is due to decreased renal production of erythropoietin; K^+^ secretion is impaired because of the decreased GFR and the hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism that is common in diabetes — hyperkalemia. Treatment: control of glycemia and BP, ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, SGLT2 inhibitors (depending on GFR).
s7638 A 40-year-old man presented with sudden severe colicky pain radiating from the loin to the groin. The urine contains many erythrocytes, pH 5.0, uric acid crystals. Blood uric acid 520 µmol/l. No stone was visible on X-ray; CT showed an 8 mm stone in the renal pelvis.
s7639 What type of stone is this? Why is it not visible on X-ray? What are the mechanism of stone formation and its treatment?
s7640 A urate (uric acid) stone. Urate stones absorb little X-ray radiation (radiolucent) and are visible on CT. Hyperuricemia and high urate excretion together with persistently acidic urine (at pH < 5.5 the solubility of uric acid is low, as it is un-ionized) lead to crystallization; in the metabolic syndrome ammoniagenesis is reduced and the urine is more acidic. Treatment: plenty of fluids, alkalinization of the urine with potassium citrate to pH 6.5–7 (urate stones dissolve — unlike other stones), allopurinol, restriction of purines and alcohol; pain relief.
s7641 A 6-year-old child has fever, vomiting and abdominal pain. The urine smells of acetone. On the test strip ketone bodies are +++, glucose negative. Blood glucose 3.8 mmol/l. The child has eaten almost nothing for 2 days.
s7642 What is the cause of the ketonuria? Why is there no glucosuria? How is it distinguished from diabetes mellitus?
s7643 An acetonemic state (ketosis) against the background of fasting and vomiting. Children have a small glycogen store that is quickly exhausted; insulin decreases and glucagon and cortisol increase — lipolysis is enhanced, and β-oxidation of fatty acids in the liver forms a lot of acetyl-CoA, which, because oxaloacetate is used for gluconeogenesis, is converted into ketone bodies; acetoacetate and acetone are detected in the urine. Blood glucose is normal or low — because it does not exceed the renal threshold, there is no glucosuria. In diabetes mellitus, by contrast, ketonuria is accompanied by hyperglycemia (> 11 mmol/l) and glucosuria. Treatment: drinking and glucose-containing fluids (also intravenously), antiemetic measures — glucose restores insulin secretion and stops ketogenesis.

s7675 1. Specific features and structural components of the nervous system
s7676 The **central nervous system** is the brain and spinal cord; the **peripheral** one consists of nerves, ganglia and nerve endings. Cellular composition: **neurons** (≈ 86 billion) and **neuroglia** — astrocytes, oligodendrocytes (myelin in the CNS), Schwann cells (myelin in the periphery), microglia (immune defense), ependyma.
s7677 **The blood–brain barrier (BBB)** — **tight junctions** between capillary endothelial cells (claudin, occludin), basement membrane, pericytes and astrocyte end-feet. Glucose (GLUT1), amino acids and ketone bodies (MCT1) cross via specific transporters; small lipophilic substances by diffusion (ethanol, anesthetics, nicotine); proteins, most drugs and conjugated bilirubin do not cross. **P-glycoprotein** pumps many drugs back out. In newborns the BBB is immature (risk of kernicterus). Dopamine does not cross the BBB — in Parkinson disease its precursor **L-DOPA** is given.
s7678 **Cerebrospinal fluid** — ≈ 500 ml is formed per day in the choroid plexuses; protein 0.15–0.45 g/l (≈ 200 times less than in plasma), glucose 2.2–3.9 mmol/l (≈ 60% of blood glucose), chlorides 120–130 mmol/l.
s7679 2. Chemical composition of nervous tissue
s7680 The brain contains ≈ 78% water (≈ 84% in gray matter, ≈ 70% in white matter); half of the dry residue is **lipids** (≈ 55% in white matter), and the rest is proteins.
s7681 **Lipids:** phospholipids (phosphatidylethanolamine, plasmalogens, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylcholine), **sphingolipids** — sphingomyelin, **cerebrosides** (galactosylceramide — specific to myelin), sulfatides, **gangliosides** (contain sialic acid; in gray matter, in neuronal membranes), **cholesterol** (in free form; the brain contains ≈ 25% of the body's cholesterol, synthesized locally). The brain contains **almost no triacylglycerols** — fatty acids are not an energy source. Membranes are rich in ω-3 **docosahexaenoic acid** (retina, synapses).
s7682 **Myelin** is the multilayered wrapping of the membrane of an oligodendrocyte or Schwann cell around an axon; ≈ 70–80% lipid (cholesterol, cerebrosides, plasmalogens), ≈ 20–30% protein (**myelin basic protein**, proteolipid protein); electrical insulation — saltatory conduction (speeds up to 100 m/s). **Demyelination:** multiple sclerosis (autoimmune response to myelin proteins), Guillain–Barré syndrome, B~12~ deficiency (spinal cord), leukodystrophies; **sphingolipidoses** (defects of lysosomal enzymes): Tay–Sachs (hexosaminidase A — GM2 ganglioside), Gaucher (glucocerebrosidase), Niemann–Pick (sphingomyelinase), Krabbe (galactocerebrosidase).
s7683 **Proteins:** neurospecific proteins — **S100** (astrocytes; a marker of brain injury), neuron-specific enolase, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), tubulin and **tau protein** (microtubules), neurofilaments, synaptic proteins (synapsins, SNARE), ion channels and pumps, receptors, neuropeptides. Axonal transport (kinesin — anterograde, dynein — retrograde) requires ATP.
s7684 3. Specific features of energy metabolism in nervous tissue
s7685 The brain makes up ≈ 2% of body weight but at rest consumes **≈ 20% of the oxygen** and ≈ 25% of the glucose (≈ 120 g per day).
s7686 ≈ 50–60% of the energy is spent on the work of **Na^+^,K^+^-ATPase** — to restore the membrane potential; the rest goes to the synthesis and reuptake of transmitters and to axonal transport.
s7687 The main substrate is **glucose** (GLUT1 — BBB, GLUT3 — neurons; insulin-independent), which is completely oxidized aerobically. **The glycogen store is very small** (in astrocytes), and there is no oxygen store: if the circulation stops, consciousness is lost in 10 seconds and irreversible damage occurs in 4–5 minutes.
s7688 **In prolonged starvation** the brain obtains ≈ 2/3 of its energy from **ketone bodies** (topic 15); in newborns (breast milk fats) ketone bodies are also an important source.
