s2546 While catching the ball with his arm raised high, a basketball player collided with an opponent; there is sharp pain in the shoulder, the shoulder contour has become "angular" (a hollow below the acromion), he cannot move the arm, and sensation over the lateral surface of the shoulder is reduced.
s2547 What is the injury? Why this particular joint and this direction? Which nerve is injured?
s2548 This is an **anteroinferior dislocation of the shoulder joint**. The shoulder joint is ball-and-socket, with a small, shallow glenoid cavity (3–4 times smaller than the head) and a wide, loose capsule — the most mobile but the least stable joint. The **anteroinferior part** of the capsule is not covered by muscle tendons and is the weakest spot; with the arm abducted and laterally rotated, a blow drives the head out in this direction. Numbness over the lateral surface of the shoulder indicates injury to the **axillary nerve** (which passes below the head, around the surgical neck).
s2549 During a game, a soccer player received a blow to the lateral side of the knee while the knee was flexed and the leg rotated outward. The knee is swollen and "gives way" when walking; on examination the "anterior drawer" sign is positive, and there is pain on the medial side of the joint.
s2550 Which structures are injured? What is this injury called, and how is the diagnosis confirmed?
s2551 This is the **"unhappy triad"**: the **anterior cruciate ligament** (anterior drawer sign — the tibia shifts forward), the **tibial (medial) collateral ligament** and the **medial meniscus** attached to it. With a lateral blow the knee bends inward (valgus) — the medial collateral ligament is stretched and torn, while the rotation leads to tears of the anterior cruciate ligament and the meniscus. The menisci and ligaments are not visible on radiographs — the diagnosis is confirmed by **MRI** or arthroscopy.
s2552 A mother sharply pulled her 2-year-old child by the hand so that the child would not fall on the street. The child is crying, holding the arm hanging at the side with the forearm in pronation, and does not use the elbow; there is no swelling.
s2553 What has happened? Explain the anatomical basis and how help is given.
s2554 This is a **subluxation of the radial head** ("nursemaid's elbow"). In small children the radial head is small and hardly wider than the neck, while the encircling **anular ligament** is wide and loose; when the arm is pulled, the head partly slips out of the ligament and the ligament becomes caught in the joint. Help: **supinate** the forearm while **flexing** the elbow at the same time — a click is felt as the head returns to its place, and the child soon begins to use the arm.

s2583 The **strength** of a muscle depends on its physiological cross-section (pennate muscles are strong), its **range of motion** on the length of its fibers (long parallel-fibered muscles), and its **effective force** on the arms of the lever. At most joints muscles work as speed levers: they lose force but gain speed and range of movement.
s2584 Rule of innervation: the **superficial** muscles of the back (those going to the upper limb and ribs) are supplied by the **anterior (ventral)** rami of the spinal nerves (brachial plexus, intercostal nerves) or by the **accessory nerve** (trapezius), whereas the **deep — intrinsic** muscles of the back are supplied by the **posterior (dorsal)** rami of the spinal nerves.
s2585 When the accessory nerve is injured (for example, during an operation in the lateral triangle of the neck), **paralysis of the trapezius** develops: the shoulder droops and raising the arm above the horizontal becomes difficult. When the dorsal scapular nerve is injured, the rhomboid muscles are paralyzed and the medial border of the scapula stands away from the thoracic cage. Because the **latissimus dorsi** is wide and has many blood vessels, it is used in plastic surgery to close tissue defects (musculocutaneous flap) and in operations supporting the heart (cardiomyoplasty).
s2586 One of the most common causes of **low back pain** is strain and spasm of the erector spinae and multifidus muscles (improper lifting, prolonged sitting). Loads should be lifted not by bending over but **with the knees bent and the back straight** — then the load falls mainly on the leg muscles, and the discs and back muscles are not overstrained. Exercises that strengthen the multifidus muscles are used in the treatment of chronic low back pain.
