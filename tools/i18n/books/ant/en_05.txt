s0628 A patient injured in a car accident has pain on the left lateral surface of the chest that increases on inspiration. The X-ray shows fractures of the bodies of ribs VII–IX along the midaxillary line.
s0629 Which structures may be injured in a rib fracture? How is an intercostal block performed to relieve the pain?
s0630 The **intercostal vein, artery and nerve** lying in the costal groove may be injured, as well as the intercostal muscles, parietal pleura and lung (pneumothorax, hemothorax). In the block, the anesthetic is injected at the **lower border of the rib** in the region of the angle of the rib or the midaxillary line — the nerve lies just below the groove. In pleural puncture, by contrast, the needle is inserted along the upper border of the lower rib.
s0631 The physician asked a student to find the patient's apex beat. The student began counting the ribs below the clavicle but could not palpate the I rib.
s0632 From which landmark should rib counting begin? Why?
s0633 The I rib is hidden behind the clavicle and cannot be palpated. Counting starts from the **sternal angle** (the transverse ridge at the junction of the manubrium and body): the **II rib** lies beside it, and below it the II intercostal space. Counting in this way down to the V intercostal space, the apex beat is found 1–1.5 cm medial to the left midclavicular line.
s0634 Parents are worried about bead-like thickenings along the anterior ends of the ribs on the chest of their 1-year-old child. The child went out little during the winter, and the sternum protrudes slightly forward.
s0635 What are these changes and what causes them? In which part of the rib are they located?
s0636 These are signs of **rickets**: the **“rachitic rosary”** — widening of the growth cartilage at the **junction of the bony and cartilaginous parts** of the ribs — and forward protrusion of the sternum (“pigeon chest”). The cause is impaired mineralization of bone tissue due to **vitamin D** deficiency (lack of sunlight). Because children's bones contain few mineral salts and grow intensively, the changes develop quickly.

s0665 Growth of the upper limb bones **“runs away from the elbow”**: in the humerus the more active growth cartilage is at the top (near the shoulder joint), and in the radius and ulna — at the bottom (near the wrist joint). Therefore, in children injuries of the proximal metaphysis of the humerus and the distal metaphysis of the radius most often lead to growth disturbances.
s0666 **Fracture of the clavicle** is one of the most common fractures: from a fall on the outstretched hand or the shoulder, usually at the junction of the middle and lateral thirds (where the bone is thinnest and curves). The medial fragment is pulled **upward** by the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and the lateral fragment is displaced **downward** by the weight of the arm. Because the subclavian vessels and the brachial plexus lie beneath the clavicle, they may be injured. In newborns, clavicle fracture is **the most common birth injury**.
s0667 In adolescents the ossification center of the acromion appears on an X-ray as a separate bone fragment — do not mistake it for a fracture. Sometimes the acromion never fuses with the scapula (*os acromiale*); this is a normal variant but may cause shoulder pain.
s0668 Remember the nerves along the humerus from above downward in the order **“A–R–U”**: the **A**xillary nerve — at the surgical neck, the **R**adial nerve — in the middle of the shaft (in the spiral groove), the **U**lnar nerve — behind the medial epicondyle.
s0669 Rule for distinguishing the two forearm bones: **the ulna is thick at the top** (it mainly forms the elbow joint), **the radius is thick at the bottom** (it mainly forms the wrist joint). Both bones have a head, but at opposite ends: the head of the radius is **at the top**, the head of the ulna is **at the bottom**.
s0670 **Fracture of the radius “at the typical site”** (Colles' fracture) occurs 2–3 cm above the distal end, from a fall on the outstretched palm; in this region the compact substance is thin and the spongy substance is sparse, i.e., the resistance of the bone is reduced. The distal fragment is displaced backward and laterally — the forearm takes on a “dinner fork” shape. It is especially common in elderly women because of osteoporosis. In children, **epiphysiolysis** (separation along the growth cartilage) occurs in this region.
s0671 Remember the carpal bones from lateral (thumb side) to medial, first the proximal and then the distal row: **“Some Lovers Try Positions That They Can't Handle”** — **S**caphoid, **L**unate, **T**riquetrum, **P**isiform; **T**rapezium, **T**rapezoid, **C**apitate, **H**amate.
s0672 **Fracture of the scaphoid** is the most common carpal fracture (from a fall on the outstretched palm). Pain is felt in the **“anatomical snuffbox”** — the depression formed at the base of the thumb when it is extended. Because the bone receives its blood supply mainly from its distal part, after a fracture the proximal fragment may be **left without blood and undergo necrosis**. The fracture may not be visible on the initial X-ray — so if it is suspected, the hand is immobilized and imaging is repeated after 10–14 days.
s0673 The carpal bones ossify **starting with the capitate and proceeding counterclockwise** (looking at the left hand from the palm): capitate → hamate → triquetrum → lunate → scaphoid → trapezium → trapezoid, and last — the pisiform. A rough rule: **after 1 year, the child's age ≈ the number of visible carpal bones** (for example, 3 at 3 years, 4 at 4 years).
s0674 In an injury of the elbow region, disruption of **Hueter's triangle** (a change in the position of the three points) indicates a fracture or dislocation. Because children have many ossification centers in this region, always evaluate the X-ray **in comparison with the healthy side**.
s0675 1. The upper limb skeleton: general information
s0676 The upper limb skeleton consists of two divisions: the **shoulder girdle** (*cingulum membri superioris*) — the clavicle and scapula, and the **free part of the upper limb** (*pars libera membri superioris*). The free part is in turn divided into three: proximal — the **humerus**; middle — the **forearm bones** (radius and ulna); distal — the **bones of the hand** (carpal bones, metacarpal bones and phalanges). In humans the upper limb has been freed from its supporting function and has become an organ of labor: the shoulder girdle is mobile, the joints have a wide range of motion, and the thumb can be opposed to the other fingers.
s0677 All upper limb bones except the clavicle pass through three stages of development (membrane → cartilage → bone): their diaphyses ossify in fetal life, and their epiphyses and apophyses after birth. In the newborn the upper limb is short relative to the trunk and long relative to the lower limb; the arm is short, and the forearm and especially the hand are long. By puberty the arm lengthens ≈4 times, the forearm 3–5 times and the hand ≈3 times; at ≈20 years the proportions of the upper limb resemble those of the adult. The humerus grows in length mainly at its **proximal** epiphysis, and the forearm bones at their **distal** epiphysis.
s0678 2. The clavicle
s0679 The **clavicle** (*clavicula*) is a short tubular bone curved in an S shape; it lies horizontally between the clavicular notch of the sternum and the acromion of the scapula and acts as a “strut” holding the upper limb away from the trunk. It has a **body** (*corpus claviculae*) and two ends. The medial **sternal end** (*extremitas sternalis*) is thick and triangular and joins the sternum by its **sternal articular surface** (*facies articularis sternalis*). The lateral **acromial end** (*extremitas acromialis*) is flat and wide and bears the **acromial articular surface** (*facies articularis acromialis*), which joins the acromion.
s0680 The medial 2/3 of the bone is convex forward, and the lateral 1/3 is concave forward. Its upper surface is smooth and palpable under the skin along its entire length. On the lower surface are: medially, the **impression for the costoclavicular ligament** (*impressio ligamenti costoclavicularis*); in the middle, the **groove for the subclavius muscle** (*sulcus musculi subclavii*); laterally, the **conoid tubercle** (*tuberculum conoideum*) and the **trapezoid line** (*linea trapezoidea*) — the attachment sites of the parts of the coracoclavicular ligament.

s0683 **Age-related features.** The clavicle is **the first bone in the body to ossify** (in the 6th week of fetal life) and the only upper limb bone that develops by the **endesmal** route (without a cartilaginous stage). In the newborn it is S-shaped and its sternal end is cartilaginous. In the first 7 years of life the clavicle grows in length and width. A secondary ossification center appears in the sternal end at 16–18 years and fuses with the body at **20–25 years** — one of the last growth zones in the skeleton to close.
s0684 3. The scapula
s0685 The **scapula** (*scapula*) is a triangular flat bone; it lies on the posterolateral surface of the thoracic cage at the level of ribs II–VII and is attached to the trunk by muscles. It has three **borders** — **medial** (*margo medialis*, facing the vertebral column), **lateral** (*margo lateralis*, facing the axilla) and **superior** (*margo superior*) — and three **angles** — **superior** (*angulus superior*), **inferior** (*angulus inferior*) and the thickened **lateral** (*angulus lateralis*).
s0686 The **anterior — costal surface** (*facies costalis*) is concave and forms the **subscapular fossa** (*fossa subscapularis*). The **posterior surface** (*facies posterior*) is convex; it is divided in two by the transversely running **spine of the scapula** (*spina scapulae*): above — the **supraspinous fossa** (*fossa supraspinata*), below — the larger **infraspinous fossa** (*fossa infraspinata*); each contains the muscle of the same name. Laterally the spine widens and ends in the **acromion** (*acromion*), which overhangs the shoulder joint — the highest point of the shoulder.
s0687 At the lateral angle lies the shallow oval **glenoid cavity** (*cavitas glenoidalis*), which articulates with the head of the humerus; above it is the **supraglenoid tubercle** (*tuberculum supraglenoidale* — the origin of the long head of the biceps), and below it the **infraglenoid tubercle** (*tuberculum infraglenoidale* — the origin of the long head of the triceps). The cavity is followed by the narrowed **neck of the scapula** (*collum scapulae*). On the superior border is the **scapular notch** (*incisura scapulae*), and lateral to it the forward-curving **coracoid process** (*processus coracoideus*).
