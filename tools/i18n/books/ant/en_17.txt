s2037 A soccer player was hit in the eye by a ball. The area around the eye is swollen, the patient cannot look upward and sees double; there is numbness of the cheek and upper lip on the same side. CT showed orbital tissue herniating into the cavity below.
s2038 Which wall of the orbit is fractured? How do you explain the numbness and the disturbed eye movement?
s2039 This is a **"blow-out" fracture of the orbital floor**. With the blow, pressure inside the orbit rose and one of the thinnest walls — the **inferior wall** (orbital surface of the maxilla) — broke, and tissue herniated into the **maxillary sinus**. Because the **inferior rectus muscle** is trapped in the fracture line, the eye does not move upward (double vision). The infraorbital nerve, lying in the **infraorbital groove and canal** of the inferior wall, is injured — it supplies the skin of the cheek, the side of the nose and the upper lip.
s2040 After a fall onto the back of the head, a patient complains of inability to swallow, a hoarse voice, and deviation of the tongue to one side when it is protruded. There is bruising of the skin of the occiput and neck.
s2041 Which fossa of the cranial base is injured? Which foramina and nerves are damaged?
s2042 This is a fracture of the **posterior cranial fossa**. The disturbed swallowing and hoarse voice result from injury to the **glossopharyngeal (IX) and vagus (X)** nerves passing through the **jugular foramen**; deviation of the tongue to one side results from injury to **CN XII**, passing through the **hypoglossal canal** (the tongue deviates toward the injured side). Fractures of the posterior fossa are the most dangerous because of proximity to the medulla oblongata.
s2043 A patient with chronic rhinitis has pain in the forehead and cheek regions and purulent nasal discharge; the pain increases when bending the head forward. Radiographs show opacification of the frontal and maxillary sinuses.
s2044 Why are these two sinuses inflamed together? Where do they open?
s2045 The frontal and maxillary sinuses (as well as the anterior and middle ethmoidal cells) open into the **middle nasal meatus**. In rhinitis the mucosa of this meatus swells and blocks the outflow openings of several sinuses at once — secretions accumulate, and **frontal and maxillary sinusitis** develop. Because the outflow opening of the maxillary sinus is in its upper part, pus does not drain well.

s2074 The types of movement depend on the axes: around the **frontal axis** — flexion (*flexio*) and extension (*extensio*); around the **sagittal axis** — abduction (*abductio*) and adduction (*adductio*); around the **vertical axis** — medial and lateral rotation (*rotatio*); in the forearm bones — pronation and supination. In joints with two or more axes there is also **circular movement** (*circumductio*) — a movement describing a cone.
s2075 With age the disc loses water and fissures appear in the anulus fibrosus — **osteochondrosis**. When lifting a heavy load, the nucleus pulposus may protrude through a tear in the anulus — **disc herniation**. Because the posterior longitudinal ligament is thick in the midline and thin at the sides, the herniation usually protrudes **posterolaterally** and compresses the **spinal nerve root** next to the intervertebral foramen (low back pain, pain radiating to the leg — radiculopathy). It occurs most often at the L~IV~–L~V~ and L~V~–S~I~ discs.
s2076 The transverse ligament of the atlas separates the dens from the spinal cord. If this ligament becomes lax or ruptures in rheumatoid arthritis, Down syndrome or injury, the dens may shift backward and **compress the upper part of the spinal cord** — this is life-threatening (respiratory center and segments of the phrenic nerve). Therefore, in children with Down syndrome, sports involving sharp flexion of the neck are restricted.
s2077 Pathological curvatures of the vertebral column: **scoliosis** — lateral curvature in the frontal plane (often at school age because of poor sitting posture, one-sided loads or uneven growth); **hyperkyphosis** ("hunchback") and **hyperlordosis**. For early detection the child is examined while bending forward (Adams forward bend test) — a rib hump on one side indicates scoliosis.
s2078 When the mouth is opened excessively wide (yawning, vomiting, dental procedures), the head of the mandible may slide forward over the articular tubercle and remain in the infratemporal fossa — **dislocation of the mandible**: the mouth is locked open and the patient cannot close it. To reduce it, the physician places the thumbs on the patient's lower molars and presses the jaw first **downward**, then **backward**. In the newborn the articular tubercle is not apparent and the fossa is flat — so the forward-backward gliding movements needed for suckling are free.
s2079 1. Types of bone joints
s2080 **Arthrology** (Greek *arthron* — joint) is the study of the joints of bones. By the way they are connected, bone joints are divided into three groups: **continuous joints** (*synarthroses*) — there is no cavity between the bones, which are joined solidly by tissue; **symphyses (hemiarthroses)** (*hemiarthroses, symphyses*) — there is a small cleft within the cartilage; **discontinuous joints — synovial joints** (*diarthroses, articulationes synoviales*) — there is a joint cavity between the bones.

s2095 2. Structure and classification of joints
s2096 Every joint has three **main elements**: 1) **articular surfaces** (*facies articulares*) — covered by **articular cartilage** (*cartilago articularis*, usually hyaline); 2) the **joint capsule** (*capsula articularis*) — consisting of a strong outer **fibrous membrane** and an inner **synovial membrane**; the synovial membrane covers all the structures inside the joint except the articular cartilage and is rich in blood vessels and nerves; 3) the **joint cavity** (*cavitas articularis*) — a hermetically closed slit-like space filled with a small amount of **synovial fluid**; the fluid moistens the articular surfaces, reduces friction and nourishes the cartilage.

s2098 Diagram of the structure of a joint: 1 — synovial membrane; 2 — joint space; 3 — joint cavity; 4 — bone; 5 — articular cartilage; 6 — periosteum. Source: A. Ahmedov et al. Anatomy, vol. I, 2018.
s2099 **Accessory elements**: **ligaments** (extracapsular, intracapsular and capsular); **articular discs** (temporomandibular and sternoclavicular joints) and **menisci** (knee joint) — they make the articular surfaces congruent and absorb shock; the **articular labrum** (*labrum articulare* — deepens the articular fossa in the shoulder and hip joints); **synovial folds** and **synovial bursae** (*bursae synoviales*).

s2124 3. Joints of the vertebral column
s2125 The vertebrae are joined by their **bodies**, **arches** and **processes**. Between the bodies of adjacent vertebrae lies the **intervertebral disc** (*discus intervertebralis*) of fibrocartilage; there are 23 discs in all (there is no disc between the atlas and axis). At the periphery of the disc is the strong **anulus fibrosus** (*anulus fibrosus*) of concentric layers, and in the center the elastic, water-rich **nucleus pulposus** (*nucleus pulposus*); the nucleus acts as a shock absorber. The discs are ≈5–6 mm high in the cervical region and ≈10–12 mm in the lumbar region; they make up ≈1/4 of the length of the vertebral column.
