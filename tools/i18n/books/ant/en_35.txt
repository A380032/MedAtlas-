s4396 The **cecum** (*caecum*) is the initial, widest (7–7.5 cm) and shortest (6–8 cm) part of the large intestine; it lies in the right iliac fossa below the entry of the ileum; it is covered by peritoneum on all sides but has no mesentery. The ileum opens into the cecum through the **ileal orifice**; around the orifice is the **ileocecal valve (Bauhin's valve)**, consisting of upper and lower lips — it prevents the contents of the large intestine from returning into the small intestine. From the posteromedial wall of the cecum, where the teniae converge, arises the **vermiform appendix** (*appendix vermiformis*): 2–20 cm long (on average 8–9 cm), 0.5–1 cm in diameter, with a mesentery (*mesoappendix*); its mucosa contains a great deal of lymphoid tissue (the "intestinal tonsil"). Its position varies: descending (into the pelvis), lateral, medial, anterior and behind the cecum (retrocecal).

s4399 The **ascending colon** (15–20 cm) rises in the right lateral region of the abdomen and below the liver forms the **right (hepatic) flexure**; the **transverse colon** (on average 50 cm) is mobile owing to its long mesentery, forms a downward-facing arch and, beside the spleen, passes by the **left (splenic) flexure** into the **descending colon** (10–15 cm); this continues in the left iliac fossa as the **sigmoid colon** (15–67 cm, with a mesentery, mobile), which passes into the rectum at the level of the 3rd sacral vertebra. The ascending and descending colon are **mesoperitoneal** (the posterior wall lacks peritoneum), the transverse and sigmoid colon **intraperitoneal**.
s4400 The **rectum** (*rectum*) is the last part of the alimentary canal, **14–18 cm** long; it begins in the lesser pelvis at the 3rd sacral vertebra and ends in the perineum with the **anus**. In the sagittal plane it forms two curves: the **sacral flexure** (convex backward) and the **perineal flexure** (convex forward). Its parts: the wide **ampulla** (pelvic part) and the narrow **anal canal** (perineal part, ≈4 cm). There are no teniae, haustra or omental appendices — the longitudinal muscle layer is continuous. The ampulla has 2–3 **transverse folds** (the largest on the right, ≈6–7 cm above the anus — Kohlrausch's fold), and the anal canal has 6–10 **anal columns** with **anal sinuses** between them; their lower boundary is the **anorectal (pectinate) line**. The circular layer forms the **internal (involuntary) sphincter**; the **external (voluntary) sphincter** is striated and belongs to the perineal muscles. The upper part of the rectum is covered by peritoneum on all sides, the middle part on three sides, and the lower part lies outside the peritoneum.

s4403 6. Development
s4404 In the 4th week of fetal life the primitive gut tube extends from the stomach to the cloaca and has dorsal and ventral mesenteries. The stomach appears as a fusiform dilation and in the **2nd month** rotates **90°** around its longitudinal axis (the left surface faces forward, the right backward), then rotates around an anteroposterior axis to take an oblique position; this is why the left vagus nerve comes to lie on the anterior wall and the right on the posterior wall. In the **5th week** the midgut lengthens rapidly, forming the **intestinal loop** toward the umbilicus; its apex is connected to the yolk sac by the **vitelline duct**. In weeks 6–10 the loop passes into the umbilical cord (**physiological umbilical herniation**), rotates **270°** (counterclockwise) around the axis of the superior mesenteric artery and returns to the abdominal cavity: the cecum lies first below the liver and then descends into the right iliac fossa. Failure of the cecum to descend or a disturbance of rotation causes intestinal obstruction and an unusual position of the appendix.
s4405 7. Age-related features
s4406 In the newborn the **esophagus** is 10–12 cm long, funnel-shaped, with a lumen of 4–9 mm; its constrictions are not apparent; it begins at the level of the 3rd–4th cervical vertebrae (4th–5th at 2 years, 6th–7th at 15 years). The mucosa is delicate, the glands poorly developed and the muscle layer thin; longitudinal folds appear at 2–3 years; it grows rapidly up to 12–15 years. The cardiac sphincter is poorly developed in the newborn — which is why **regurgitation** is common in infants.
s4407 In the newborn the **stomach** lies vertically to the left of the vertebral column; the fundus, cardiac and pyloric parts are poorly defined; it is ≈5 cm long with a capacity of **≈30–50 mL**, ≈80 mL at 10 days, **250–300 mL** at 1 year and 1300–1500 mL at 10–12 years. At 5 months it becomes horizontal; the pyloric part becomes apparent at 4–6 months and the fundus at 10 months; the cardiac part forms by 8 years.
s4408 The mucosa of the newborn's stomach is relatively thick with few folds; there are ≈200 thousand gastric pits (≈4 million at 15 years) and ≈0.5 million glands (35 million in adults). Because the glands are immature, little hydrochloric acid and pepsin are secreted. The muscle layer, especially the oblique fibers, is poorly developed; the pyloric sphincter is stronger than the cardiac sphincter.
s4409 The **small intestine** is relatively long in the newborn (1.5–3 m): the ratio of intestinal length to body length is **8.3:1** in the newborn, 6:1 at 1 year and 5.4:1 in adults. Periods of rapid growth are 1–3 and 10–15 years. The mucosa is thin and rich in blood vessels, with incompletely developed folds, villi and glands; its permeability is high (antibodies, but also toxins, are easily absorbed). The duodenum is ring-shaped, 7.5–10 cm long, at the level of L~I~; by 12 years its position resembles that of adults.
s4410 The **large intestine** is ≈63 cm long in the newborn (83 cm at 1 year, 118 cm at 10 years); the teniae are poorly developed, and haustra (appearing at 6 months) and omental appendices (at 2 years) are absent; they are fully formed by 6–7 years.
s4411 In the newborn the **cecum** lies above the iliac wing (it descends into the right iliac fossa by 14 years); the appendix is ≈4–5 cm long and opens by a wide funnel-shaped orifice (hence appendicitis is rare in infants), and its lymphoid tissue is greatest in childhood. The ileocecal valve is poorly developed, and the orifice is round until 1 year.
s4412 The **sigmoid colon** is relatively long in the newborn (≈20 cm), with a long mesentery, and lies in the upper part of the abdominal cavity (hence constipation and volvulus are more common in children); at 7 years it descends into the lesser pelvis. The **rectum** in the newborn is cylindrical, 5–6 cm long, with no ampulla or flexures; the ampulla forms in early childhood and the flexures after 8 years.

s4511 A 19-year-old man presented with pain that began around the umbilicus and after 6 hours shifted to the lower right abdomen, with nausea and a temperature of 37.8 °C. Pressure at the lateral third of the line joining the umbilicus and the right anterior superior iliac spine causes sharp pain.
s4512 What disease should be suspected? Why was the pain at first around the umbilicus? Name the positional variants of this organ.
s4513 **Acute appendicitis**. The point of tenderness is **McBurney's point**, the projection of the base of the appendix. The initial pain is **visceral** — because the appendix is a midgut derivative, its visceral afferents go to segment T~X~ (the umbilical dermatome); once the inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum, the pain becomes **somatic** and is clearly localized in the right iliac region. The appendix arises from the posteromedial wall of the cecum where the teniae converge and may be directed downward (into the pelvis), laterally, medially, anteriorly or behind the cecum (retrocecal) — this changes the clinical picture.
s4514 A 3-week-old boy has begun "projectile" vomiting after every feed; the vomit contains no bile, and he is losing weight. After feeding, wave-like peristalsis is seen moving from left to right in the epigastric region, and a firm olive-sized mass is palpable below the right costal margin.
s4515 Which part of which organ is affected? Why is there no bile in the vomit?
s4516 This is **pyloric stenosis** — congenital hypertrophy of the circular muscle layer (pyloric sphincter) in the **pyloric part** of the stomach; the "olive" is the thickened pylorus. Because the obstruction is **at the level of the pylorus**, i.e., **above the major duodenal papilla** where the bile duct opens, there is no bile in the vomit. The strong gastric contractions (peristalsis) are an attempt to overcome the obstruction. Treatment is surgical (pyloromyotomy).
