s0231 10. The sacrum and coccyx
s0232 The **sacrum** (*os sacrum*) is a triangular bone formed by the fusion of 5 sacral vertebrae. Its broad **base** (*basis ossis sacri*) faces upward and its **apex** (*apex ossis sacri*) downward and forward. Where the base joins the V lumbar vertebra, an anteriorly projecting **promontory** (*promontorium*) is formed — an important landmark in measuring the pelvis.
s0233 The **anterior — pelvic surface** (*facies pelvica*) is concave and smooth: it bears 4 **transverse lines** (*lineae transversae*) — traces of fusion of the vertebral bodies — and at their ends 4 pairs of **anterior sacral foramina** (*foramina sacralia anteriora*). The **posterior surface** (*facies dorsalis*) is convex and rough and bears 5 longitudinal crests: the unpaired **median sacral crest** (*crista sacralis mediana*) — from the fusion of the spinous processes; the paired **medial crests** (*cristae sacrales mediales*) — from the articular processes; and the paired **lateral crests** (*cristae sacrales laterales*) — from the transverse processes. Between the medial and lateral crests lie 4 pairs of **posterior sacral foramina** (*foramina sacralia posteriora*).
s0234 Figure 1.14
s0235 Sacrum and coccyx, pelvic surface: 1 — base of the sacrum; 2 — superior articular processes; 3 — lateral part; 4 — anterior sacral foramina; 5 — apex of the sacrum; 6 — coccyx; 7 — transverse lines. Source: A. Ahmedov et al. Anatomy, vol. I, 2018.
s0236 Lateral to the foramina lies the **lateral part** (*pars lateralis*); the **auricular surface** (*facies auricularis*) on its side articulates with the corresponding surface of the ilium, and behind it is the **sacral tuberosity** (*tuberositas ossis sacri*), to which ligaments attach. The vertebral foramina join to form the **sacral canal** (*canalis sacralis*); the canal opens below by the **sacral hiatus** (*hiatus sacralis*), on either side of which stand the **sacral cornua** (*cornua sacralia*) — remnants of the articular processes.

Source: A. Ahmedov et al. Anatomy, vol. I, 2018.
s0239 The **coccyx** (*os coccygis*) is formed by the fusion of 3–5 rudimentary vertebrae; it is triangular and curved forward. The I coccygeal vertebra has a small body that joins the sacrum and upward-directed **coccygeal cornua** (*cornua coccygea*).
s0240 **Age-related features.** In the newborn the sacrum is wedge-shaped and consists mainly of cartilage. At 3–8 years the ossification centers of each vertebra fuse; during this period vertebra I shifts forward and vertebrae II–IV backward, forming the **sacral kyphosis**. The sacral vertebrae fuse with each other into a single bone at **17–25 years**. In the newborn the coccygeal vertebrae are 4–5 cartilaginous vertebrae consisting only of bodies; the ossification center of the I coccygeal vertebra appears at 1–7 years, and the coccyx ossifies completely at ≈30 years.

s0269 Practical skill: algorithm for identifying the region of a vertebra
s0270 Place the vertebra in the **anatomical position**: the body in front, the spinous process facing backward and downward, the vertebral foramen directed vertically.
s0271 Look at the transverse process: if it **has a foramen**, it is a **cervical** vertebra. If it has no body and two arches — the **atlas**; if a dens projects from the body — the **axis**; if the spinous process is long and not bifid — **C~VII~**.
s0272 If there are **costal facets** on the side of the body, it is a **thoracic** vertebra; distinguish T~I~, T~X~ and T~XI–XII~ by whether the facets are whole or half facets.
s0273 If the body is large and bean-shaped, there are no foramina or costal facets, and the spinous process is horizontal and broad — it is a **lumbar** vertebra.
s0274 Name and show the parts of the vertebra in Latin: *corpus, arcus, pediculus, lamina, foramen vertebrale, processus spinosus, transversus, articularis superior et inferior, incisura vertebralis*.
s0275 Hold the sacrum with its base upward and its concave pelvic surface facing forward; show the transverse lines, the anterior and posterior foramina, the three crests, the auricular surface, the sacral canal and the hiatus.
s0276 Practical skill: palpation in a living person
s0277 Ask the seated patient to bend the head forward; find the most prominent process at the lower part of the neck — **C~VII~**.
s0278 Count the spinous processes of the thoracic vertebrae downward from it; with the arms hanging down, the inferior angle of the scapula lies at the level of the T~VII~ process.
s0279 With the patient standing, join the highest points of the two iliac crests — the line passes through the **L~IV~** process or the L~IV~–L~V~ interspace.
s0280 Palpate the median sacral crest and the sacral cornua at its lower end, and show the location of the sacral hiatus.

s0382 Case 1
s0383 An 8-year-old child fell from a tree and complains of pain in the forearm. The X-ray shows a fracture of the radial diaphysis, but the fragments are not displaced and the periosteum is intact.
s0384 What type of fracture is this? Why is it common in children and how does it heal?
s0385 This is a **“greenstick” (subperiosteal) fracture**. Children's bones contain relatively more organic matter and fewer mineral salts — the bone is flexible; the periosteum is thick and strong, so it holds the fragments together. The cambial layer of the periosteum is well developed and the bone is rich in blood vessels — so the fracture heals faster than in adults.
s0386 Case 2
s0387 After an injury, the neck X-ray of a patient shows that a vertebra without a body and with two arches has fractures of its anterior and posterior arches in several places. The patient had dived head first into a shallow pool.
s0388 Which vertebra is injured? Why is this injury dangerous?
s0389 The vertebra without a body is the **atlas (C~I~)**; with a vertical blow to the head, the occipital condyles force the lateral masses of the atlas apart — a **Jefferson fracture**. The injury is dangerous because it lies close to the upper cervical segments of the spinal cord and to the vertebral artery (which passes through the foramen transversarium of the atlas and lies in the groove on its posterior arch); if C~III~–C~V~ are damaged, diaphragmatic paralysis and respiratory arrest may occur.
s0390 Case 3
s0391 A physician is going to perform a diagnostic lumbar puncture. The patient is lying on his side with his knees drawn up to his abdomen.
s0392 Between which vertebrae is the needle inserted? How can the site be found, and why is it performed at this level?
s0393 The puncture is performed between the spinous processes of **L~III~–L~IV~** or **L~IV~–L~V~**. The landmark is the line joining the highest points of the two iliac crests, which passes through the L~IV~ process. In adults the spinal cord ends at the L~I~–L~II~ level and below it there are only nerve roots — so there is no risk of injuring the spinal cord. The spinous processes of the lumbar vertebrae are horizontal and the spaces between them are wide, and in the flexed position the space widens further.

s0422 Remember the classification of the ribs by the number **“7 – 3 – 2”**: **7** pairs of true, **3** pairs of false and **2** pairs of floating ribs. 12 pairs in total. In the newborn, however, rib X has not yet joined the costal arch — the classification is **7 – 2 – 3**.
s0423 In the costal groove the blood vessels and nerve lie from above downward in the order **VAN**: **V**ein — **A**rtery — **N**erve; the nerve is lowest and projects slightly from the groove. Therefore, in pleural puncture and intercostal block the needle is inserted along **the upper border of the lower rib** — so that the vessels and nerve are not injured.
s0424 Sometimes an additional **cervical rib** develops from the transverse process of the VII cervical vertebra (in ≈0.5–1 % of the population). It may compress the subclavian artery and fibers of the brachial plexus over the I rib — **thoracic outlet syndrome**: pain, numbness and weakness in the arm and a weakened pulse. The compression most often occurs in the region of the tubercle of the anterior scalene muscle.
s0425 **The sternal angle is the “key” to counting the ribs.** It is easily palpated under the skin as a transverse ridge; the **II rib** lies beside it, and below that is the **II intercostal space**. The I rib cannot be palpated under the clavicle, so counting begins with the II rib. At the level of the sternal angle lie, posteriorly, the T~IV~–T~V~ intervertebral space and, internally, the tracheal bifurcation and the beginning and end of the aortic arch.
s0426 **Sternal puncture** (piercing the sternum to obtain red marrow) is performed in the upper part of the sternal body at the level of the III rib, or in the manubrium: here the bone is superficial and its spongy substance is rich in red marrow. A special needle with a stop (Arinkin needle) is used, because the posterior plate of the sternum is thin and the heart and great vessels lie behind it. In children the sternum is thin, so marrow is usually taken from the ilium or the tibia.
s0427 Pathological shapes of the thoracic cage are important in diagnosis: **“pigeon chest”** (*pectus carinatum*) — the sternum protrudes forward, often after rickets; **funnel chest** (*pectus excavatum*) — the lower sternum is sunken inward (congenital); **emphysematous (barrel) chest** — the anteroposterior diameter is increased and the ribs are horizontal (in pulmonary emphysema); **rachitic rosary** — thickenings at the junctions of the bony and cartilaginous parts of the ribs. In the elderly the costal cartilages calcify and the mobility of the thoracic cage decreases.
