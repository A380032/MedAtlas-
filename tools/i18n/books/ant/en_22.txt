s2587 When studying the back muscles topographically, do not mix up the order of the layers: **trapezius and latissimus → rhomboids and levator scapulae → serratus posterior muscles → splenius → erector spinae → transversospinalis → short segmental and suboccipital muscles**. The three superficial layers are "migrated" muscles (of the upper limb and ribs); the rest are the intrinsic muscles of the back.
s2588 In school-age children, uneven development of the back muscles and poor sitting posture lead to **postural disorders** and **scoliosis**. Physical exercises that strengthen the back and abdominal muscles in a balanced way, swimming, a properly chosen desk and chair, and carrying a backpack on both shoulders are the basis of prevention.
s2589 1. Skeletal muscle as an organ
s2590 **Myology** (Greek *mys* — muscle) is the study of muscles. Skeletal muscles are built of striated muscle tissue and contract voluntarily; together with the bones and their joints they form the **active part of the locomotor apparatus**. A human has more than 400 (according to some authors nearly 600) skeletal muscles; in adults their mass makes up ≈36–45% of body weight in men and ≈28–35% in women; about half of the muscle mass is in the lower limbs, 30% in the upper limbs, and the rest in the trunk and head.
s2591 Each muscle consists of a **belly** (*venter*) — the contractile part — and non-contractile **tendons** (*tendines*) of dense connective tissue. The proximal end of a muscle (nearer the trunk) is the **head** (*caput*), and the distal end the **tail** (*cauda*). A muscle usually **arises** from one bone (*origo* — usually the fixed point, *punctum fixum*) and **inserts** on another (*insertio* — the mobile point, *punctum mobile*); depending on the situation these points may swap. Broad flat tendons are called **aponeuroses** (for example, those of the broad abdominal muscles). Some muscles have **tendinous intersections** (*intersectiones tendineae*) dividing the belly into parts (the rectus abdominis).
s2592 Each muscle fiber is surrounded by the **endomysium**, each bundle of fibers by the **perimysium**, and the whole muscle by the **epimysium**; blood vessels and nerves enter along these connective tissue layers, which continue into the tendons. Muscles are rich in blood vessels; their contraction "pumps" the blood in the veins toward the heart (the **muscle pump**) — with inactivity (hypokinesia) this pump weakens, and the risk of venous stasis and thrombosis increases. A nerve usually enters a muscle at one place — the **neurovascular hilum**; the nerve contains motor (efferent) and sensory (afferent, from proprioceptors) fibers.

s2607 The strength of a muscle depends on its **physiological cross-section** — the area of a section cutting across all its fibers. In fusiform and strap-like muscles the anatomical and physiological cross-sections are the same; in **pennate muscles** the fibers are short and numerous, so the physiological cross-section is large — they are **strong**, but their range of contraction is small. Long parallel-fibered muscles are weaker but contract **over a large range and quickly**.
s2608 3. The accessory apparatus of muscles and their action
s2609 The work of muscles is supported by the **accessory apparatus**: **fasciae** — connective tissue sheaths surrounding a muscle or group of muscles; they form intermuscular septa that separate muscles, reduce friction during contraction and form sheaths for neurovascular bundles. **Tendon sheaths** — sheaths with fibrous and synovial parts where tendons pass over bone (hand, foot); the synovial sheath has two layers with fluid between them. **Synovial bursae** — flat sacs filled with fluid, located where tendons and muscles rub against bone. **Muscle trochleae** — bony prominences or fibrous rings around which a tendon turns (the trochlea of the superior oblique muscle of the eye). **Sesamoid bones** — bones within a tendon (the patella, the pisiform); they lengthen the lever arm of the muscle's pull.

s2626 4. Muscles of the back: general information
s2627 The back muscles form two layers. The **superficial muscles** are flat, broad muscles; developmentally they are related to the upper limb and the ribs; they arise from the vertebral column and insert on the shoulder girdle, the humerus or the ribs. The **deep (intrinsic) muscles** are the back's own (autochthonous) muscles; they lie on both sides of the vertebral column from the sacrum to the occipital bone, are supplied mainly by the **posterior (dorsal) rami** of the spinal nerves, and hold the vertebral column upright, extend it and rotate it.
s2628 5. Superficial muscles of the back
s2629 The first layer is formed by the **trapezius** and the **latissimus dorsi**, the second by the **rhomboid muscles** and the **levator scapulae**, and the third by the **serratus posterior superior and inferior**.

s2667 6. Deep (intrinsic) muscles of the back
s2668 The deep muscles also form layers. The **superficial layer** is formed by the **splenius capitis and splenius cervicis** (*m. splenius capitis et cervicis*): they arise from the nuchal ligament and the spinous processes of the upper thoracic vertebrae and insert on the mastoid process and superior nuchal line (capitis) and on the transverse processes of the upper cervical vertebrae (cervicis); contracting bilaterally they extend the head and neck, unilaterally they turn them to the same side.
s2669 The **erector spinae** (*m. erector spinae*) is the strongest muscle of the back; it arises from the sacrum, the iliac crest, the spinous processes of the lumbar vertebrae and the thoracolumbar fascia, and above it divides into three parts: lateral — the **iliocostalis** (*m. iliocostalis*, to the angles of the ribs and the transverse processes of the cervical vertebrae), middle — the **longissimus** (*m. longissimus*, to the transverse processes, ribs and mastoid process), medial — the **spinalis** (*m. spinalis*, between the spinous processes). Contracting bilaterally it **extends** the vertebral column and holds the trunk upright; unilaterally it bends it to the side.

s2672 Deeper still lie the **transversospinalis muscles** (*m. transversospinalis*): their fibers run obliquely from the transverse processes to the spinous processes of higher vertebrae. Deepest of all are the short **interspinales** (*mm. interspinales*) and **intertransversarii** (*mm. intertransversarii*), the **levatores costarum**, and below the occiput the **suboccipital muscles** (*mm. suboccipitales* — rectus capitis posterior major and minor, obliquus capitis superior and inferior; they move the head at the atlanto-occipital and atlanto-axial joints).

s2693 7. Fasciae and regions of the back
s2694 The **thoracolumbar fascia** (*fascia thoracolumbalis*) surrounds the intrinsic muscles of the back and consists of two layers: the **superficial (posterior) layer** attaches to the spinous processes, the supraspinous ligament and the median sacral crest and is thick in the lumbar region (fused with the aponeurosis of the latissimus dorsi); the **deep (anterior) layer** stretches between the transverse processes of the lumbar vertebrae, the 12th rib and the iliac crest, separating the erector spinae from the quadratus lumborum. The two layers fuse at the lateral edge of the erector spinae and give origin to the transversus abdominis.
