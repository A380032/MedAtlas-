s4146 7. The pharynx
s4147 The **pharynx** (*pharynx*) is an unpaired funnel-shaped organ **12–14 cm** long where the digestive and respiratory tracts cross. Above it is attached to the cranial base (the **vault of the pharynx**), and below, at the level of the **6th–7th cervical vertebrae**, it passes into the esophagus. Behind it lie the prevertebral muscles and fascia (with the **retropharyngeal space** between them), and at the sides the neurovascular bundle of the neck. According to the organs in front of it, it has three parts:
s4148 The **nasal part** (*pars nasalis*, nasopharynx) — between the vault and the soft palate; in front it communicates with the nasal cavity through the choanae; on its lateral walls lie the **pharyngeal opening of the auditory tube** (connecting it with the middle ear) and the **torus tubarius** above it; at the junction of the vault and posterior wall — the **pharyngeal tonsil** (adenoids), around the tubal opening — the **tubal tonsils**.
s4149 The **oral part** (*pars oralis*, oropharynx) — between the soft palate and the laryngeal inlet; it opens into the oral cavity through the isthmus of the fauces; here the respiratory and digestive tracts cross.
s4150 The **laryngeal part** (*pars laryngea*, laryngopharynx) — from the laryngeal inlet to the esophagus; in its anterior wall is the laryngeal inlet, and on either side of it the **piriform recesses** (where foreign bodies can become lodged).

s4153 The **wall of the pharynx** consists of a mucosa (pseudostratified ciliated epithelium in the nasal part, stratified squamous in the lower parts), the dense **pharyngobasilar fascia** in place of the submucosa (which above anchors the pharynx to the cranial base), a muscular coat and an adventitia. The **muscles** are striated: the **constrictors** — superior, middle and inferior pharyngeal constrictors (meeting at the pharyngeal raphe in the posterior wall; contracting in sequence from above downward, they push the bolus into the esophagus) — and the **elevators** — the stylopharyngeus and palatopharyngeus (raise the pharynx during swallowing). The pharyngeal muscles are supplied mainly by the **pharyngeal plexus** (IX, X).

s4156 In the **act of swallowing** the tongue pushes the bolus through the isthmus of the fauces into the pharynx; the soft palate rises, separating off the nasal part of the pharynx; the pharynx and larynx rise, and the **epiglottis** closes the laryngeal inlet; the constrictor muscles pass the bolus into the esophagus.
s4157 The **Pirogov–Waldeyer lymphoepithelial ring** consists of aggregations of lymphoid tissue encircling, like a ring, the entrance into the pharynx from the oral and nasal cavities: **two paired** — the **palatine tonsils** and **tubal tonsils**, and **two unpaired** — the **pharyngeal tonsil** and **lingual tonsil**. They act as the first immune barrier against antigens entering the respiratory and digestive tracts.
s4158 In the newborn there are no teeth and the alveolar processes are poorly developed, so the oral cavity is small and its vestibule is a narrow slit; after the teeth erupt (infancy and early childhood) the oral cavity enlarges considerably. The gums bear ridges at the sites of the future teeth.
s4159 The newborn's lips are thick, with transverse ridges on their inner surface and a tubercle in the middle of the upper lip — adaptations for suckling; the orbicularis oris is well developed. The **buccal fat pad** is well developed in the newborn — it stiffens the cheek and prevents the oral wall from being sucked inward during suckling.
s4160 In the newborn the hard palate is broad and flat, without a noticeable vault; the soft palate lies horizontally and does not touch the posterior wall of the pharynx — the baby can breathe freely while suckling. The length of the palate is 3.9 cm in the newborn, 5.2 cm at 5 months and 7.9 cm at 20 years.
s4161 The newborn's tongue is short, broad and thick, filling the oral cavity; the fungiform, foliate and vallate papillae and taste buds are well developed, the filiform papillae few; the frenulum is short. The tongue muscles are well developed because they take an active part in suckling.
s4162 In the newborn the parotid gland is relatively large (≈1.8 g); salivation increases from 2–3 months ("physiological drooling" — the baby cannot yet swallow saliva); the gland grows rapidly from 4 months to 2 years (≈9 g) and reaches 30 g by 20 years. The sublingual gland weighs ≈0.4 g in the newborn.
s4163 The newborn's pharynx is wide and short (≈3 cm long), funnel-shaped, with a thin wall; its lower border lies at the level of the 3rd–4th cervical vertebrae (6th–7th in adults). The vault is not apparent. The pharyngeal opening of the auditory tube is slit-like, patent and close to the hard palate, and the torus tubarius is undeveloped — infection passes easily into the middle ear; by 12–14 years the opening becomes round.
s4164 In the newborn the oral part of the pharynx is very short (≈0.5 cm) and the laryngeal part relatively long; the epiglottis stands high — this allows the baby to suckle (swallow) and breathe at the same time.
s4165 The tonsils are poorly developed in the newborn; they enlarge from 1–2 years, are largest at 4–7 years (adenoids are common at this age), and gradually involute after puberty.

s4250 The mother of a 9-month-old baby is worried that not a single tooth has erupted yet. The baby has softness of the skull bones, a widely open anterior fontanelle, a "rachitic rosary" on the ribs and excessive sweating.
s4251 When and in what order do the deciduous teeth normally begin to erupt? With what disease may this condition be associated?
s4252 Normally the deciduous teeth erupt from **6–8 months**, beginning with the **lower central incisors**, then the upper central and lateral incisors (7–10 months), first molars (12–21 months), canines (15–20 months) and second molars (21–30 months); by **2–2.5 years** there are 20 deciduous teeth (formula 2·1·0·2). The absence of teeth at 9 months and the bone changes noted are signs of **rickets** (vitamin D deficiency); late tooth eruption is also seen in hypothyroidism. The child should be examined by a pediatrician.
s4253 An 8-year-old child always keeps the mouth open, snores at night, has difficulty breathing through the nose, and often complains of earache and reduced hearing. On examination the face is elongated and the upper lip short.
s4254 Which lymphoid structure is enlarged? Why are ear diseases common? Which ring does this structure belong to?
s4255 The **pharyngeal tonsil** (adenoids) is enlarged. It lies in the **nasal part** of the pharynx, at the junction of the vault and posterior wall; when enlarged it blocks the choanae, making nasal breathing difficult, and covers the **pharyngeal opening of the auditory tube** on the lateral wall of the nasopharynx, impairing ventilation of the middle ear — resulting in otitis and reduced hearing. The pharyngeal tonsil is part of the **Pirogov–Waldeyer lymphoepithelial ring** (the paired palatine and tubal tonsils and the unpaired pharyngeal and lingual tonsils).
s4256 A 35-year-old patient complains that every time he eats, a painful swelling appears under the left side of the mandible and gradually subsides after the meal. A hard mass is palpable in the floor of the mouth in the region of the left sublingual papilla.
s4257 Which gland and duct are affected? Why does this disease occur so often in precisely this gland?
s4258 This is a **stone in the duct of the left submandibular gland** (sialolithiasis). The gland lies in the submandibular triangle; its **Wharton's duct** runs forward along the floor of the mouth and opens on the **sublingual papilla**. During meals salivation increases, and because the stone blocks the duct, the gland swells and becomes painful. Sialolithiasis occurs mainly in this gland because its duct is long and ascending (saliva flows against gravity), and its secretion is mixed — thicker and rich in calcium.
