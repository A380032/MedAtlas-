s4290 **Gastric ulcers** are most often located on the **lesser curvature** and in the pyloric part; when an ulcer of the posterior wall perforates it may penetrate into the pancreas (penetration), and an ulcer of the lesser curvature may be complicated by bleeding from the left gastric artery. **Pyloric stenosis** is congenital thickening of the muscle of the pyloric sphincter in infants (most often boys aged 2–6 weeks): "projectile" vomiting after feeds, and an "olive"-like mass is palpable in the epigastric region; it is treated surgically (pyloromyotomy).
s4291 **Meckel's diverticulum** is a remnant of the embryonic **vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct** (connecting the umbilicus with the gut); it occurs in ≈2% of people, in the ileum ≈60–100 cm above the cecum, on the antimesenteric wall. Its inflammation (diverticulitis) mimics appendicitis; if it contains gastric mucosa it causes ulceration and bleeding. Therefore, at an operation for appendicitis, if the appendix is found unchanged, the ileum is examined. **Duodenal ulcers** are located in the **duodenal bulb** in ≈95% of cases.
s4292 **Acute appendicitis** is the most common surgical emergency. The base of the appendix projects onto the anterior abdominal wall at **McBurney's point** — at the junction of the lateral and middle thirds of the line joining the umbilicus with the right anterior superior iliac spine. Depending on the position of the appendix, the pain is felt in different places: with a retrocecal position — in the lower back; with a pelvic position — above the pubis (with disturbed urination); in pregnant women — higher up. In children the greater omentum is short and thin, so the inflammation is not walled off and **peritonitis** develops rapidly.
s4293 The **rectal venous plexus** lies in the submucosa of the anal canal; its varicose dilation is **hemorrhoids**. Above the pectinate line sensation is visceral (painless), below it somatic (very painful) — so internal hemorrhoids and intestinal cancer are painless, while anal fissures are very painful. **Digital rectal examination** (per rectum) allows assessment of the prostate, the cervix of the uterus and the lower part of the ampulla. In children the rectal mucosa is loosely attached, so **rectal prolapse** is common.
s4294 1. The abdominal cavity and the abdominal regions
s4295 The **abdominal cavity** (*cavitas abdominis*) is bounded above by the diaphragm, behind by the lumbar part of the vertebral column, the quadratus lumborum and psoas major, and in front and at the sides by the abdominal muscles; below it continues into the cavity of the lesser pelvis. The walls of the cavity are lined internally by the **endoabdominal fascia** and the **parietal peritoneum**. The relation of the organs to the peritoneum is considered in later topics (topic 19).
s4296 The anterior abdominal wall is divided by two horizontal lines — the **subcostal (intercostal) line** (joining the lowest points of the 10th ribs) and the **interspinous line** (joining the anterior superior iliac spines) — into three tiers, and by two vertical lines (along the lateral borders of the recti) into **9 regions**.

s4312 2. The esophagus
s4313 The **esophagus** (*oesophagus*) is a muscular tube flattened anteroposteriorly, **25–30 cm** long; it carries the bolus from the pharynx to the stomach. It begins as the continuation of the pharynx at the level of the **6th–7th cervical vertebrae** and passes into the stomach at the level of the **10th–11th thoracic vertebrae** (on the left). It has three parts: the **cervical part** (C~VI~–T~II~; in front — the trachea, at the sides — the recurrent laryngeal nerves and common carotid arteries), the **thoracic part** (the longest; in the posterior mediastinum, in front of the vertebral column, behind the trachea down to the 4th thoracic vertebra, then crossed by the aortic arch and the left main bronchus; in its lower part the vagus nerves lie — the left in front, the right behind) and the **abdominal part** (1–3 cm; passing through the diaphragm, it enters the stomach behind the left lobe of the liver).

s4333 3. The stomach
s4334 The **stomach** (*gaster, ventriculus*) is the widest part of the alimentary canal; it lies in the upper part of the abdominal cavity below the diaphragm and liver: **3/4 in the left hypochondrium** and **1/4 in the epigastric region**. It has **anterior and posterior walls**, a **lesser curvature** facing upward and to the right, and a **greater curvature** facing downward and to the left. On the lesser curvature, at the junction of the body and the pyloric part, is the **angular notch**.
s4335 The **cardiac part** (*pars cardiaca*) — the area around the **cardiac orifice**, where the esophagus opens (T~XI~ on the left).
s4336 The **fundus of the stomach** (*fundus gastricus*) — the part rising as a dome to the left of the cardia; in the upright position air collects in it (the "gas bubble" on radiographs); between it and the esophagus is the **cardiac notch**.
s4337 The **body of the stomach** (*corpus gastricum*) — the largest part, between the fundus and the pyloric part.
s4338 The **pyloric part** (*pars pylorica*) — consisting of the wide **pyloric antrum** and the narrow **pyloric canal**; it opens into the duodenum through the orifice of the **pylorus** (L~I~ on the right).

s4341 The capacity of the stomach averages **1.5–3 L**; its length (when empty) is 18–20 cm, and the distance between the curvatures 7–8 cm. **Syntopy**: the anterior wall is in contact with the diaphragm, the left lobe of the liver and the anterior abdominal wall; the posterior wall — through the omental bursa — with the pancreas, the left kidney and suprarenal gland, and the spleen; the greater curvature with the transverse colon. s4344 **Wall of the stomach**: the **mucosa** has simple columnar epithelium; numerous **gastric folds** (the longitudinal folds along the lesser curvature form the **gastric canal** — liquids pass quickly along it), **gastric areas** (1–6 mm) and the **gastric pits** in them, into which the gastric glands open. The glands (≈35 million) are **cardiac**, **proper (fundic)** (in the fundus and body; **chief cells** — pepsinogen; **parietal (oxyntic) cells** — hydrochloric acid and Castle's intrinsic factor, necessary for absorption of vitamin B~12~) and **pyloric** glands (mucus; G cells — gastrin). The **muscular coat** consists of three layers of smooth muscle: outer **longitudinal**, middle **circular** (the strongest; at the pylorus it forms the **pyloric sphincter**) and inner **oblique fibers**. Outside is the **serosa** (the stomach is covered by peritoneum on all sides — intraperitoneal).

s4347 The **shapes of the stomach** (on radiographs) depend on body build: **J-shaped (fishhook)** (the most common, ≈80%, normosthenic), **horn-shaped** (wide and high-lying; hypersthenic, in children and the elderly), **elongated hook (stocking-shaped)** (low-lying; asthenic, more often in women).
s4348 4. The small intestine
s4349 The **small intestine** (*intestinum tenue*) is the longest part of the alimentary canal (**2.2–4.4 m** in the living, 5–6 m in the cadaver); it extends from the pylorus to the ileocecal valve. Here food undergoes final digestion under the action of intestinal juice, pancreatic juice and bile, and nutrients are absorbed into the blood and lymph. It has three parts: the **duodenum**, the **jejunum** and the **ileum** (the last two, having a mesentery, are called the **mesenteric part of the small intestine**).
s4350 The **duodenum** (*duodenum*) is **25–30 cm** long and lies in a horseshoe shape around the **head of the pancreas** at the level of L~I~–L~III~. Its parts: the **superior part** (4–5 cm; its initial dilated part — the **bulb (ampulla)** — is covered by peritoneum and mobile), the **descending part** (8–10 cm; to the right of L~I~–L~III~), the **horizontal (inferior) part** and the **ascending part** (to the left of L~II~ it forms the **duodenojejunal flexure** and passes into the jejunum; the flexure is suspended from the diaphragm by the **suspensory muscle (ligament) of Treitz**). Except for the bulb, the duodenum is **retroperitoneal**.
s4351 On the medial wall of the descending part is a **longitudinal fold**; at its lower end, on the **major papilla** (papilla of Vater), the **common bile duct** and the **main pancreatic duct** open together (surrounded by the sphincter of Oddi); 2–3 cm above it, on the **minor papilla**, the **accessory pancreatic duct** opens. The **duodenal (Brunner's) glands** lie in the submucosa.

s4354 The **jejunum** (*jejunum*, ≈2/5) forms horizontal loops in the upper left part of the abdominal cavity; the **ileum** (*ileum*, ≈3/5) forms vertical loops in the lower right part and enters the cecum in the right iliac fossa. s4380 5. The large intestine
s4381 The **large intestine** (*intestinum crassum*) is the continuation of the small intestine, **1–1.5 m** long, with a diameter ≈2 times that of the small intestine (4–8 cm); it extends from the cecum to the anus. Water and electrolytes are absorbed here and feces are formed; the microflora takes part in the synthesis of vitamins (K, B group). Its parts: the **cecum** (with the vermiform appendix), the **colon** — **ascending**, **transverse**, **descending** and **sigmoid** — and the **rectum**. The large intestine surrounds the loops of the small intestine like a "frame."
