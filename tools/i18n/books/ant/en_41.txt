s5140 After a thyroidectomy the patient's voice became hoarse. On laryngoscopy the left vocal fold is motionless in the median position, while the right fold moves normally.
s5141 Which nerve is injured? Which muscles does it supply, and why has the voice changed? What happens with bilateral injury?
s5142 The **left recurrent laryngeal nerve** (inferior laryngeal nerve) is injured — it runs in the tracheoesophageal groove behind the lobes of the thyroid gland and may be damaged during operation. It supplies **all the intrinsic laryngeal muscles except the cricothyroid**. The vocal fold on the injured side remains motionless, and the rima glottidis does not close completely — the voice becomes hoarse. With bilateral injury the **only dilators — the posterior cricoarytenoid muscles** — are paralyzed, the vocal folds become fixed in the median position and the rima glottidis narrows — severe respiratory failure develops, and a tracheostomy may be needed.
s5143 At night a 2-year-old child suddenly woke with a "barking" cough, a hoarse voice and noisy, labored breathing (inspiratory stridor); during the day the child had had a runny nose.
s5144 What condition (false croup) has developed? Which age-related features of the larynx predispose to it?
s5145 This is **acute stenosing laryngotracheitis (false croup)** — swelling of the laryngeal mucosa, especially of the **infraglottic cavity**, in a viral infection. In young children the laryngeal cavity and rima glottidis are **narrow**, the cartilages are soft, the mucosa is delicate and **rich in blood and lymph vessels**, and there is much **loose submucosa** below the vocal folds — even 1 mm of swelling narrows the lumen considerably and makes breathing difficult. At night, when the child is lying down, the swelling increases.
s5146 For 10 days a 30-year-old woman has complained of purulent nasal discharge, heaviness and pain in the cheek below the eye that increases when she bends over, and toothache. A month ago an upper molar was treated.
s5147 Which paranasal sinus is inflamed? Where does it open, and why is the inflammation prone to become chronic? What is the connection with the tooth?
s5148 The **maxillary sinus (antrum of Highmore)** is inflamed — **maxillary sinusitis**. The sinus opens into the **middle nasal meatus** through the semilunar hiatus; because the opening lies in the **superomedial** part of the sinus, high above its floor, pus drains poorly in the upright position and the process becomes chronic. The floor of the sinus is very close to the **roots of the upper molars and premolars** (sometimes separated only by a thin bony plate or mucosa), so a dental infection can pass into the sinus (**odontogenic maxillary sinusitis**).

s5176 **Foreign bodies** most often enter the **right main bronchus** (and the right lower lobe bronchus) — it is wide, short and almost a straight continuation of the trachea. Widening and immobility of the carina (on bronchoscopy) may be a sign of enlarged bifurcation lymph nodes — for example, metastasis of lung cancer.
s5177 The lungs have two blood circulations: **functional** — pulmonary artery (venous blood) → alveolar capillaries → pulmonary veins (arterial blood) — the pulmonary circulation; **nutritive** — the **bronchial arteries**, arising from the thoracic aorta, nourish the bronchi, pleura and lung tissue.
s5178 Fluid (**pleurisy**, **hydrothorax**), blood (**hemothorax**) or air (**pneumothorax**) may accumulate in the pleural cavity. Fluid collects first in the **costodiaphragmatic recess**; **pleural puncture** is performed in the 7th–8th intercostal space along the midaxillary or posterior axillary line, **along the upper border of the lower rib** (to avoid injuring the neurovascular bundle). In pneumothorax the negative pressure is lost and the lung collapses. Because the cupula of the pleura lies above the clavicle, it may be injured in neck wounds and during subclavian vein catheterization.
s5179 Because the mediastinal tissue communicates with the fascial spaces of the neck, in neck phlegmons and perforation of the esophagus pus enters it, and severe **mediastinitis** develops. Mediastinal tumors (for example, thymoma, lymphoma) compress neighboring organs, causing signs such as shortness of breath, difficulty swallowing, **superior vena cava syndrome** (swelling of the face and neck) and hoarseness (recurrent nerve).
s5180 1. The trachea
s5181 The **trachea** (*trachea*) is the air-conducting tube that continues the larynx; it begins at the level of the **6th cervical vertebra** (at the lower border of the cricoid cartilage) and at the level of the **4th–5th thoracic vertebrae** (the level of the sternal angle) forms the **tracheal bifurcation** (*bifurcatio tracheae*), dividing into the right and left main bronchi. It is **9–11 cm** long and 15–18 mm in diameter. At the bifurcation the **carina** (*carina*) projects internally — an important landmark at bronchoscopy.
s5182 **Cervical part** — in front: the isthmus of the thyroid gland (over rings II–IV), the infrahyoid muscles and cervical fascia; behind: the esophagus; at the sides: the lobes of the thyroid gland and the neurovascular bundle of the neck.
s5183 **Thoracic part** — in the superior mediastinum; in front: the thymus (in children), the left brachiocephalic vein, the aortic arch and the brachiocephalic trunk; behind: the esophagus; at the sides: the mediastinal pleura, on the right the azygos vein, on the left the aortic arch and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve.
s5184 **Wall**: the basis of the trachea consists of **16–20 hyaline cartilaginous half-rings** joined by **anular ligaments**; they are open behind, where the ends of the cartilages are joined by smooth muscle bundles and connective tissue — the **membranous wall** (allowing food to pass through the esophagus). Inside is a **mucosa** with pseudostratified ciliated epithelium (mucous glands, lymphoid nodules), a **submucosa** (tracheal glands), a **fibrocartilaginous membrane** and on the outside an **adventitia**.

s5212 3. The lungs: external structure
s5213 The **lung** (*pulmo*, Greek *pneumon*) is the organ of gas exchange; the right and left lungs lie in the lateral parts of the thoracic cavity within the pleural sacs, with the **mediastinum** between them. They are irregularly conical; the right lung is shorter and wider (the right dome of the diaphragm is higher), the left lung longer and narrower (the heart is displaced to the left). Each adult lung weighs ≈0.5–0.6 kg, and the total alveolar surface is ≈70–100 m².
s5214 **Surfaces**: the **costal surface** (convex), the **diaphragmatic surface (base)** (concave), the **medial surface** — the anterior **mediastinal part** (cardiac impression, deeper in the left lung) and the posterior **vertebral part**; between the lobes — the **interlobar surfaces**.
s5215 **Borders**: the **anterior border** (sharp; in the left lung the **cardiac notch** and below it the **lingula of the left lung**), the **inferior border** (sharp), and the posterior border (blunt, rounded).
s5216 **Apex** — 2–3 cm above the clavicle and 3–4 cm above the 1st rib (behind, at the level of the spinous process of the 7th cervical vertebra).
s5217 **Fissures and lobes**: in the right lung the **oblique fissure** and the **horizontal fissure** divide it into **superior, middle and inferior** lobes; in the left lung only the **oblique fissure** divides it into **superior and inferior** lobes. Posteriorly the oblique fissure begins at the level of the spinous process of the 3rd thoracic vertebra and runs forward and downward along the 6th rib; the horizontal fissure branches off the oblique fissure in the midaxillary line and runs along the 4th rib to the sternum.

s5234 4. Internal structure of the lung: segments, lobule, acinus
s5235 A **bronchopulmonary segment** is a cone-shaped part of the lung supplied by a segmental bronchus and a segmental branch of the pulmonary artery and separated by connective tissue septa (its apex faces the hilum and its base the lung surface). The segmental veins lie in the septa between the segments — a landmark for the segment boundaries. According to the international nomenclature each lung has **10 segments** (in the left lung the apical and posterior, and the medial basal and anterior basal segments often fuse, giving 8–9). The segmental structure allows **segmentectomy** (removal of a single lung segment) and precise localization of a lesion on radiographs.
