s1420 The **greater wings** arise from the lateral surfaces of the body and extend laterally and upward. Four surfaces are distinguished on them: the **cerebral surface** (*facies cerebralis* — part of the middle cranial fossa, with digital impressions and arterial grooves), the **orbital surface** (the lateral wall of the orbit), the **temporal surface** (the wall of the temporal fossa; it is separated from the infratemporal surface by the **infratemporal crest**) and the **maxillary surface**. At the base of the greater wing three foramina lie from front to back: the **foramen rotundum** (*foramen rotundum*), the **foramen ovale** (*foramen ovale*) and the **foramen spinosum** (*foramen spinosum*); the **sphenoidal spine** (*spina ossis sphenoidalis*) projects downward from the posterior angle of the wing.

s1423 The pterygoid processes
s1424 The **pterygoid processes** descend from the junction of the body and the greater wing; each consists of a **medial** and a **lateral plate**, between which is the posteriorly open **pterygoid fossa** (*fossa pterygoidea*); below, the plates are separated by the **pterygoid notch**. The medial plate ends below in the **pterygoid hamulus** (*hamulus pterygoideus*) — the tendon of the tensor veli palatini muscle winds around it. The base of the process is pierced from front to back by the **pterygoid canal** (*canalis pterygoideus*). The masticatory muscles (pterygoid muscles) originate from the outer surface of the lateral plate.

s1449 **Age-related features.** The sphenoid bone develops from many (12–14) ossification centers: the medial plates of the pterygoid processes ossify endesmally, and the remaining parts via cartilage. In the newborn the bone consists of three parts: the body (its anterior and posterior segments separated by thin cartilage) with the attached lesser wings, and on each side the greater wing with the pterygoid process. The two segments of the body fuse **in the 1st year**, and the greater wings with the body at **≈3 years**. The sphenoidal sinus is very small at birth, begins to enlarge from 3–4 years and takes shape at puberty. The cartilage between the sphenoid and occipital bones (**spheno-occipital synchondrosis**) ossifies in adolescence (15–20 years) — it is the main zone providing growth of the cranial base in length.
s1450 2. The temporal bone: general structure
s1451 The **temporal bone** (*os temporale*) is a paired bone; it forms part of the lateral wall and base of the neurocranium, contains the **organ of hearing and balance**, and articulates with the mandible. The temporal bone consists of three parts arranged around the **external acoustic opening** (*porus acusticus externus*): above — the **squamous part** (*pars squamosa*), medially — the **petrous part** or **pyramid** (*pars petrosa*), and below and in front — the **tympanic part** (*pars tympanica*).

s1454 The squamous part
s1455 The **squamous part** is a thin plate directed upward and convex outward; its upper border forms the **squamous suture** with the parietal bone and the greater wing of the sphenoid bone. On its outer — **temporal surface** runs the groove for the middle temporal artery. The **zygomatic process** (*processus zygomaticus*) projects forward from it; it joins the temporal process of the zygomatic bone to form the **zygomatic arch** (*arcus zygomaticus*). At the base of the zygomatic process is the **mandibular fossa** (*fossa mandibularis*), in which the head of the mandible lies, and in front of it the **articular tubercle** (*tuberculum articulare*). The inner — cerebral surface bears digital impressions and arterial grooves.
s1456 The petrous part (pyramid)
s1457 The **pyramid** is the hardest part of the temporal bone; it is shaped like a three-sided pyramid, its **base** continuing laterally into the mastoid process and its **apex** (*apex partis petrosae*) directed forward and medially toward the body of the sphenoid bone. The pyramid has **three surfaces** — anterior, posterior and inferior — and **three borders** — superior, posterior and anterior.
s1458 **Anterior surface** (facing the middle cranial fossa): near the apex is the **trigeminal impression** (*impressio trigeminalis*), in which the trigeminal ganglion lies; in the middle is the **arcuate eminence** (*eminentia arcuata*), formed by the anterior semicircular canal of the inner ear; lateral to it is the thin roof of the tympanic cavity — the **tegmen tympani** (*tegmen tympani*); and there are the hiatuses and grooves of the canals of the greater and lesser petrosal nerves.
s1459 **Posterior surface** (facing the posterior cranial fossa): in its middle is the **internal acoustic opening** (*porus acusticus internus*), leading into the **internal acoustic meatus** (through which cranial nerves VII and VIII and the labyrinthine artery pass); above and lateral to it is the **subarcuate fossa**, and below it the **external aperture of the vestibular aqueduct**.
s1460 **Inferior surface** (facing the external surface of the cranial base): the **external opening of the carotid canal**, behind it the **jugular fossa** (which with the occipital bone forms the jugular foramen), between them the **petrosal fossula**; laterally the thin **styloid process** (*processus styloideus*) and behind it the **stylomastoid foramen** (*foramen stylomastoideum*).
s1461 Grooves for venous sinuses run along the borders of the pyramid: on the **superior border** — the groove for the superior petrosal sinus, and on the **posterior border** — the groove for the inferior petrosal sinus. Behind the base of the pyramid, on the inner surface of the mastoid part, is the broad **groove for the sigmoid sinus**.

s1464 The mastoid process and tympanic part
s1465 The **mastoid process** (*processus mastoideus*) is the outer part of the base of the pyramid; it lies behind the external acoustic opening and is clearly palpable under the skin. The sternocleidomastoid muscle attaches to it; the posterior belly of the digastric muscle arises from the **mastoid notch** on its medial side. Inside the process are air-filled **mastoid cells** (*cellulae mastoideae*); the largest of them — the **mastoid antrum** (*antrum mastoideum*) — communicates with the tympanic cavity.
s1466 The **tympanic part** is a thin, horseshoe-shaped curved plate; it forms the anterior, inferior and posterior walls of the **external acoustic meatus** (*meatus acusticus externus*). The tympanic part is separated from the squamous part by the **petrosquamous** and **petrotympanic fissures**; the **chorda tympani** emerges from the petrotympanic fissure.

s1497 4. Age-related features of the temporal bone
s1498 In the newborn the temporal bone consists of **three parts** (squama, pyramid, tympanic ring) separated by layers of cartilage. The squamous part is thin and consists only of two compact plates; the spongy substance has not formed. The **tympanic part** is not yet a complete ring but has the form of the **tympanic ring** (*anulus tympanicus*), so the **external acoustic meatus is short and mainly cartilaginous**, and the tympanic membrane lies more superficially and more horizontally. **The mastoid process is almost absent** — it develops from the 2nd year, after the child begins to walk, under the pull of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and cells appear within it; the mastoid antrum, however, is present at birth and relatively large. The styloid process develops from two ossification centers (one before birth, the other at ≈2 years).
s1499 In the first year of life the parts of the temporal bone fuse with each other. Up to three years the bone grows evenly; the tympanic part grows rapidly and forms the bony part of the external acoustic meatus, which takes shape at ≈5 years. The mastoid cells enlarge during childhood and at puberty come to resemble those of the adult.
