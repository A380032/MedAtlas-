s2864 3. Intrinsic muscles of the thoracic cage
s2865 The **external intercostal muscles** (*mm. intercostales externi*), 11 on each side, arise from the lower border of the upper rib; their fibers run **downward and forward** (obliquely, in the "hands in pockets" direction) and insert on the upper border of the lower rib. They extend from the tubercle of the rib to the cartilage; between the cartilages the space is covered by the **external intercostal membrane**. Function — raising the ribs for **inspiration**.
s2866 The **internal intercostal muscles** (*mm. intercostales interni*) lie deep to the external ones; they arise from the upper border of the lower rib, their fibers run **upward and forward** (perpendicular to the external ones) and insert on the lower border of the upper rib, internal to the costal groove. They extend from the sternum to the angle of the rib; from the angle to the vertebra they are continued by the **internal intercostal membrane**. Function — lowering the ribs for **expiration** (their interchondral part, however, assists inspiration). Their deepest part is distinguished as the **innermost intercostal muscles**; the intercostal **neurovascular bundle** (vein, artery, nerve — from above downward) lies in the costal groove between the internal and innermost muscles.

s2869 The **subcostal muscles** (*mm. subcostales*) lie on the posteroinferior inner surface of the thoracic cage near the angles of ribs X–XII and span one or two ribs; the **transversus thoracis** (*m. transversus thoracis*) lies on the posterior surface of the anterior chest wall — it arises from the lower part of the body of the sternum and the xiphoid process and inserts on costal cartilages II–VI. Both lower the ribs and take part in expiration. All the intrinsic muscles are supplied by the **intercostal nerves**.
s2870 4. The diaphragm
s2871 The **diaphragm** (*diaphragma*) is a flat musculotendinous partition, domed upward, separating the thoracic and abdominal cavities; it is the main respiratory muscle. It arises from the bones surrounding the inferior thoracic aperture and is divided into three parts according to its origin; its fibers run radially and form the **central tendon** (*centrum tendineum*) in the middle.
s2872 The **lumbar part** (*pars lumbalis*) arises by the right and left **crura** from the bodies of the lumbar vertebrae: the right crus is stronger, from L~I~–L~IV~, and the left crus from L~I~–L~III~. This part also includes the **medial and lateral arcuate ligaments** (stretched over the psoas major and quadratus lumborum) and the **median arcuate ligament** (over the aorta).
s2873 The **costal part** (*pars costalis*) arises from the inner surface of the lower six ribs, interdigitating with the slips of the transversus abdominis.
s2874 The **sternal part** (*pars sternalis*), the smallest part, arises from the posterior surface of the xiphoid process.

s2892 Between the parts of the diaphragm there are weak spots without muscle fibers: the **lumbocostal triangle** (Bochdalek's triangle — between the lumbar and costal parts) and the **sternocostal triangle** (Larrey's triangle — between the sternal and costal parts). They are covered above by the pleura and endothoracic fascia and below by the peritoneum and endoabdominal fascia; **diaphragmatic hernias** may occur through them. The diaphragm is supplied by the **phrenic nerve** (*n. phrenicus*, C~III~–C~V~).
s2893 5. Mechanics of inspiration and expiration
s2894 In quiet **inspiration** the diaphragm contracts, and its dome descends and flattens — the vertical dimension of the thoracic cavity increases (in quiet breathing the diaphragm provides ≈2/3 of ventilation); the external intercostal muscles raise the ribs, increasing the anteroposterior and transverse dimensions of the thoracic cage. Quiet **expiration** is mainly **passive** — due to the elastic recoil of the lungs, the elasticity of the costal cartilages and the pressure of the abdominal organs. In deep (forced) breathing the **accessory muscles** join in.

s2904 6. Fasciae of the thorax
s2905 In the thoracic region the superficial fascia is poorly developed; it encloses the mammary gland and sends septa into it (the suspensory ligaments of the breast). The **pectoral fascia** (*fascia pectoralis*), with its superficial layer, encloses the pectoralis major (attaching above to the clavicle and medially to the sternum, continuing laterally into the deltoid and axillary fasciae); its deep layer — the **clavipectoral fascia** — encloses the pectoralis minor and subclavius. The **proper fascia of the thorax** covers the external intercostal muscles from outside; the **endothoracic fascia** (*fascia endothoracica*) lines the thoracic cage from inside, bordering the parietal pleura.
s2906 7. Muscles of the abdomen
s2907 The abdominal muscles form the walls of the abdominal cavity and, by topography, are divided into muscles of the **lateral wall** (three broad muscles), the **anterior wall** (rectus and pyramidalis) and the **posterior wall** (quadratus lumborum). The three broad muscles of the lateral wall — the **external oblique**, **internal oblique** and **transversus** — have fibers running in three different directions (the external downward and medially, the internal mainly upward and medially, the transversus horizontally); this "three-ply plywood" gives strength to the abdominal wall. Their aponeuroses form the sheath of the rectus abdominis and, in the midline, the **linea alba**.

s2942 8. Abdominal fasciae, the linea alba and the rectus sheath
s2943 The **superficial fascia of the abdomen** lies between the subcutaneous fat and the muscles. Layers of the **proper fascia** enclose each broad muscle; in the region of the superficial inguinal ring the fascia of the external oblique forms the **intercrural fibers** and continues onto the spermatic cord as the **external spermatic fascia**. The **transversalis fascia** (*fascia transversalis*) lines the abdominal wall from inside — it is part of the endoabdominal fascia; internal to it lie the preperitoneal fat and the parietal peritoneum.
s2944 The **linea alba** (*linea alba*) is a fibrous band stretched from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis, formed by the crossing and interweaving of the fibers of the aponeuroses of the three broad muscles. Above the umbilicus it is wide (≈2–2.5 cm) and thin; below it is narrow and thick. Because it has few blood vessels, the **midline laparotomy** incision is made along this line in surgery. The **umbilical ring** lies in the middle of the linea alba.
