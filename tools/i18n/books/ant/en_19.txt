s2308 A 42-year-old porter felt sudden low back pain while lifting a heavy box; the pain radiates along the left buttock and the back of the thigh down to the heel, and there is numbness on the lateral side of the left foot. MRI showed protrusion of the L~V~–S~I~ disc posteriorly and to the left.
s2309 What is this condition? How is the disc built, and why did it protrude precisely in the posterolateral direction?
s2310 This is an **intervertebral disc herniation**. The disc consists of a peripheral **anulus fibrosus** and a central **nucleus pulposus**; under a heavy load the nucleus is pushed out under pressure through a tear in the anulus. Posteriorly in the midline the **posterior longitudinal ligament** is thick and blocks the herniation, but at the sides it is thin — so the herniation protrudes **posterolaterally** and compresses the **S~I~ nerve root** next to the intervertebral foramen (pain radiating to the buttock, back of the thigh, leg and heel — sciatica). The L~IV~–L~V~ and L~V~–S~I~ discs are affected most often, because load and movement are greatest in the lumbar region.
s2311 While laughing, a patient yawned widely and afterward could not close the mouth; the mouth is locked open, the area in front of the ear is depressed, and speech and swallowing are difficult.
s2312 What injury has occurred, and at which joint? How should it be reduced?
s2313 This is a **dislocation of the temporomandibular joint** (anterior dislocation). When the mouth opens wide, the head of the mandible, together with the disc, moves onto the **articular tubercle**; with excessive opening it slips forward over the tubercle and becomes locked there by muscle contraction (in front of the ear the empty mandibular fossa forms a depression). Reduction: the thumbs (wrapped in gauze) are placed on the lower molars, the jaw is first pressed **downward** to bring it below the tubercle and then pushed **backward**.
s2314 A pediatrician is examining a 4-month-old infant. The baby holds the head well but does not yet sit. The mother is worried that the baby's spine "is not straight — there is a curve in the neck."
s2315 Is this normal? In what order do the curvatures of the vertebral column form?
s2316 Yes, it is **normal**. In the newborn the vertebral column is almost straight; once the child begins to hold the head upright (≈3 months), the **cervical lordosis** forms under the action of the posterior neck muscles. Later, when the child begins to sit (≈6 months), the **thoracic kyphosis** appears, and when the child begins to stand and walk (≈1 year), the **lumbar lordosis**; the sacral kyphosis forms during childhood. The curvatures become fixed by 7 years (cervical and thoracic) and at 12–14 years (lumbar).

s2345 Dislocation of the shoulder joint is the most common dislocation (≈50% of all dislocations). The weakest part of the capsule is its **anteroinferior** part, which is not covered by muscles, so the head usually dislocates **forward and downward** (below the coracoid process). The rounded contour of the shoulder is lost (a hollow below the acromion), and the arm is fixed in slight abduction. The **axillary nerve**, which passes below the head, may be injured — so sensation over the lateral surface of the shoulder is checked before and after reduction.
s2346 **Subluxation of the radial head** ("nursemaid's elbow") occurs in children aged 1–4 years when the child's arm is pulled sharply (catching a falling child, "swinging" the child): in children the **anular ligament** is wide, while the head is small and hardly wider than the neck, so the head partly slips out of the ligament. The child does not move the arm, and the forearm hangs in pronation. It is easily reduced by flexing the elbow while supinating.
s2347 The most important **saddle** joint of the hand is the joint between the trapezium and the 1st metacarpal. Thanks to it, the thumb can be **opposed** to the other fingers (opposition) — the basis of the grasping function of the human hand. Osteoarthritis of this joint (rhizarthrosis) sharply impairs grasping in elderly people.
s2348 At the end of pregnancy, under the action of hormones (relaxin), the ligaments of the sacroiliac joints and symphysis soften and the cleft in the symphysis widens — the pelvis can widen slightly during delivery. With excessive laxity, **symphysitis** develops (pain in the symphysis region, a "waddling gait"). In the newborn the **pubic symphysis** has the form of a synchondrosis; its cavity appears at 1–2 years and is clearly defined at 5–7 years.
s2349 In the newborn the **acetabulum is shallow and oval**, most of the femoral head lies outside the socket, the capsule is thin and tight, and the ligaments are weak. That is why **developmental dysplasia and congenital dislocation of the hip** occur at this age (more often in girls and in those born in breech presentation). Signs: asymmetry of the thigh skin folds, shortening of the leg, limited abduction of the thigh, the "clunk" (Ortolani) sign. Ultrasound screening is performed in newborns; on radiographs a break in Shenton's line indicates dislocation.
s2350 **Three structures** may be injured together in a knee injury — the **"unhappy triad"**: the anterior cruciate ligament, the medial collateral (tibial collateral) ligament and the **medial meniscus** (because the medial meniscus is attached to the medial collateral ligament). This injury occurs when a blow strikes the knee from the lateral side while it is flexed and the leg is rotated outward (soccer, skiing).
s2351 When the **anterior cruciate ligament** is torn, the leg can be pulled forward — the **"anterior drawer" sign** (with a torn posterior ligament — the "posterior drawer"). A **meniscal tear** presents with pain along the joint line and "locking" of the joint (the torn fragment is caught in the joint). When fluid or blood accumulates in the joint, the patella "floats" (ballottement), and the **suprapatellar bursa** swells and bulges above the patella; joint aspiration is performed precisely in the region of this bursa. The best method for visualizing the menisci and ligaments is **MRI**; they are treated arthroscopically.
s2352 When the foot turns inward (a fall, walking on uneven ground), the **lateral ligaments** are injured most often, especially the anterior talofibular ligament ("ankle sprain"). With a stronger injury the malleoli fracture. Congenital **clubfoot** (*pes equinovarus*) is a position of the foot that is plantarflexed, turned inward (supinated) and adducted; it is treated from the first weeks of life with serial casting (the Ponseti method).
s2353 On radiographs of children's joints most of the bone ends are **cartilaginous** and invisible, so the joint space appears **much wider** than in adults, and the epiphyses look like small "islands." To avoid misjudging this as a dislocation or joint effusion, one must know the age-specific norms and compare with the healthy side.
s2354 1. Joints of the shoulder girdle
s2355 The shoulder girdle is attached to the axial skeleton by only one joint — the **sternoclavicular joint**. The **sternoclavicular joint** (*articulatio sternoclavicularis*) is formed between the sternal end of the clavicle and the clavicular notch of the sternum; it is saddle-shaped, but because of the **articular disc** inside it, it moves (to a limited extent) around three axes like a ball-and-socket joint: elevation and depression of the clavicle, forward and backward movement, and circumduction. It is reinforced by the **anterior and posterior sternoclavicular**, **interclavicular** and **costoclavicular** ligaments.

s2358 The **acromioclavicular joint** (*articulatio acromioclavicularis*) is a plane joint between the acromial end of the clavicle and the acromion. It is reinforced by the **acromioclavicular ligament** and the very strong **coracoclavicular ligament** (*lig. coracoclaviculare*: conoid and trapezoid parts). Intrinsic ligaments of the scapula: the **coracoacromial ligament** (forms a "vault" over the shoulder joint) and the **superior transverse scapular ligament** over the scapular notch.
s2359 **Age-related feature.** In the newborn the sternoclavicular joint is ball-shaped, and the articular disc is twice as large as the articular surfaces of the clavicle and sternum.
s2360 2. The shoulder joint
s2361 The **shoulder joint** (*articulatio humeri*) is formed by the head of the humerus and the glenoid cavity of the scapula. The surface of the head is 3–4 times larger than that of the cavity; this mismatch is partly made up by the fibrocartilaginous **glenoid labrum** (*labrum glenoidale*) at the rim of the cavity. The joint capsule is wide, loose and thin; it attaches on the scapula along the labrum and on the humerus along the **anatomical neck**, and below (with the arm lowered) it forms a fold — the **axillary recess**. The tendon of the long head of the biceps passes through the capsular cavity; the synovial membrane forms the **intertubercular synovial sheath** around the tendon and communicates with the bursa under the subscapularis tendon.
s2362 The capsule is reinforced by the **coracohumeral ligament** (*lig. coracohumerale*) and three **glenohumeral ligaments** in the capsular wall (*ligg. glenohumeralia*); but the joint is held mainly by the surrounding muscles — the tendons of the subscapularis, supraspinatus, infraspinatus and teres minor (the **rotator cuff**). From above, the joint is protected by the **coracoacromial arch** (the "vault" of the shoulder joint), consisting of the acromion, the coracoid process and the coracoacromial ligament; it limits raising the arm above shoulder level — raising it higher is done by movement of the scapula.
s2363 The shoulder joint is **ball-and-socket, multiaxial** — the most mobile joint in the body: flexion and extension around the frontal axis, abduction and adduction around the sagittal axis, medial and lateral rotation around the vertical axis, and circumduction.

s2368 **Radiographic anatomy.** On an AP radiograph the glenoid cavity of the scapula appears as an oval with two convex lines, with the head of the humerus partly superimposed on it; above the head the acromion and acromioclavicular joint are seen, and medially the coracoid process. The joint space is a lucent band between the inner rim of the cavity and the head, on average **4 mm** wide.

s2371 **Age-related features.** In the newborn the articular surfaces are cartilaginous, the articular surface of the scapula is flat and oval, the labrum is thin and low, the capsule is tight (forming folds below), and the ligaments are poorly developed. Formation of the joint and the increase in its range of motion accelerate when the child begins to use the arm actively.
s2372 3. The elbow joint and the joints of the forearm bones
s2373 The **elbow joint** (*articulatio cubiti*) is a **compound** joint: there are three joints within one capsule. 1) The **humeroulnar joint** (*articulatio humeroulnaris*) — between the trochlea of the humerus and the trochlear notch of the ulna; **hinge (helical)**, uniaxial. 2) The **humeroradial joint** (*articulatio humeroradialis*) — between the capitulum and the fovea on the head of the radius; ball-shaped, but movement occurs only around the frontal and vertical axes. 3) The **proximal radioulnar joint** (*articulatio radioulnaris proximalis*) — between the articular circumference of the radial head and the radial notch of the ulna; **pivot**.
s2374 The capsule is thin in front and behind and is reinforced at the sides by the **ulnar collateral ligament** (*lig. collaterale ulnare*) and the **radial collateral ligament** (*lig. collaterale radiale*); the epicondyles remain outside the capsule. The head of the radius is encircled by the **anular ligament** (*lig. anulare radii*), which attaches to the edges of the radial notch of the ulna. At the elbow joint **flexion and extension** occur around the frontal axis, and at the radioulnar joints **pronation and supination** around the vertical axis.
