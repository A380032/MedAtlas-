s7159 A 65-year-old man who smokes complains of pain in his left calf after walking 200 meters, forcing him to stop (intermittent claudication). On the left side the femoral pulse is good, but there is no pulse in the popliteal, posterior tibial or dorsalis pedis arteries.
s7160 Determine which artery is narrowed and where. By what collateral routes is blood reaching the leg?
s7161 There is a pulse in the femoral artery but none in the popliteal — the narrowing is in the **distal part of the femoral artery**, most often in the **adductor (Hunter's) canal** (a typical site of atherosclerosis). Collateral routes: branches of the **deep artery of the thigh** (which arises higher up from the femoral artery) — the **perforating arteries** and the **descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery** — anastomose through the **arterial network of the knee joint** with the genicular arteries of the popliteal artery (superior lateral and medial); the descending genicular artery also takes part in this network. At rest the collaterals are sufficient, but on walking the demand of the calf muscles rises and ischemic pain appears. Treatment — stopping smoking, walking exercise, and in severe cases femoropopliteal bypass or angioplasty.
s7162 After coronary angiography a painful pulsating swelling appeared in the patient's right groin (a false aneurysm). The doctor found that the puncture site had been well above the inguinal ligament.
s7163 Where is the correct site for puncturing the femoral artery? Why is a puncture above the inguinal ligament dangerous? State the order of the structures in the femoral triangle.
s7164 The correct site is 1–2 cm below the inguinal ligament, at the **midinguinal point** (midway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the pubic symphysis), where the artery lies **over the head of the femur** — after the puncture, bleeding can be stopped by pressing the artery against the bone. Above the inguinal ligament the artery is the **external iliac artery** and lies behind the peritoneum — it cannot be pressed against bone, so the risk of **retroperitoneal hemorrhage** and false aneurysm is high; too low, there is the origin of the deep artery of the thigh or the crossing with the femoral vein, with a risk of arteriovenous fistula. In the femoral triangle, from lateral to medial: the **femoral nerve, femoral artery, femoral vein** (NAV), and medial to the vein the femoral canal (lymph nodes).
s7165 After childbirth there is heavy bleeding because the uterus fails to contract, and conservative measures are ineffective. The surgeon ligated the anterior trunks of the internal iliac arteries on both sides; the bleeding stopped, and no necrosis of the uterus or bladder was seen.
s7166 From which arteries does the uterus receive blood? After the internal iliac arteries are ligated, how do the pelvic organs remain supplied with blood?
s7167 The uterus receives blood mainly from the **uterine artery** (from the anterior trunk of the internal iliac artery; at the base of the broad ligament it crosses over the ureter and ascends along the lateral border of the uterus); it anastomoses with the **ovarian artery** (from the abdominal aorta). After ligation the blood pressure falls and the bleeding lessens, but the organs receive blood through the following collaterals: the **ovarian arteries** (from the aorta); the **superior rectal artery** (from the inferior mesenteric artery) ↔ the middle and inferior rectal arteries; the **median sacral artery** (from the aorta) ↔ the lateral sacral arteries; the **lumbar arteries** and the **deep circumflex iliac artery** ↔ the iliolumbar artery; the **inferior epigastric artery** (from the external iliac) ↔ the obturator artery; the **circumflex femoral arteries** ↔ the gluteal arteries; the branches of the two sides also join at the midline. That is why there is no necrosis.

s7196 **Emissary veins** and **diploic veins** connect the sinuses with veins outside the cranium, and the **ophthalmic veins** connect the cavernous sinus with the **facial vein** (angular vein). That is why squeezing a boil in the nasolabial triangle (the "**danger triangle**") can carry infection into the cavernous sinus and cause **cavernous sinus thrombosis** (exophthalmos, paralysis of the oculomotor nerves) and meningitis. Injury to the superior sagittal sinus causes heavy bleeding.
s7197 **Varicose veins of the leg** — due to incompetence of the valves of the great saphenous vein (especially the ostial valve where it joins the femoral vein) and of the valves of the perforating veins; complication — trophic ulcers. **Deep vein thrombosis** (prolonged bed rest, after surgery, pregnancy) is dangerous — a thrombus may break off and pass via the inferior vena cava → the right heart → causing **pulmonary embolism**. The great saphenous vein is harvested as an **autograft** for coronary artery bypass and peripheral bypass (it is reversed because of its valves). **Left varicocele** is more common: the left testicular vein drains into the left renal vein at a right angle.
s7198 In **cirrhosis of the liver** the pressure in the portal vein rises (**portal hypertension**): bleeding from esophageal varices (one of the main causes of death), hemorrhoids, "caput medusae," **splenomegaly** and **ascites** are seen. One method of treatment is **TIPS** (creating an artificial portocaval shunt within the liver between the portal vein and a hepatic vein by means of a stent).
s7199 Congenital heart defects: **patent ductus arteriosus** (shunting of blood from the aorta into the pulmonary artery, a "machinery murmur"; common in premature infants — closed with indomethacin or ibuprofen), **atrial septal defect**, **ventricular septal defect** (the most common), **tetralogy of Fallot** (pulmonary stenosis, ventricular septal defect, overriding aorta, right ventricular hypertrophy — cyanosis). A patent foramen ovale in adults may cause **paradoxical embolism** (passage of a venous thrombus to the brain).
s7200 Enlargement of the lymph nodes (**lymphadenopathy**) is seen in infections, tumor metastasis and blood diseases; to know which nodes a tumor will metastasize to, the **regional lymphatic drainage** of the organ is important: for example, breast cancer — mainly the **axillary** nodes (the first being the "sentinel node"), gastric cancer — the left supraclavicular node (**Virchow's node**, beside the point where the thoracic duct ends). When the thoracic duct is injured (mediastinal operations) lymph collects in the pleural cavity — **chylothorax**. When lymph flow is obstructed — **lymphedema** (for example, swelling of the arm after mastectomy).
s7201 Although the spleen is protected by the ribs, it **ruptures** in fractures of the lower left ribs or in abdominal trauma — heavy internal bleeding (in children a spleen-preserving operation is performed if possible). After splenectomy, especially in children, the risk of severe infection with encapsulated bacteria (pneumococcus) increases — vaccination is needed. Normally the spleen is not palpable; if it is palpable — splenomegaly (portal hypertension, blood diseases, infections). Tumors of the thymus (thymoma) may be associated with **myasthenia gravis**.
s7202 1. Veins: general principles
s7203 **Veins** carry blood from the organs to the heart. Compared with arteries their walls are **thin** (the middle muscular layer is scant), their lumen is wide and they are more numerous; ≈70 % of the blood is in the veins (**capacitance vessels**). Most medium-sized veins have folds of endothelium — **valves** (especially in the veins of the lower limb); they prevent the backflow of blood. There are no valves in the venae cavae, the portal vein, or the cerebral, renal and pulmonary veins. Veins are **superficial** (subcutaneous, without arteries) and **deep** (accompanying arteries; 2 each for medium and small arteries); they are connected by **perforating veins** and form many **venous plexuses**. Venous return is aided by the **muscle pump**, the negative pressure in the thorax (inspiration), the suction effect of the heart and the pulsation of neighboring arteries.
s7204 The veins of the systemic circulation unite into three systems: the **superior vena cava system**, the **inferior vena cava system** and the **hepatic portal vein system** (part of the inferior vena cava system); separately — the **cardiac veins** (coronary sinus). In the pulmonary circulation — the **4 pulmonary veins**.

s7207 2. The superior vena cava system
s7208 The **superior vena cava** (*v. cava superior*, 5–6 cm long, ≈2 cm in diameter) is formed by the union of the right and left **brachiocephalic veins** behind the junction of the right 1st costal cartilage with the sternum; it descends to the right of the ascending aorta and drains into the **right atrium** at the level of the 3rd costal cartilage. Before it enters the pericardium the **azygos vein** drains into it from behind. It collects blood from the head, neck, upper limbs, thoracic walls and part of the abdominal wall.
s7209 The **brachiocephalic veins** are formed on each side by the union of the **internal jugular** and **subclavian veins** behind the sternoclavicular joint (the **venous angle** — where the thoracic and right lymphatic ducts drain); the left one is longer (5–6 cm) and passes obliquely in front of the branches of the aortic arch. The **vertebral**, **internal thoracic**, **inferior thyroid** and **supreme intercostal** veins drain into them.
s7210 The **internal jugular vein** begins at the jugular foramen (the continuation of the **sigmoid sinus**; at its start is the superior bulb) and descends in the carotid sheath lateral to the common carotid artery; it has an inferior bulb. **Intracranial tributaries** — the dural sinuses (cerebral, ophthalmic and inner ear veins); **extracranial tributaries** — the **facial vein**, the **retromandibular vein**, and the **pharyngeal**, **lingual**, **superior** and **middle thyroid veins**.
