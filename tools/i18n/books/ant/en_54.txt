s6648 In the newborn the arteries of the upper limb are relatively thin but well developed relative to the size of the limb; the diameter of the subclavian artery grows rapidly from birth **to 4 years**. The length and diameter of the arteries increase 3–5 times by 12–16 years.
s6649 In children the muscles, fasciae and grooves of the arm and forearm are poorly developed — the large arteries lie **more superficially** and are easily palpated, but the risk of injury is also higher.
s6650 In the newborn the superficial palmar arch is often formed also by the **median artery** (the artery accompanying the median nerve) (in the fetus the median artery is the main artery of the forearm and hand, then it regresses); the shape of the arches varies.
s6651 In youth the arterial walls contain many elastic fibers; in the elderly the walls thicken and the vessels become tortuous (for example, the brachial artery).

s6743 An 8-year-old child fell on his arm and sustained a supracondylar fracture of the humerus. In the emergency department no pulse can be felt at the wrist, the hand is cold and pale, and passive extension of the fingers causes severe pain.
s6744 Which artery may be injured? Explain its topography and the complication. By what routes can circulation be restored?
s6745 The **brachial artery** has been injured or compressed by a bone fragment. It lies with the median nerve in the **medial bicipital groove**, distally in front of the humerus and in the cubital fossa under the biceps aponeurosis, and is close to the bone in the supracondylar region. Ischemia of the forearm flexors soon leads to **ischemic contracture (Volkmann's)** (pain on passive extension is a sign of compartment syndrome). Collateral flow is via the **arterial network of the elbow joint**: from above, the deep artery of the arm (the middle and radial collateral arteries) and the superior and inferior ulnar collateral arteries; from below, the radial, ulnar and interosseous recurrent arteries. But because the injury is in the region of the network, the collaterals may be inadequate — urgent reduction and exploration of the artery are needed.
s6746 A cardiologist intends to perform coronary angiography on a patient via the radial artery. Before the puncture he performed the Allen test: after both arteries were compressed and the ulnar artery was released, the palm stayed pale even after 15 seconds.
s6747 What is the anatomical basis of the Allen test? What does the result show, and what decision is made?
s6748 The hand receives blood from two arteries: the **ulnar artery** mainly forms the **superficial palmar arch** and the **radial artery** mainly the **deep arch**; the arches anastomose with each other (through the palmar metacarpal and common digital arteries) and through the carpal networks. The test checks whether, if the radial artery became occluded (thrombosis after puncture), the hand could receive enough blood through the ulnar artery and the arches alone. If the palm does not flush within 5–10 seconds the test is **positive (abnormal)**: the arches are incomplete (for example, the superficial arch is not closed) or the ulnar artery is diseased. In this patient radial access is dangerous, and access through the femoral artery (transfemoral) is chosen.
s6749 A patient with a stab wound in the axillary region is bleeding heavily from an artery. The surgeon had to ligate the axillary artery below the origin of the subscapular artery. After the operation the blood supply of the arm is unsatisfactory.
s6750 Why is ligation at exactly this site dangerous? In which region is ligation of the axillary artery relatively safe, and why?
s6751 The main collateral route of the axillary artery is the **scapular arterial anastomosis**: branches of the subclavian artery (the **suprascapular artery** from the thyrocervical trunk, the **dorsal scapular artery**) connect with branches of the **subscapular artery** arising from part III of the axillary artery (the **circumflex scapular** and thoracodorsal arteries); by this route blood flows backward into the subscapular artery and returns to the distal part of the axillary artery. So if the artery is ligated **above the subscapular artery** (in parts I–II), collateral flow is preserved. When it is ligated **below** the subscapular artery this route does not work — the remaining collaterals (the anastomosis between the circumflex humeral arteries and the deep artery of the arm) are few, so the risk of ischemia of the arm is high; repair of the artery (suture or a graft) is preferable.

s6778 **Pleural puncture** is performed along the **upper border** of the rib, so as not to injure the main intercostal neurovascular bundle in the costal groove (posteriorly, in the scapular line — the 7th–8th intercostal space). In coarctation of the aorta the dilated intercostal arteries erode the lower borders of the ribs and produce "rib notching" on radiographs.
s6779 A **gastric ulcer** on the lesser curvature may erode the left gastric artery, and an **ulcer on the posterior wall of the duodenal bulb** may erode the **gastroduodenal artery** passing behind it, causing heavy bleeding. In **cholecystectomy** the cystic artery is found in **Calot's triangle** (between the cystic duct, the common hepatic duct and the inferior surface of the liver) and ligated; variants of the hepatic arteries (for example, a right hepatic artery arising from the superior mesenteric artery) are common — care is needed. In liver operations blood flow can be stopped temporarily by compressing the hepatoduodenal ligament between the fingers (the **Pringle maneuver**).
s6780 **Acute thrombosis or embolism of the superior mesenteric artery** (often in atrial fibrillation) leads to necrosis of almost the whole small intestine and the right half of the colon — the risk of death is very high; at the onset there is severe pain while the abdomen remains soft. In the acute angle between the superior mesenteric artery and the aorta the **left renal vein** (**"nutcracker" syndrome** — hematuria, left varicocele) or the **horizontal part of the duodenum** may be compressed. In appendectomy the **appendicular artery** is ligated in the mesoappendix.
s6781 An **abdominal aortic aneurysm** usually forms below the renal arteries (infrarenal); it is palpated as a pulsating mass in the umbilical region; if it ruptures — retroperitoneal bleeding. When the aorta is clamped below the renal arteries, the blood supply of the inferior mesenteric artery territory is maintained through the arc of Riolan and the rectal arteries. Renal artery stenosis (atherosclerosis, fibromuscular dysplasia) leads to **renovascular hypertension**.
