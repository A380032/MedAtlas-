s2151 The articular processes of adjacent vertebrae form the **zygapophysial (facet) joints** (*articulationes zygapophysiales*) — these are plane joints; their surfaces are oriented differently in each region (see topic 1) and determine the direction of movement. The 5th lumbar vertebra forms the **lumbosacral joint** with the sacrum, and the sacrum forms the **sacrococcygeal joint** with the coccyx.

s2154 Joints of the vertebral column with the skull
s2155 The **atlanto-occipital joint** (*articulatio atlantooccipitalis*) lies between the occipital condyles and the superior articular facets of the atlas; it is a paired, **ellipsoid**, combined joint: around the frontal axis the head can be nodded forward and backward (the "yes" movement), and around the sagittal axis tilted slightly to the side. It is reinforced by the **anterior and posterior atlanto-occipital membranes**.
s2156 **Atlanto-axial joints**: the unpaired **median** joint — between the dens of the axis and the anterior arch of the atlas and the **transverse ligament of the atlas** — is a **pivot** joint; the paired **lateral** joints — between the inferior articular facets of the atlas and the superior facets of the axis — are plane joints. Together they allow the head, with the atlas, to turn right and left around the vertical axis (the "no" movement, ≈30–40° to each side). The dens is held by the **cruciform ligament** (transverse ligament + longitudinal bands), the **apical ligament of the dens** and the **alar ligaments**; they are covered from behind by the **tectorial membrane** (continuation of the posterior longitudinal ligament).

s2161 The vertebral column as a whole
s2162 In adults the vertebral column is not straight; in the sagittal plane it forms four **physiological curvatures**: convex forward — the **lordoses** (cervical and lumbar) and convex backward — the **kyphoses** (thoracic and sacral). The curvatures are related to upright walking: like a spring they soften shocks during walking and jumping and help maintain balance. The vertebral column performs flexion and extension (most in the cervical and lumbar regions), lateral bending, rotation (in the cervical and thoracic regions) and circumduction.

s2180 Development of muscles and ligaments
s2181 **Age-related features.** In the newborn the vertebral column is almost straight (only a mild general kyphosis), ≈20 cm long, with a relatively long cervical part and short thoracic and lumbar parts. The discs are thick — making up **45–48%** of the length of the column (≈25–30% in adults) — and rich in blood vessels; the ligaments are poorly developed and mature by 12 years. Up to 20 years the height of the discs gradually decreases and the anulus fibrosus thickens. In old age the vertebral column shortens by up to 5 cm because of thinning of the discs and increased curvatures.

s2184 4. Joints of the thoracic cage
s2185 The ribs form two joints with the thoracic vertebrae. The **joint of the head of the rib** (*articulatio capitis costae*) lies between the head of the rib and the costal facets of two adjacent vertebrae; in ribs II–X there is an **intra-articular ligament** inside the joint running from the crest of the head to the disc; the capsule is reinforced by the **radiate ligament**. The **costotransverse joint** (*articulatio costotransversaria*) lies between the tubercle of the rib and the costal facet of the transverse process (absent in ribs XI–XII). The two joints together act as a **combined** joint that raises and lowers the rib around the axis of the rib neck — the basis of the respiratory movements.

s2188 Anteriorly the ribs join the sternum: the cartilage of the **1st rib** forms a **synchondrosis** with the manubrium; the cartilages of **ribs II–VII** form the **sternocostal joints** (*articulationes sternocostales*) with the costal notches of the sternum — these are plane joints whose capsule is formed by the perichondrium, reinforced by the anterior and posterior **radiate sternocostal ligaments**; the ligaments blend with the periosteum of the sternum to form the **sternal membrane**. The cartilages of ribs VIII–X join one another by **interchondral joints**. s2191 5. Joints of the skull bones and the temporomandibular joint
s2192 The bones of the calvaria are joined mainly by **sutures**: **serrate** (sagittal, coronal, lambdoid), **squamous** (between the temporal squama and the parietal bone) and **plane** (between facial bones, for example between the nasal bones). Between the bones of the cranial base there are **synchondroses**: permanent — petro-occipital, sphenopetrosal (cartilage in the region of the foramen lacerum); temporary — spheno-occipital, between the parts of the sphenoid bone, and others. In the newborn the bones of the calvaria do not form sutures — between them there are connective tissue membranes and fontanelles; the spaces between the bones of the cranial base are filled with cartilage. The sutures gradually ossify in old age (synostosis). Except for the mandible and hyoid bone, the skull bones are joined immovably.
s2193 The **temporomandibular joint** (*articulatio temporomandibularis*) is a paired, **combined** (the joints of both sides move together), **complex** (contains a disc) joint, **ellipsoid (condylar)** in shape. It is formed by the head of the mandible and the **mandibular fossa** and **articular tubercle** of the temporal bone; the articular surfaces are covered with fibrocartilage. Inside the joint lies the biconcave fibrocartilaginous **articular disc** (*discus articularis*) — fused at its edges to the capsule, it divides the joint cavity into **upper and lower compartments**; part of the tendon of the lateral pterygoid muscle attaches to the disc.

s2213 **Age-related features of joints.** In the newborn all parts of the joints are anatomically formed, but the articular ends are cartilaginous, and ossification centers appear in them from 1–2 years up to adolescence. The joint capsule is tight and the ligaments are poorly developed; the folds and villi of the synovial membrane increase after birth and become more complex at 6–10 years. Joint growth accelerates at 2–3 years, when the child begins to move actively; full maturation of the joints is completed at **21–25 years**. In children the joints are more mobile and the ligaments more elastic.
