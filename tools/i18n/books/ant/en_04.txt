s0428 To avoid mistakes when counting ribs on an X-ray, count them by their **posterior parts**, starting next to the spine: the posterior parts are horizontal and distinct, while the anterior parts are oblique and their cartilaginous portion is not visible. The shadows of the scapulae fall on the upper lateral part of the lung fields — do not interpret them as changes in the lung.
s0429 Because a newborn's ribs are horizontal, the thoracic cage expands little during inspiration — infants breathe mainly **with the diaphragm (abdominal type)**. That is why abdominal distension (meteorism) or tight swaddling makes breathing difficult for an infant. Once the child begins to walk upright, the ribs slope downward and thoracic-type breathing is added.
s0430 1. Ribs: general information and classification
s0431 The **ribs** (*costae*) are 12 pairs of flat, long, arched spongy bones. Each rib consists of two parts: a long posterior **bony part** (*os costale*) and a short anterior **costal cartilage** (*cartilago costalis*). Posteriorly the ribs articulate with the thoracic vertebrae, and anteriorly they attach to the sternum through their cartilages or end freely.

s0444 2. Structure of a rib
s0445 At the posterior end of the rib lies the **head of the rib** (*caput costae*); its **articular surface** (*facies articularis capitis costae*) articulates with the costal facets on the bodies of the thoracic vertebrae. Because the heads of ribs II–X articulate with two adjacent vertebrae, their articular surface is divided in two by the **crest of the head** (*crista capitis costae*); ribs I, XI and XII lack this crest.
s0446 The head is followed by the narrowed **neck of the rib** (*collum costae*), whose upper border bears the **crest of the neck** (*crista colli costae*). At the junction of the neck with the body lies the **tubercle of the rib** (*tuberculum costae*); its articular surface (*facies articularis tuberculi costae*) articulates with the costal facet of the transverse process (ribs XI and XII lack an articular surface on the tubercle).
s0447 The longest part — the **body (shaft) of the rib** (*corpus costae*) — is flat and has external and internal surfaces and superior and inferior borders. Slightly lateral to the tubercle the body bends sharply to form the **angle of the rib** (*angulus costae*): in ribs I–II the angle almost coincides with the tubercle, and in the lower ribs the distance between them increases. Along the inferior border of the inner surface of the body runs the **costal groove** (*sulcus costae*), which contains the intercostal vein, artery and nerve.

s0450 Features of individual ribs
s0451 The **I rib** (*costa prima*) is the shortest, widest and most curved; instead of external and internal surfaces it has **superior and inferior surfaces** and medial and lateral borders. Its superior surface bears the **tubercle for the anterior scalene muscle** (*tuberculum musculi scaleni anterioris*); behind it runs the **groove for the subclavian artery** (*sulcus arteriae subclaviae*), and in front of it the **groove for the subclavian vein** (*sulcus venae subclaviae*). Its head has no crest.
s0452 The **II rib** (*costa secunda*) bears the **tuberosity for the serratus anterior muscle** (*tuberositas musculi serrati anterioris*) on its outer surface.
s0453 **Ribs XI and XII** have no crest on the head and no articular surface on the tubercle; their angle and groove are poorly developed and their anterior end is free. Rib XII is shorter than rib XI.

s0456 3. The sternum
s0457 The **sternum** (*sternum*) is a long flat (spongy) bone located in the anterior wall of the thoracic cage in the frontal plane. It consists of three parts: above — the **manubrium** (*manubrium sterni*), in the middle — the **body of the sternum** (*corpus sterni*), and below — the **xiphoid process** (*processus xiphoideus*).
s0458 The **manubrium** is the widest and thickest part of the bone. Its upper border bears the **jugular notch** (*incisura jugularis*); on either side are the **clavicular notches** (*incisurae claviculares*), which articulate with the clavicle, and below them are notches for the cartilage of the I rib. Where the manubrium joins the body, the anteriorly projecting **sternal angle** (*angulus sterni*, angle of Louis) is formed; the cartilage of the **II rib** attaches on each side of it.
s0459 The **body of the sternum** is its longest part and widens downward; its anterior surface shows transverse lines left by the fusion of its ossification segments (sternebrae). Its lateral borders bear **costal notches** (*incisurae costales*) for ribs III–VII; the notch for the VII rib lies at the junction of the body and the xiphoid process. The **xiphoid process** is the smallest part and variable in shape: its tip may be bifid or perforated, and it remains cartilaginous for a long time.

s0464 4. The thoracic cage
s0465 The **thoracic cage** (*cavea thoracis, compages thoracis*) is formed by the union of the 12 thoracic vertebrae, 12 pairs of ribs and the sternum. It protects the organs of the thoracic cavity — the heart, lungs, esophagus and great vessels — and takes an active part in respiratory movements. In humans the thoracic cage has the shape of a **truncated cone** flattened from front to back: its transverse diameter is greater than its anteroposterior diameter (associated with upright walking; in quadrupeds it is the reverse).

s0500 5. Radiological anatomy
s0501 On an anteroposterior X-ray the bones of the thoracic cage visible are mainly the ribs; the sternum is covered by the shadow of the heart and great vessels and can only be seen in the lateral projection. The **posterior parts** of the ribs run almost horizontally lateral to the spine and are seen more clearly; the **anterior parts** start from the lateral edge of the thoracic cage and slope medially and downward. The shadows of the anterior and posterior parts intersect and divide the lung field into quadrangular “cells.” The costal cartilages are not visible on X-ray (they are visible if calcified in the elderly).

s0504 6. Age-related features
s0505 In the newborn the ratio of the bony and cartilaginous parts of the rib is similar to that in the adult, but the **head and tubercle are cartilaginous**; the body is rounded, and the angle and groove of the rib are poorly defined. A newborn has 7 pairs of true, 2 pairs of false and 3 pairs of floating ribs. In early childhood the ribs grow rapidly, their anterior part flattens and turns downward, and the angle becomes distinct. In the second period of childhood secondary ossification centers appear in the head and tubercle and fuse with the body at **18–25 years**; during this period the cartilage of rib X joins the costal arch.
s0506 The sternum
s0507 In the newborn there is one ossification center in the manubrium and 4–5 in the body (sometimes one also at the base of the xiphoid process); they are separated by layers of cartilage. The parts of the body (sternebrae) fuse with each other **at puberty**, forming a single body. The xiphoid process ossifies and fuses with the body **at ≈30 years**; the manubrium and the body fuse very late — **after 40 years** — or remain separate for life.

s0522 Practical skill: studying a rib and the sternum
s0523 Place the rib in the anatomical position: the head posterior and medial, the groove on the **lower** border of the inner surface, the convex outer surface facing laterally. From this, determine whether it is a right or left rib.
s0524 Show the parts of the rib in sequence: *caput, crista capitis, collum, crista colli, tuberculum, angulus, corpus, sulcus costae*.
s0525 Distinguish the I rib from the others: the shortest and widest, with superior and inferior surfaces; show the tubercle and the two grooves on its superior surface.
s0526 On the sternum, show the manubrium, body, xiphoid process, jugular and clavicular notches, costal notches and the sternal angle.
s0527 On the skeleton, show the superior and inferior apertures of the thoracic cage, the costal arch and the infrasternal angle, and determine its shape.
s0528 Practical skill: counting ribs in a living person
s0529 Run your finger down from the jugular notch and find the transverse ridge — the **sternal angle**.
s0530 Move the finger laterally — this is the **II rib**; the depression below it is the II intercostal space. Count downward in this way (in women counting below the breast is harder — continue along the midaxillary line).
s0531 Posterior landmark: with the arms hanging down, the **inferior angle of the scapula** lies at the level of the VII rib (or the VII intercostal space); the XII rib can be found by palpating from lateral to medial.
s0532 Measure the infrasternal angle: place both thumbs along the costal arches and estimate the angle between them (acute, right or obtuse) — this is used to determine the body build.
