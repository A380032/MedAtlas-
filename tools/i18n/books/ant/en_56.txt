s6866 The origin of the abdominal aorta is at the level of T~XI~ in the newborn; in the second period of childhood it descends to the middle of T~XII~, and in adolescence to the lower 1/3 of T~XII~. In the newborn the abdominal aorta is ≈5 cm long, with a diameter of 5.5–6.8 mm.
s6867 The diameter of the **superior mesenteric artery** is 2.5–3 mm in the newborn; it increases 2 times by the end of early childhood and increases 4.4 times by the end of the first period of childhood. The **inferior mesenteric artery** in the newborn has a diameter of 1.5–2 mm and a length of 1–2 cm.
s6868 The superior suprarenal artery in the newborn is ≈0.9 mm wide and 2.4 cm long; at 20 years, 1.5 mm and 3.26 cm. The arteries of the internal organs change in step with the growth and changes in position of the organs (for example, the testicular artery lengthens as the testis descends into the scrotum).
s6869 In children the density of the intraorgan vessels is higher than in adults; in the first year the intraorgan vessels mature rapidly. In the elderly the aorta and its branches undergo atherosclerosis, become dilated and tortuous.

s6951 A 55-year-old man has had a duodenal ulcer for 10 years. Today he passed black stools (melena) and vomited coffee-ground material; his blood pressure is 85/50 mmHg. Endoscopy shows a deep bleeding ulcer on the posterior wall of the bulb.
s6952 Which artery is bleeding? State its origin and topography. What anastomoses does this artery have?
s6953 The source of bleeding is the **gastroduodenal artery**. It arises from the **common hepatic artery** (a branch of the celiac trunk), descends **behind** the superior part (bulb) of the duodenum and divides into the **right gastro-omental artery** and the **anterior and posterior superior pancreaticoduodenal arteries**. A deep ulcer on the posterior wall of the bulb erodes the arterial wall directly. Its anastomoses: the superior pancreaticoduodenal arteries with the inferior ones arising from the **superior mesenteric artery**; the right gastro-omental artery with the left one arising from the splenic artery (the arch of the greater curvature). So when the ulcer is sutured, the artery must be ligated above and below the ulcer — otherwise blood arrives from both directions.
s6954 A 72-year-old patient has atrial fibrillation. Suddenly unbearable pain developed around the umbilicus; on examination the abdomen is soft and the pain is out of proportion to the physical findings. After 6 hours signs of peritoneal irritation and bloody stools appeared.
s6955 Embolism of which artery is suspected? State the area supplied by this artery and explain the collateral routes.
s6956 **Embolism of the superior mesenteric artery** (mesenteric ischemia). The artery arises from the aorta at the level of L~I~ at an acute angle and runs almost parallel to the aorta, so emboli lodge in it easily. It supplies the lower half of the duodenum, the head of the pancreas, **the whole jejunum and ileum**, the cecum and appendix, and the ascending colon and right 2/3 of the transverse colon. Collaterals: from above — branches of the **celiac trunk** (superior pancreaticoduodenal arteries ↔ inferior ones); from below — the **inferior mesenteric artery** (left colic artery ↔ middle colic artery, the **arc of Riolan** and the marginal artery). In acute occlusion these routes are inadequate, so intestinal necrosis develops; urgent embolectomy is needed.
s6957 During a laparoscopic cholecystectomy the surgeon noticed that the vessel he was about to ligate as the cystic artery was in fact a larger artery running to the porta hepatis.
s6958 Which anatomical landmark is used to find the cystic artery? Which variants of the hepatic artery are dangerous?
s6959 The cystic artery usually arises from the **right branch of the proper hepatic artery** and is found in **Calot's triangle** — the area bounded by the cystic duct (below), the common hepatic duct (medially) and the inferior surface of the liver (above). The surgeon must not divide anything until the "critical view of safety" is established (only two structures — the cystic duct and the cystic artery — entering the gallbladder in the triangle). Dangerous variants: the **right hepatic artery** passing in front of the common hepatic duct or forming a loop in Calot's triangle ("Moynihan's hump") — it may be ligated in mistake for the cystic artery; a right hepatic artery arising from the **superior mesenteric artery** (≈10–15 %); an accessory left hepatic artery arising from the **left gastric artery**; two cystic arteries. If the right hepatic artery is ligated, ischemia of the right lobe of the liver may develop.

s6986 In **hysterectomy**, when the uterine artery is ligated there is a risk of injuring the **ureter**, which crosses below and lateral to it (≈1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix). In **severe postpartum hemorrhage** the uterine arteries or the internal iliac arteries are ligated or **embolized** — because the pelvic organs have many anastomoses through the branches of both sides and the ovarian and rectal arteries, necrosis is rare. **Aberrant origin of the obturator artery** (from the inferior epigastric artery, ≈25–30 %) — the "**corona mortis**" (crown of death): it may be injured when the lacunar ligament is divided in a femoral hernia operation, causing heavy bleeding.
s6987 The inferior epigastric artery is the landmark for distinguishing inguinal hernias: in an **indirect (lateral) inguinal hernia** the hernial sac emerges **lateral** to the artery (through the deep inguinal ring), and in a **direct (medial) hernia** — **medial** to the artery (through Hesselbach's triangle).
s6988 The pulse of the **femoral artery** is felt slightly medial to the middle of the inguinal ligament (midway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the pubic symphysis — the **midinguinal point**); in bleeding it is pressed here against the **pubis**. This site is the main access for **angiography** and **coronary angiography** (Seldinger technique): the artery is punctured below the inguinal ligament, over the head of the femur. The femoral vein lies medial to it — the site for central venous catheterization. Injury to the **medial circumflex femoral artery** (fracture of the femoral neck) leads to **avascular necrosis** of the head of the femur.
s6989 Because the **popliteal artery** lies close to the bone, it is injured in **knee dislocation** and distal femoral fractures; its pulse is palpated deeply with the knee flexed. **Popliteal aneurysm** is the most common peripheral aneurysm. If the popliteal artery is ligated, collateral flow through the genicular network is poorer than when the femoral artery is ligated (the risk of gangrene is high).
s6990 In **peripheral arterial disease** (atherosclerosis, diabetes mellitus) the degree of ischemia of the foot is assessed by taking the pulse of the **dorsalis pedis artery** (lateral to the tendon of the extensor hallucis longus, below the midpoint of the line between the malleoli) and the **posterior tibial artery** (midway between the medial malleolus and the Achilles tendon); the ankle-brachial index is measured. In ≈10 % of people the dorsalis pedis artery cannot be palpated or arises from the perforating branch of the fibular artery. In **compartment syndrome of the leg** (fracture, crushing) the pressure in the fascial compartments rises and the anterior tibial artery and nerve are compressed — fasciotomy is needed.
s6991 1. The common iliac artery
s6992 The **common iliac arteries** (*aa. iliacae communes*) begin at the bifurcation of the abdominal aorta at the level of **L~IV~**, run downward and laterally (5–7 cm long) and in front of the **sacroiliac joint** divide into the **internal** and **external iliac arteries**. The **ureter** crosses in front of the point of division. The common iliac arteries give off no large branches (only small branches to the peritoneum, the ureter and the psoas muscle).

s6995 2. The internal iliac artery
s6996 The **internal iliac artery** (*a. iliaca interna*) descends from the level of the sacroiliac joint into the **lesser pelvis** and at the upper border of the greater sciatic foramen usually divides into **anterior** and **posterior trunks**. It supplies the pelvic walls, the pelvic organs, the external genitalia, the perineum, the buttock and the medial group of thigh muscles.
