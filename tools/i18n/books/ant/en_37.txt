s4607 The **liver** appears in the 3rd–4th week of fetal life as an endodermal outgrowth from the ventral wall of the foregut (the future duodenum) — the **hepatic diverticulum**. From its cranial part develop the **hepatic cords** and hepatic ducts, from its caudal part the **gallbladder** and cystic duct, and from the base of the common outgrowth the **common bile duct**. The liver bud grows into the mesenchyme of the ventral mesentery and the septum transversum; the ventral mesentery remains as the **lesser omentum** and the **falciform ligament**. The hepatic cords surround the vitelline and umbilical veins, and sinusoids form. Because in the fetus the liver is a **hematopoietic organ** (from the 6th week), it is very large (≈10% of body weight at 3 months).
s4608 The **pancreas** develops in the 4th week from the **dorsal** and **ventral** buds of the duodenum. Owing to rotation of the duodenum, the ventral bud moves backward and fuses with the dorsal bud: the ventral bud forms the lower part of the head and the uncinate process, and the dorsal bud forms the upper part of the head, the body and the tail. The main duct forms from the duct of the ventral bud and the distal part of the dorsal duct, and the accessory duct from the proximal part of the dorsal duct. If the buds fail to fuse completely, a gland with two ducts develops (pancreas divisum); if the ventral bud rotates abnormally, an **annular pancreas** encircling the duodenum develops (a cause of intestinal obstruction).
s4609 In the newborn the liver is **relatively very large**: it weighs ≈135–150 g (**4–4.5%** of body weight, vs. 2–3% in adults) and occupies the upper half of the abdominal cavity; its four lobes are clearly visible; it is full of blood and soft, and the boundaries of the lobules are indistinct.
s4610 In the newborn the **left lobe** is equal to or larger than the right — in the fetus the left lobe received the oxygen-rich blood of the umbilical vein. After birth, because the circulation changes, the growth of the left lobe slows.
s4611 In the newborn the inferior border of the liver lies **2.5–4 cm** below the costal margin in the right midclavicular line and 3.5–4 cm below the xiphoid process in the midline; in early childhood it projects 1.5–2 cm below the margin; **after 7 years** it hides behind the costal margin. Palpation of the liver in children is a normal finding.
s4612 The mass of the liver increases 2 times by 1 year, 3 times by 2–3 years, increases 6 times by 9 years and 10 times by puberty; its maximum mass is reached at 21–30 years. The boundaries of the lobules become distinct at 1 year, and their structure resembles that of adults by 8 years.
s4613 In the newborn the gallbladder is cylindrical or fusiform, ≈3 cm long, deeply embedded in the liver tissue; at 6–7 months it becomes pear-shaped and at 2 years reaches the border of the liver; by 13–14 years it resembles that of adults. Its capacity is ≈3 mL at 3 months, ≈8.5 mL at 3 years and ≈34 mL at 9 years.
s4614 In the newborn the pancreas is very small — it weighs **2–3 g**, is 4–6 cm long and lies at the level of T~XII~ (higher); it is mobile because there is no retroperitoneal fat. There is much connective tissue and little glandular tissue; most of the gland is the head. Its mass increases 2 times by 6 months, is ≈10 g at 1 year, ≈20 g at 3 years, 30–35 g at 10 years and reaches 65–90 g by 14–16 years.

s4705 In a man injured in a road traffic accident, severe bleeding into the abdominal cavity was found; at operation a tear of the liver was discovered. To stop the bleeding temporarily, the surgeon inserted his fingers into the omental (epiploic) foramen and compressed the hepatoduodenal ligament (the Pringle maneuver).
s4706 Which structures lie in this ligament, and how are they arranged? Why does compressing the ligament reduce, but not completely stop, bleeding from the liver?
s4707 In the **hepatoduodenal ligament** (the right part of the lesser omentum; the anterior wall of the omental foramen) lie the structures carrying blood to the liver: **on the right — the common bile duct**, **on the left — the proper hepatic artery**, **behind — the portal vein**. When the ligament is compressed, **both** inflows of blood to the liver — the portal vein (≈75%) and the hepatic artery (≈25%) — stop. Yet the bleeding does not stop completely, because the **hepatic veins**, which carry blood away from the liver, do not pass through the porta but drain directly from the posterior surface of the liver into the **inferior vena cava** and are not compressed.
s4708 An overweight 45-year-old woman presented with severe colicky pain below the right costal margin and nausea after a fatty meal. Two days later her skin and the whites of her eyes turned yellow, her urine darkened and her stools became pale. Ultrasound showed stones in the gallbladder and dilation of the common bile duct.
s4709 By what route does bile flow? Where may the stone be lodged, and why did jaundice appear? What complication is a risk?
s4710 Bile is formed in the liver: right and left hepatic ducts → **common hepatic duct** → (collecting in the gallbladder via the cystic duct and returning) → **common bile duct** → **hepatopancreatic ampulla** → **major papilla** (sphincter of Oddi) → duodenum. Jaundice and dilation of the common bile duct indicate that the stone is lodged in the **common bile duct** or at the **major papilla** (choledocholithiasis): bile does not reach the intestine (pale stools), and bilirubin returns to the blood (yellow skin, dark urine). If the stone lodges in the ampulla, bile may reflux into the pancreatic duct through the common channel, and **acute pancreatitis** may develop.

s4743 Intraperitoneal organs cannot be reached surgically **without opening the peritoneal cavity**; extraperitoneal organs (kidney, ureter), by contrast, can be approached through the lumbar region without opening the peritoneal cavity — this reduces the risk of peritoneal infection (peritonitis).
s4744 The **greater omentum** is the "policeman of the abdomen": being mobile, it adheres to a focus of inflammation (for example, an inflamed appendix or a perforated ulcer) and walls off the process (forming an inflammatory mass). In children the greater omentum is short and thin, so this protection is weak — appendicitis rapidly progresses to **generalized peritonitis**. In surgery the omentum is used as "natural material" to close defects.
s4745 In bleeding from the liver, the surgeon inserts the index finger into the **omental foramen** and compresses the hepatoduodenal ligament with the thumb (the **Pringle maneuver**) — the vessels bringing blood to the liver are temporarily occluded. When an **ulcer of the posterior wall of the stomach** perforates, the gastric contents pour into the omental bursa and may remain confined there for a time; pancreatic pseudocysts and fluid in acute pancreatitis also collect in the omental bursa.
s4746 Peritoneal recesses are sites where **internal hernias** can form: if an intestinal loop enters a recess and becomes trapped, intestinal obstruction occurs (most often at the duodenojejunal flexure — **Treitz's hernia**). In a supine patient, fluid in the abdominal cavity collects in the lowest places — the **hepatorenal recess (Morison's pouch)** and the pelvis; on ultrasound fluid is looked for here first (the FAST examination).
s4747 Blood from the abdominal cavity (in a ruptured ectopic pregnancy) and pus (from appendicitis or adnexitis) collect in the **pouch of Douglas**; it is **punctured** through the posterior fornix of the vagina (culdocentesis) or examined digitally through the rectum (bulging and tenderness). In men the rectovesical pouch is examined through the rectum.
s4748 1. The peritoneum and the peritoneal cavity
s4749 The **peritoneum** (*peritoneum*) is the serous membrane lining the walls of the abdominal cavity and covering the organs within it. Its total area is **≈1.7 m²**; its surface is covered by **mesothelium**, beneath which lie a basement membrane and connective tissue layers. The peritoneum consists of two layers: the **parietal peritoneum** (*peritoneum parietale*), lining the abdominal walls, and the **visceral peritoneum** (*peritoneum viscerale*), covering the organs; they are continuous with each other. Between the parietal layer and the abdominal wall lies the **subperitoneal (subserous) layer** — fatty tissue; on the posterior wall it is well developed and fills the **retroperitoneal space** (*spatium retroperitoneale*) — where the kidneys, suprarenal glands, ureters, aorta, inferior vena cava and other structures lie.
s4750 The narrow slit between the layers is the **peritoneal cavity** (*cavitas peritonealis*); it contains a small amount (20–30 mL) of serous fluid that allows the organs to glide. In males the cavity is closed; in females it communicates through the openings of the uterine tubes with the uterus and through the vagina with the exterior. The peritoneum **produces and absorbs serous fluid** (its diaphragmatic part is especially active in absorption), **fixes** the organs, and performs protective (phagocytosis, walling off inflammation by forming adhesions) and **fat-storage** functions.
