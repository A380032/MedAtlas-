s5870 A **pituitary adenoma** growing upward compresses the **optic chiasm** — loss of the temporal visual fields on both sides (**bitemporal hemianopia**) is observed; on radiographs the sella turcica is enlarged. Excess **growth hormone** causes **gigantism** in children and, in adults (after the growth plates have closed), **acromegaly** (enlargement of the facial bones, hands and feet); deficiency causes **pituitary dwarfism**. Deficiency of **vasopressin** causes **diabetes insipidus** (5–20 L of urine per day). The pituitary is reached by the **transsphenoidal** route — through the nasal cavity and sphenoidal sinus.
s5871 Tumors of the pineal gland (pinealomas) in children may present with **precocious puberty** owing to loss of its inhibitory effect; by compressing the tectal (quadrigeminal) plate they impair upward gaze (Parinaud's syndrome) and, by compressing the cerebral aqueduct, lead to **hydrocephalus**.
s5872 Enlargement of the thyroid gland is **goiter** (struma); endemic goiter is common in iodine-deficient regions. Congenital **hypothyroidism** causes **cretinism** (retarded mental and physical development; hence newborns are screened), and in adults **myxedema**. **Hyperthyroidism** (Graves' / Basedow's disease) causes tachycardia, weight loss, irritability and protrusion of the eyes (exophthalmos). In **thyroidectomy** the recurrent laryngeal nerve (beside the inferior thyroid artery) and the parathyroid glands must not be injured; when the superior thyroid artery is ligated, the **external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve**, which lies close to it, may be damaged.
s5873 If the parathyroid glands are accidentally removed or their blood supply is disrupted during thyroidectomy, blood calcium falls sharply and **tetany** develops — painful muscle spasms (the "obstetrician's hand" position of the hand, laryngospasm); this is life-threatening. In parathyroid adenoma, by contrast, hypercalcemia, softening of the bones and kidney stones are observed.
s5874 Insufficiency of the suprarenal cortex is **Addison's disease** (weakness, low blood pressure, bronzing of the skin — owing to increased ACTH). Excess cortisol causes **Itsenko–Cushing syndrome** (obesity of the face and trunk, "moon face," hypertension, diabetes mellitus, red striae on the skin). A tumor of the medulla is a **pheochromocytoma** (paroxysmal severe hypertension). The right suprarenal vein is very short and drains directly into the inferior vena cava — the risk of bleeding in adrenalectomy is high.
s5875 1. The endocrine glands: general information
s5876 **Endocrine glands (glands of internal secretion)** have no ducts and release the biologically active substances they produce — **hormones** — directly into the blood or lymph. Hormones act via the blood on distant **target organs**, regulating metabolism, growth, development, reproduction and adaptation; together with the nervous system they provide **neurohumoral regulation**. General features of the endocrine glands: **absence of ducts**, **rich blood supply** (the organs receiving the most blood per unit mass), **fenestrated (sinusoidal) capillaries**, small size and **close connection with the nervous system**.

s5890 2. The hypothalamic-pituitary system
s5891 The **pituitary gland** (*hypophysis, glandula pituitaria*) is the central endocrine gland: it regulates the activity of many peripheral glands. It is bean-shaped, ≈10–17 × 5–15 × 5–10 mm, and weighs **≈0.5–0.6 g** (larger in women, especially in pregnancy). It lies in the **hypophysial fossa of the sella turcica** of the sphenoid bone; above it is covered by the **diaphragma sellae** of the dura mater, and through its opening the **infundibulum** connects it with the tuber cinereum of the hypothalamus. At its sides lie the **cavernous venous sinuses** (internal carotid artery, nerves III, IV, V~1~, V~2~, VI), and above and in front the **optic chiasm**.

s5902 The **hypothalamus** controls the pituitary by two routes: 1) the **hypothalamohypophysial tract** — the axons of the magnocellular nuclei carry hormones to the neurohypophysis; 2) the **hypophysial portal system** — the parvocellular neurosecretory nuclei release **releasing hormones (liberins)** and **inhibiting hormones (statins)** into the **primary capillary network** in the infundibulum; via the **hypophysial portal veins** they reach the **secondary capillary network** in the adenohypophysis and stimulate or inhibit hormone secretion. The pituitary is supplied by the **superior and inferior hypophysial arteries** (from the internal carotid artery).

s5905 3. The pineal gland
s5906 The **pineal gland** (*glandula pinealis, corpus pineale*) is a small gland resembling a pine cone (8–15 mm long, weighing ≈0.2 g), belonging to the epithalamus of the diencephalon. It lies in the groove between the superior colliculi of the tectal plate and is attached to the thalami by the **habenulae**. Its parenchyma consists of **pinealocytes** and glial cells; with age calcium salts accumulate in it, forming **brain sand** (a midline landmark on radiographs). Depending on light (retina → hypothalamus → sympathetic pathway), the pineal gland produces **melatonin** — it regulates **circadian rhythms** (sleep-wakefulness) and in childhood **inhibits sexual maturation**; it also secretes serotonin and other peptides.

s5909 4. The thyroid gland
s5910 The **thyroid gland** (*glandula thyroidea*) is the largest endocrine gland (weighing **20–40 g**, larger in women); it lies in the anterior region of the neck, in front of and beside the larynx and trachea. It consists of **right and left lobes** and the **isthmus** joining them (over tracheal rings II–IV); in ≈30–50% of people there is a **pyramidal lobe** extending upward from the isthmus (a remnant of the thyroglossal duct). The upper poles of the lobes reach the laminae of the thyroid cartilage, and the lower poles the 5th–6th tracheal rings.
s5911 The gland is enclosed in a **fibrous capsule**, from which septa pass inward, dividing it into lobules; externally the pretracheal layer of the cervical fascia forms a **fascial sheath** (the gland is attached to the larynx by ligaments, which is why it **rises with the larynx during swallowing**). The structural-functional unit of the gland is the **follicle**: a vesicle lined by a single layer of **thyrocytes** and filled with **colloid** (thyroglobulin). The thyrocytes produce the iodine-containing **thyroxine (T~4~)** and **triiodothyronine (T~3~)**, and the **parafollicular (C) cells** between the follicles produce **calcitonin** (lowers blood calcium).

s5914 **Topography**: in front — the infrahyoid muscles (sternohyoid, sternothyroid, omohyoid), the cervical fasciae and skin; posteromedially — the larynx, trachea, pharynx and esophagus, with the **recurrent laryngeal nerve** in the groove between them; laterally — the **common carotid artery**, internal jugular vein and vagus nerve. **Blood supply**: the **superior thyroid artery** (from the external carotid artery) and the **inferior thyroid artery** (from the thyrocervical trunk of the subclavian artery), sometimes the unpaired **thyroid ima artery** (from the aortic arch or brachiocephalic trunk). Its veins drain into the internal jugular and brachiocephalic veins.
s5915 5. The parathyroid glands
s5916 The **parathyroid glands** (*glandulae parathyroideae*) are usually **4** (2 superior and 2 inferior), pea-sized (4–8 × 3–4 × 2–3 mm), with a total mass of **0.1–0.35 g**; they lie on the **posterior surface** of the lobes of the thyroid gland, between its fibrous capsule and fascial sheath; their number and location vary (sometimes within the gland, in the mediastinum or beside the thymus). The superior glands develop from the 4th pharyngeal pouch and the inferior glands from the 3rd (together with the thymus) — which is why the inferior glands may be displaced lower. The chief cells produce **parathyroid hormone** — it increases the release of calcium from bone, its reabsorption in the kidney and its absorption in the intestine (via vitamin D), **raising blood calcium** (antagonist of calcitonin).

s5919 6. The suprarenal glands
s5920 The **suprarenal (adrenal) glands** (*glandulae suprarenales*) are paired glands on the upper poles of the kidneys, behind the peritoneum, within the renal fascia (but separated from the kidney by a layer of fat), at the level of T~XI~–T~XII~. The **right gland** is triangular (in front of it — the liver and inferior vena cava), the **left gland** crescentic (in front of it — the stomach, pancreas and splenic vessels). Each weighs **≈6–7 g** (both 12–13 g) and is 40–60 mm long. The suprarenal vein leaves through the **hilum** on the anterior surface. The gland is enclosed in a fibrous capsule and consists of two substances differing in origin and function.

s5937 The **blood supply** is very rich: the **superior** (from the inferior phrenic artery), **middle** (from the abdominal aorta) and **inferior** (from the renal artery) suprarenal arteries. There is a single **vein**: the right drains into the **inferior vena cava** and the left into the **left renal vein**. The medulla is supplied by sympathetic preganglionic fibers (splanchnic nerves) — it is regarded as a modified sympathetic ganglion. **Paraganglia** (for example, the carotid body and the para-aortic paraganglia) also consist of chromaffin tissue.
