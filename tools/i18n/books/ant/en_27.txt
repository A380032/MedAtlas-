s3299 6. Fasciae, retinacula and tendon sheaths of the upper limb
s3300 By region, the fasciae of the upper limb are divided into the **deltoid**, **supraspinous and infraspinous**, **brachial** and **antebrachial** fasciae. The **brachial fascia** sends the medial and lateral **intermuscular septa** to the humerus. The **antebrachial fascia** is even stronger; in the wrist region it thickens to form the **retinacula**. In the palm the fascia forms the strong triangular **palmar aponeurosis** (*aponeurosis palmaris*) — its apex faces the flexor retinaculum and the tendon of the palmaris longus, and its base faces the fingers.
s3301 The **flexor retinaculum** (*retinaculum musculorum flexorum*) bridges the carpal groove to form the **carpal tunnel** (*canalis carpi*); through it pass the **median nerve** and nine tendons — the 8 tendons of the flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus (in a common synovial sheath) and the tendon of the flexor pollicis longus (in a separate sheath). On the medial side of the retinaculum is the **ulnar canal** (Guyon's canal), through which the ulnar nerve and vessels pass, and on the lateral side the **radial canal**, through which the tendon of the flexor carpi radialis passes.
s3302 The space under the **extensor retinaculum** (*retinaculum musculorum extensorum*) is divided into **six osteofibrous compartments** (from the radial side): 1) abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis; 2) extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis; 3) extensor pollicis longus; 4) extensor digitorum and extensor indicis; 5) extensor digiti minimi; 6) the tendon of the extensor carpi ulnaris. The tendons in each compartment are surrounded by a synovial sheath.

The synovial sheaths of **fingers II–IV**, by contrast, are separate and lie only from the metacarpophalangeal joint to the distal phalanx.
s3308 7. Topography of the upper limb
s3309 The **axillary fossa** (*fossa axillaris*) is bounded in front by the skin fold corresponding to the lower border of the pectoralis major and behind by the fold corresponding to the lower border of the latissimus dorsi; through it pass the **axillary artery and vein**, the cords of the **brachial plexus** and lymph nodes. Three triangles are distinguished in the **anterior wall** of the axillary cavity: the **clavipectoral** (between the clavicle and the upper border of the pectoralis minor), the **pectoral** (the region of the pectoralis minor) and the **subpectoral** (between the lower borders of the pectoralis minor and major).

s3326 8. Age-related features
s3327 In the newborn the muscles of the shoulder girdle are well defined, but their tendons are short and thin.
s3328 The anterior and posterior groups of arm muscles are well developed; all three heads of the triceps are well formed, and their tendinous parts are clearly visible, though thin.
s3329 The anterior group of forearm muscles is sufficiently developed, with well-defined tendons; of the posterior group, the extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis have long bellies and short tendons; the proximal part of the extensor digitorum is broad; the supinator is flat and broad.
s3330 In the newborn the tone of the flexors exceeds that of the extensors — the arms are flexed and the fingers clenched into a fist (physiological hypertonia), which decreases during the first months of life.
s3331 The palmar muscles are poorly developed in the newborn and consist of fine muscle fibers; they grow considerably in the first year of life — in parallel with grasping objects and, later, learning fine finger movements.

s3412 A 35-year-old woman has complained for 4 months of numbness in the thumb, index and middle fingers of the right hand and pain that wakes her at night; she works at a computer all day. On examination the thenar eminence is slightly flattened and opposition of the thumb to the little finger is weak; holding the wrist flexed intensifies the symptoms.
s3413 Which nerve is compressed, and where? What forms the canal, and what else passes through it?
s3414 **Carpal tunnel syndrome** — the **median nerve** is compressed in the carpal tunnel. The tunnel is formed by the groove of the carpal bones and the **flexor retinaculum** stretched over it. Besides the median nerve, the 8 tendons of the flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus (in a common synovial sheath) and the tendon of the flexor pollicis longus pass through it. The median nerve supplies the thenar muscles (abductor pollicis brevis, opponens, superficial head of the flexor brevis) — hence the thenar atrophy and weak opposition.
s3415 A young man fractured the middle third of the humerus. Since then he cannot extend his hand and fingers — when he stretches the arm forward, the hand hangs down; sensation is reduced on the back of the thumb, between metacarpals I and II.
s3416 Which nerve is injured, and why precisely in this fracture? Which muscles are paralyzed?
s3417 The **radial nerve** is injured: it lies directly on the bone in the **radial groove** on the posterior surface of the humerus (in the humeromuscular canal), so it is easily injured in fractures of the middle third. As a result, the **posterior group of forearm muscles** — the radial and ulnar extensors of the wrist, extensor digitorum, extensor pollicis and abductor pollicis longus, and the supinator — is paralyzed: **"wrist drop."** The triceps is usually spared, because its branches arise above the groove.
s3418 A cook pricked the palmar surface of her right little finger with a fish bone. 3 days later the whole little finger is swollen and held flexed, and any attempt to extend it is acutely painful; the swelling is spreading to the medial part of the palm and the wrist, and pain has also appeared in the thenar eminence.
s3419 How is the purulent process spreading? Which anatomical structure is responsible?
s3420 This is **purulent tenosynovitis** of the little finger. The synovial sheath of the little finger usually continues into the **common flexor synovial sheath**, which passes through the carpal tunnel into the forearm; often the common sheath communicates in the forearm region with the sheath of the flexor pollicis longus. Therefore pus can spread from the little finger to the palm and forearm (Pirogov's/Parona's space), and then into the sheath of the thumb, forming a **V-shaped phlegmon**. Because the sheaths of fingers II–IV are separate, the process in them is usually confined to the finger. Urgent surgical opening and drainage of the sheath are required.
