s1032 Arches of the foot
s1033 The bones of the foot do not lie flat — they form upwardly convex **arches**, and when standing the foot rests mainly on three points — the **calcaneal tuberosity** and **the heads of the I and V metatarsals**. The **longitudinal arch** (its medial part is high — support and shock absorption; its lateral part is low — support) and the **transverse arch** (at the level of the bases of the metatarsals) are distinguished. The arches cushion impact during walking and jumping; they are maintained by the shape of the bones, the ligaments (especially the long plantar ligament and the plantar aponeurosis) and the muscles.
s1034 **Radiological anatomy and age-related features.** In the newborn the tarsal bones are mainly cartilaginous; ossification centers are present only in the **calcaneus** (from the 6th month of fetal life), **talus** (7th–8th month) and **cuboid** (9th month). After birth centers appear in the lateral cuneiform (1st year), intermediate cuneiform (3–4 years), medial cuneiform (2–4 years) and navicular (3–5 years). An additional center appears in the calcaneal tuberosity at 7–10 years and fuses with the body at **16–17 years**. Centers in the epiphyses of the metatarsals and phalanges appear at 2–4 years and fuse at **16–19 years**.

s1184 When intracranial pressure rises sharply (edema, hemorrhage), the cerebellar tonsils and the medulla oblongata may **herniate into the foramen magnum** — this compresses the respiratory and circulatory centers and leads to death. That is why lumbar puncture is dangerous when intracranial pressure is high. The **external occipital protuberance** is a landmark for measuring the head (head circumference) and for blocking the occipital nerves.
s1185 In the region of the **sphenoidal angle** of the parietal bone (the temporal region — the **pterion**) the bone is thin, and the **middle meningeal artery** lies on its inner surface. When a blow falls here, the artery ruptures and blood collects between the bone and the dura mater — an **epidural hematoma**. The patient may initially regain consciousness (the “lucid interval”), after which the condition deteriorates sharply — emergency trepanation is required.
s1186 Inflammation of the frontal sinus — **frontal sinusitis** — is accompanied by pain in the forehead and supraorbital region; pressure over the supraorbital foramen (where the nerve emerges) is painful. Because the sinus opens into the nose by a narrow passage, drainage is difficult during inflammation. Infection may spread into the cranial cavity through the thin posterior wall of the frontal sinus (meningitis, brain abscess).
s1187 On adult X-rays a thin light line along the midline of the frontal bone is often a **persistent metopic suture** — do not confuse it with a fracture. The suture is **serrated, symmetric** and exactly in the midline, whereas a fracture line is irregular and usually deviates from the midline.
s1188 In a fracture of the anterior cranial fossa (usually through the **cribriform plate**), the meninges tear and cerebrospinal fluid leaks from the nose — **CSF leak** (rhinorrhea); loss of smell (anosmia) and “spectacle” bruising around the eyes are also typical. If clear fluid flows from the nose, it should be tested with the blood-mixed spot (halo) test and for glucose. Such a patient must not blow the nose hard or have a nasal tube inserted — infection may spread into the cranial cavity. Because the orbital plate is very thin, **inflammation of the ethmoid cells** (ethmoiditis, especially in children) easily spreads to the orbit.
s1189 Remember the fontanelles by **“4 – 2 – 1 – 0”**: there are **4** types (6 in total) of fontanelles; **2** are unpaired (anterior and posterior), and two pairs are lateral (sphenoidal and mastoid). The anterior fontanelle closes at **1–1.5 years**, the posterior at **2–3 months**, and the lateral fontanelles are often already **closed at birth (0)**.
s1190 The **anterior fontanelle** serves as a “window” for the pediatrician. A **bulging, tense** fontanelle indicates increased intracranial pressure (meningitis, hydrocephalus, hemorrhage); a **sunken** fontanelle is a sign of dehydration (exsiccosis). **Early closure** of the fontanelle and premature ossification of the sutures (**craniosynostosis**) lead to deformity of the head and restriction of brain growth; **late closure** is seen in rickets, hypothyroidism or hydrocephalus. In infants, ultrasound examination of the brain (neurosonography) is performed precisely through the anterior fontanelle.
s1191 1. The skull: general information
s1192 The **skull** (*cranium*) protects the brain and the sense organs and serves as a support for the initial parts of the digestive and respiratory tracts. It is divided into two parts: the **neurocranium** (*neurocranium*) — forming the cavity containing the brain, and the **viscerocranium (facial skeleton)** (*viscerocranium*) — the skeleton of the orbits and the nasal and oral cavities. The neurocranium is formed by **8** bones: unpaired — the **occipital, sphenoid, frontal and ethmoid** bones, and paired — the **parietal** and **temporal** bones. In the neurocranium the **cranial vault (calvaria)** (*calvaria*) and the **cranial base** (*basis cranii*) are distinguished.

s1195 Development of the skull bones
s1196 The skull bones pass through three stages: **membranous** (end of the 1st — beginning of the 2nd month of fetal life), **cartilaginous** (end of the 2nd — beginning of the 3rd month, when the cranial base becomes cartilage) and **bony**. Most bones of the **cranial vault and face** do not pass through a cartilaginous stage but ossify directly from connective tissue by the **endesmal** route (primary bones); the bones of the **cranial base** ossify via cartilage (secondary bones). The facial bones develop from the **visceral (branchial) arches** of the embryo: arch I — the mandibular arch (maxilla and mandible, malleus, incus), arch II — the hyoid arch (stapes, lesser horns of the hyoid bone), and so on.
