s4913 A 6-year-old child was brought to hospital 2 days after the onset of abdominal pain. At operation the vermiform appendix was found perforated and pus had spread throughout the abdominal cavity (generalized peritonitis).
s4914 Why does appendicitis progress rapidly to generalized peritonitis in children? Which features of the peritoneum and omentum are responsible?
s4915 In children the **greater omentum is short and thin** (almost undeveloped until 2 years, covering the intestines only by 7 years) — it cannot reach the focus of inflammation and wall it off with adhesions. In addition, in children the peritoneum is **thin** and **highly absorptive** (toxins are absorbed rapidly), the mesenteries and omenta contain little fat, and the peritoneal recesses are shallow — there are no barriers to confine the infection. Because the right paracolic gutter communicates freely above with the hepatic bursa and below with the lesser pelvis, pus spreads rapidly.
s4916 A patient with a gastric ulcer suddenly developed "dagger-like" pain in the epigastric region. A few hours later the pain was also felt in the lower right abdomen, and the abdominal wall was board-like. A radiograph showed free air under the diaphragm.
s4917 What complication has occurred? By what anatomical route did the gastric contents reach the right iliac region? Why has the free air collected under the diaphragm?
s4918 **Perforation of the ulcer into the abdominal cavity**. The gastric (or duodenal) contents flow from the **hepatic bursa** of the upper compartment (the right subhepatic space) down along the **right paracolic gutter** (the groove between the ascending colon and the right lateral abdominal wall) into the **right iliac fossa** — which is why pain resembling appendicitis appears. In an upright patient, free air rises to the highest point of the peritoneal cavity — **under the diaphragm** — and is seen on the radiograph as a "crescent" of lucency.
s4919 A 26-year-old woman was brought in with sharp lower abdominal pain, dizziness and fainting; her period is late and her blood pressure is low. The physician performed a puncture through the posterior fornix of the vagina and obtained blood from the abdominal cavity.
s4920 In which part of the peritoneal cavity has the blood collected, and why was the puncture made precisely from this site?
s4921 The probable diagnosis is a **ruptured ectopic (tubal) pregnancy** with intra-abdominal bleeding. In women the **lowest point** of the peritoneal cavity is the **rectouterine pouch (pouch of Douglas)** between the uterus and rectum; blood in the abdominal cavity collects here in both the upright and supine positions. The floor of this pouch is in direct contact with the **posterior fornix of the vagina** (separated only by the vaginal wall and peritoneum), so the presence of blood can be quickly established by puncture through the vagina (culdocentesis).

s4951 **Nosebleed** (epistaxis) most often comes from **Kiesselbach's area** — the vascular region in the anteroinferior part of the nasal septum — which is close to the nasal vestibule, so pinching the alae of the nose or anterior packing is effective. Deviation of the nasal septum makes breathing difficult (septoplasty). The roof of the nasal cavity — the **cribriform plate** — is thin: in fractures of the cranial base, **CSF leak** (rhinorrhea) and loss of smell occur through it.
s4952 The opening of the **maxillary sinus** lies **high** above the floor of the sinus (into the middle nasal meatus, in the superomedial wall of the sinus), so in the upright position fluid drains poorly from it — **maxillary sinusitis** is common and tends to become chronic. The floor of the sinus is very close to the roots of the upper molars (odontogenic sinusitis). Puncture of the maxillary sinus is performed through the inferior nasal meatus. Inflammation of the frontal sinus is **frontal sinusitis**, and of the ethmoidal cells **ethmoiditis** (which may spread into the orbit).
s4953 **Cricothyrotomy (coniotomy)** is emergency opening of the larynx through the **median cricothyroid (conus elasticus) ligament** when the upper airway is suddenly obstructed (foreign body, laryngeal edema). This ligament lies superficially under the skin and is easily palpated between the lower border of the thyroid cartilage and the arch of the cricoid cartilage; because it lies above the large vessels and the isthmus of the thyroid gland, the procedure can be performed quickly and relatively safely.
s4954 All the intrinsic laryngeal muscles are supplied by the **inferior laryngeal nerve** (the continuation of the recurrent laryngeal nerve, X), except the **cricothyroid** — supplied by the **external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve**. The **only** muscle that opens the rima glottidis is the posterior cricoarytenoid; if it is paralyzed bilaterally, the rima glottidis remains closed and there is a risk of **asphyxia**.
s4955 The **recurrent laryngeal nerve** is injured in thyroid operations, aneurysm of the aortic arch or mediastinal tumors (the left nerve, which loops around the aortic arch, is longer and more often affected): unilateral injury causes **hoarseness**; bilateral injury fixes the vocal folds in the median position, and severe respiratory failure develops. Sensation of the laryngeal mucosa: above the vocal folds — the superior laryngeal nerve (internal branch); below — the inferior laryngeal nerve.
s4956 1. The respiratory system: general information
s4957 The **respiratory system** (*systema respiratorium*) provides **gas exchange** between the organism and the environment — saturating the blood with oxygen and removing carbon dioxide. It consists of the **airways** and the organ of gas exchange — the paired **lungs**. The airways are divided into **upper** (nasal cavity, nasal and oral parts of the pharynx) and **lower** (larynx, trachea, bronchi) tracts. Because the walls of the airways contain a bony or cartilaginous skeleton, they do not collapse; their inner surface is lined mainly by **pseudostratified ciliated epithelium** — the cilia sweep mucus and the dust stuck to it outward. In the airways air is **cleaned, moistened and warmed**; smell and voice production also take place here.

s4960 **Development.** The external nose and nasal cavity develop together with the skull bones and the olfactory organ. The **lower airways** appear in the **3rd–4th week** of fetal life as an outgrowth of the ventral wall of the foregut — the **laryngotracheal (respiratory) diverticulum**; its endoderm gives rise to the epithelium and glands of the airways, and the surrounding mesenchyme to the cartilages, muscles and connective tissue. The diverticulum is separated from the foregut by the **tracheoesophageal septum** — a defect in it causes anomalies such as **tracheoesophageal fistula**. Its distal end divides into two **lung buds** (3 lobar buds on the right, 2 on the left). The **laryngeal cartilages** develop from the mesenchyme of the 4th–6th branchial arches (the cricoid first, the thyroid last); the laryngeal muscles arise from the 4th and 6th branchial arches and are supplied by branches of the vagus nerve.
s4961 2. The external nose
s4962 The **external nose** (*nasus externus*) has a **root** (between the eyebrows, separated from the forehead by a notch), a **dorsum**, a **tip (apex)** and **alae**; the lower borders of the alae bound the **nostrils** (*nares*). Its skeleton is formed above by the **nasal bones** and the frontal processes of the maxillae, and below by **hyaline cartilages**: the paired **lateral cartilage** (the lateral wall of the nose), the paired **major alar cartilage** (whose medial and lateral crura bound the nostril), the **minor alar cartilages** and **accessory cartilages**, and the unpaired quadrangular **septal cartilage**.

s4965 3. The nasal cavity
s4966 The **nasal cavity** (*cavitas nasi*) opens in front to the exterior through the nostrils and behind into the nasal part of the pharynx through the **choanae**. The **nasal septum** (*septum nasi*) divides it into right and left halves; the posterior **bony part** of the septum is formed by the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone and the vomer, and the anterior part by **cartilage** and a **membranous part** (mobile). The walls of the cavity: **roof** — the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone (in front the nasal and frontal bones, behind the body of the sphenoid); **floor** — the hard palate; **medial** — the nasal septum; **lateral** — the most complex wall (maxilla, lacrimal, ethmoid, palatine and sphenoid bones and the inferior nasal concha).

s4969 In the front of each half, under the ala of the nose, is the **nasal vestibule**, lined by skin with hairs and sebaceous and sweat glands. Three **nasal conchae** hang from the lateral wall — the **superior** and **middle** (parts of the ethmoid bone) and the **inferior** (an independent bone); between them lie the **superior**, **middle** and **inferior nasal meatuses**. Between the conchae and the septum is the slit-like **common nasal meatus**; behind the conchae the **nasopharyngeal meatus** leads to the choanae. Behind the superior concha is the **sphenoethmoidal recess**.
