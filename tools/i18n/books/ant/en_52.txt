s6418 The **arterial circle of the brain** (the **circle of Willis**, *circulus arteriosus cerebri*) lies on the base of the brain **around the sella turcica and the pituitary gland**, encircling the optic chiasm and the tuber cinereum. It is formed by: both **anterior cerebral arteries** (A~1~ segments) and the unpaired **anterior communicating artery** that connects them, the terminal parts of both **internal carotid arteries**, both **posterior communicating arteries** and both **posterior cerebral arteries** (P~1~ segments). By uniting the carotid and vertebrobasilar systems and the right and left sides, the circle provides **collateral circulation** when one artery is narrowed. The circle has the "classic" form in ≈50 % of people; in the rest some of its parts are thin or absent.

s6441 6. Anastomoses and age-related features
s6442 **Between the external and internal carotid systems**: the angular artery (from the facial artery) ↔ the dorsal nasal artery (from the ophthalmic artery); the superficial temporal artery ↔ the supraorbital and supratrochlear arteries.
s6443 **Between the two sides**: the right and left branches of the facial, lingual (few), occipital, superficial temporal and superior thyroid arteries across the midline; at the base of the brain — the anterior communicating artery.
s6444 **Between the carotid and subclavian systems**: the posterior communicating arteries of the circle of Willis; the superior ↔ inferior thyroid arteries; the occipital artery ↔ the vertebral and deep cervical arteries.
s6445 In the newborn the diameter of the aorta is 18–20 mm, and ≈4 cm at 15–16 years; the descending aorta lengthens ≈4 times by the age of 50. Because the heart lies high in the newborn, the ascending aorta passes into the arch at a higher level; the aortic arch lies at the level of T~I~ and descends to the level of T~II~ at 17–20 years and T~III~ at 25–30 years.
s6446 The diameter of the common carotid artery is 3–6 mm until 1 year, 6–7 mm at 15–16 years and 9–14 mm in adults; in the newborn the bifurcation is at C~III~–C~IV~ (C~IV~ in adults). The intracranial part of the internal carotid artery and the middle cerebral artery grow rapidly in infancy and early childhood in step with the growth of the brain.
s6447 In children the muscles and fasciae of the neck are thin, so the large arteries lie more superficially; the arterial walls contain many elastic fibers (they are distensible), and by 12–16 years the length and diameter of the arteries increase 3–5 times. In the elderly the aorta and carotid arteries undergo atherosclerosis and become tortuous and rigid.

s6540 A 20-year-old man was struck hard on the temple by a football, lost consciousness for a few minutes, then came round and felt well. After 3 hours he developed a severe headache and vomiting and lost consciousness again; his right pupil is dilated. CT shows a fracture of the temporal bone and a lens-shaped collection of blood between the bone and the dura mater.
s6541 What condition has developed? Which artery is injured? State its origin and course.
s6542 **Epidural hematoma**. The injured artery is the **middle meningeal artery**: it arises from the mandibular part of the **maxillary artery** (a terminal branch of the external carotid artery), enters the middle cranial fossa through the **foramen spinosum**, divides into frontal and parietal branches and lies in grooves on the inner surface of the skull bones, outside the dura mater. Its anterior branch passes under the thinnest bone, in the region of the **pterion**, so it tears in a temporal fracture. Arterial blood strips the dura mater from the bone and compresses the brain after a "lucid interval"; displacement of the temporal lobe compresses nerve III and dilates the pupil. Treatment — emergency trephination and ligation of the artery.
s6543 A 67-year-old woman suddenly developed paralysis of the left arm and the lower left half of the face, with reduced sensation on the left; the leg is relatively spared. Speech is preserved (she is right-handed). CT/MRI shows a focus of ischemia on the lateral surface of the right hemisphere.
s6544 Which arterial territory is affected? Explain its area of supply and why the arm is affected more than the leg.
s6545 The affected artery is the **right middle cerebral artery**. It is the largest terminal branch of the internal carotid artery; it enters the lateral sulcus, winds around the insula and supplies the **superolateral surface** of the hemisphere, including the **face and arm** areas of the precentral and postcentral gyri; its lenticulostriate branches supply the internal capsule. The **leg** area (paracentral lobule) lies on the medial surface and receives blood from the **anterior cerebral artery**, so it is less affected. The signs are on the opposite (left) side because the motor and sensory pathways cross. In a right-handed person the speech centers are in the left hemisphere, so there is no aphasia.
s6546 A patient's left common carotid artery is completely occluded by an atherosclerotic plaque, yet the neurological signs are minimal. On angiography the left middle and anterior cerebral arteries fill with contrast.
s6547 By what routes does blood reach the left hemisphere? Explain the anatomy of these routes.
s6548 The main route is the **arterial circle of the brain (of Willis)**: 1) from the right internal carotid artery, the right anterior cerebral artery → the **anterior communicating artery** → the left anterior cerebral artery and the terminal part of the left internal carotid artery → the left middle cerebral artery; 2) the vertebral arteries → the basilar artery → the left posterior cerebral artery → the **left posterior communicating artery** → the cerebral part of the left internal carotid artery. Additional routes: since the left external carotid artery is also occluded, the external carotid branches of the opposite side (facial, superficial temporal, superior thyroid) anastomose across the midline, and then blood passes through the **angular artery ↔ dorsal nasal artery** and the supraorbital artery into the **ophthalmic artery** and, by reversed flow, from it into the internal carotid artery. Because the structure of the circle varies between individuals, collateral flow is not equally adequate in everyone.
