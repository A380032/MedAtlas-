s3588 The **dorsal muscles** are the **extensor digitorum brevis** and **extensor hallucis brevis**; they arise from the calcaneus, pass to toes II–IV and the great toe, and take part in extending the toes (deep fibular nerve). s3603 6. Fasciae and retinacula of the lower limb
s3604 The **iliac fascia** covers the iliopsoas; its thickened part between the inguinal ligament and the iliopubic eminence — the **iliopectineal arch** — divides the space under the inguinal ligament into the **muscular lacuna** and the **vascular lacuna**. The **fascia lata** (*fascia lata*) is the strongest fascia in the body; laterally it thickens to form the **iliotibial tract** (the tensor fasciae latae and gluteus maximus insert into it). In the upper third of the thigh the fascia lata splits into two layers; its superficial layer contains the **saphenous opening** (*hiatus saphenus*, fossa ovalis) — bounded by the **falciform margin**, closed by the cribriform fascia — through which the **great saphenous vein** drains into the femoral vein.
s3605 The **crural fascia** forms the anterior and posterior intermuscular septa; posteriorly it splits into superficial and deep layers, separating the triceps surae from the deep group. In the region of the malleoli the fascia thickens to form the **retinacula**: the **superior and inferior extensor retinacula** (Y-shaped; for the tendons of the anterior group), the **flexor retinaculum** (between the medial malleolus and the calcaneus — forming the **medial malleolar canal**, through which the tendons of the deep posterior group and the tibial neurovascular bundle pass), and the **superior and inferior fibular retinacula** (behind the lateral malleolus). On the sole the fascia forms the thick **plantar aponeurosis** — it holds the longitudinal arch like a "tie-rod."

s3629 The **femoral canal** (*canalis femoralis*) does not exist in a healthy person — it forms only when a **femoral hernia** develops. Normally, in the medial angle of the vascular lacuna there is the **femoral ring** (*anulus femoralis*): in front — the inguinal ligament, behind — the pectineal ligament (fascia), medially — the lacunar ligament, laterally — the femoral vein; from the abdominal side it is closed by the transversalis fascia and peritoneum. The hernia enters through the femoral ring (internal opening), passes between the layers of the fascia lata and emerges under the skin through the **saphenous opening** (external opening). Walls of the canal: anterior — superficial layer of the fascia lata (superior horn of the falciform margin), lateral — sheath of the femoral vein, posterior — deep layer of the fascia lata (pectineal fascia).
s3630 In the newborn the lower limb muscles are less developed than those of the upper limb; they grow rapidly in the first year of life — as the child crawls, stands and begins to walk. In the newborn the lower limb muscles make up ≈38% of the total muscle mass (in adults — more than half); intensive growth is observed up to 5–6 years and during puberty.
s3631 In the newborn the gluteus maximus and medius are well developed and the gluteus minimus poorly; the tensor fasciae latae is short and flat, and the iliotibial tract thin. The gluteus maximus grows intensively once the child begins to walk upright (2–4 years) and forms the prominence of the buttock.
s3632 In the newborn the sartorius is relatively broad and runs straight; the quadriceps and its heads are well developed. The semitendinosus has few tendinous fibers, and the semimembranosus is relatively large.
s3633 Because the contractile part of the leg muscles is long, the newborn's leg is of the same girth in its upper and lower parts (cylindrical); the deep-layer muscles are not fully mature.
s3634 The plantar muscles are poorly developed; when the child begins to walk they mature first and take part in forming the arch of the foot.
s3635 In the newborn the femoral triangle is relatively large (occupying ≈1/3 of the thigh), the muscular and vascular lacunae are narrow and vertically placed, and the popliteal fossa is shallow.

s3723 A 65-year-old woman noticed a painful walnut-sized bulge in the groin, below the inguinal ligament and medial to the femoral vein; for 6 hours she has had severe pain, vomiting and no bowel movement. The bulge does not return to the abdominal cavity.
s3724 What hernia and what complication should be suspected? By what route did the hernia emerge, and why is it prone to strangulation?
s3725 This is a **strangulated femoral hernia**. The hernia entered through the **femoral ring** (in front — inguinal ligament, behind — pectineal ligament, medially — lacunar ligament, laterally — femoral vein), passed between the layers of the fascia lata and emerged under the skin through the **saphenous opening** (hiatus saphenus) — which is why the bulge lies below the inguinal ligament (an inguinal hernia lies above it). The femoral ring is narrow and its walls, especially the lacunar ligament, are rigid and do not stretch — so femoral hernias are frequently strangulated. It is common in women because of the width of the pelvis. Emergency surgery is required.
s3726 After a nurse gave an intramuscular injection into a patient's buttock, the patient felt an "electric shock" pain running down the leg; later weakness developed in the posterior thigh group and all the muscles of the leg and foot.
s3727 Which nerve is injured? Where is the correct injection site, and why?
s3728 The **sciatic nerve** is injured: it leaves the pelvis through the **infrapiriform foramen**, descends under the gluteus maximus in the inferomedial part of the buttock and supplies the posterior thigh group and all the muscles of the leg and foot. The correct injection site is the **upper outer quadrant** of the buttock (or the region of the gluteus medius and minimus — the "ventrogluteal" site between the anterior superior iliac spine and the iliac crest), because there are no large nerves or vessels there.
s3729 While playing volleyball, a 40-year-old man felt a blow above the heel "as if someone had kicked him" as he tried to jump, and fell. On examination a gap is palpable 3–4 cm above the calcaneal tuberosity, and the patient cannot stand on tiptoe; lying prone, squeezing the calf does not plantarflex the foot.
s3730 Which structure is injured? Which muscles form it, and which reflex, carried by which nerve, is lost?
s3731 The **calcaneal (Achilles) tendon is ruptured**. It is formed by the union of the tendons of the **triceps surae** — the two-headed **gastrocnemius** (arising from the femoral condyles) and the **soleus** (from the tibia and fibula) — and inserts on the calcaneal tuberosity; it plantarflexes the foot (standing on tiptoe, jumping, push-off in walking). It is supplied by the tibial nerve; the **Achilles reflex** (S~I~–S~II~) is lost. Failure of the foot to plantarflex when the calf is squeezed is the classic sign of tendon rupture (Thompson test).
