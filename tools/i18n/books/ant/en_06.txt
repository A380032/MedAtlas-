s0692 **Age-related features.** The first ossification center appears at the end of the 2nd month of fetal life in the region of the neck of the scapula, and the body and spine ossify from it. In the newborn the scapula lies higher and more laterally than in the adult; the coracoid process, acromion, glenoid cavity, medial border and inferior angle are cartilaginous. An ossification center appears in the coracoid process at the end of the first year, and in the acromion, inferior angle and medial border at 10–15 years. The acromion and coracoid process fuse with the body of the scapula at **18–21 years**, and the medial border and inferior angle at **20–21 years**.
s0693 4. The humerus
s0694 The **humerus** (*humerus*) is the longest tubular bone of the upper limb; it has a body and two ends. At the **proximal end** lies the spherical **head of the humerus** (*caput humeri*), facing medially, upward and slightly backward. The head is separated from the rest of the bone by a shallow groove — the **anatomical neck** (*collum anatomicum*). Lateral to it is the **greater tubercle** (*tuberculum majus*), and in front the **lesser tubercle** (*tuberculum minus*); the **crests of the greater and lesser tubercles** (*crista tuberculi majoris et minoris*) continue downward from them. Between the tubercles and crests lies the **intertubercular groove** (*sulcus intertubercularis*), through which the tendon of the long head of the biceps passes. Below the tubercles is the narrowed part of the bone — the **surgical neck** (*collum chirurgicum*) — the most common site of fracture.
s0695 The **body** (*corpus humeri*) is cylindrical above and triangular below; it has **anteromedial**, **anterolateral** and **posterior surfaces** and **medial** and **lateral borders**. In the middle of the body, on the anterolateral surface, is the **deltoid tuberosity** (*tuberositas deltoidea*). On the posterior surface the **groove for the radial nerve** (*sulcus nervi radialis*) spirals from medial to lateral and downward.
s0696 The **distal end** widens to form the **condyle of the humerus** (*condylus humeri*). Its medial part bears the **trochlea** (*trochlea humeri*), which articulates with the ulna, and its lateral part the **capitulum** (*capitulum humeri*), which articulates with the radius. Anteriorly, above the trochlea is the **coronoid fossa** (*fossa coronoidea*) and above the capitulum the **radial fossa** (*fossa radialis*); posteriorly, above the trochlea, is the deep **olecranon fossa** (*fossa olecrani*). On either side of the condyle are the **medial and lateral epicondyles** (*epicondylus medialis et lateralis*), palpable under the skin; the **supracondylar ridges** continue upward from them. The **groove for the ulnar nerve** (*sulcus nervi ulnaris*) runs on the posterior surface of the medial epicondyle.

s0713 5. The ulna
s0714 The **ulna** (*ulna*) lies on the medial side of the forearm (the little-finger side); its proximal end is thick and its distal end thin. Its **proximal end** bears the deep **trochlear notch** (*incisura trochlearis*), which articulates with the trochlea of the humerus; it is bounded posterosuperiorly by the large **olecranon** (*olecranon*) and anteriorly by the **coronoid process** (*processus coronoideus*). On the lateral surface of the coronoid process is the **radial notch** (*incisura radialis*) for the head of the radius, and below it the **tuberosity of the ulna** (*tuberositas ulnae*), to which the brachialis muscle attaches.
s0715 The **body** is triangular: it has **anterior**, **posterior** and **medial** surfaces and **anterior**, **posterior** and sharp **interosseous borders** (*margo interosseus*), the latter facing the radius. At the **distal end** lies the **head of the ulna** (*caput ulnae*) — its **articular circumference** (*circumferentia articularis*) articulates with the ulnar notch of the radius — and posteromedially the **styloid process** (*processus styloideus ulnae*).

An ossification center appears in the distal epiphysis at ≈7 years and in the olecranon at 8–11 years; the olecranon fuses with the diaphysis at **16–17 years**, and the distal end at **20–24 years**.
s0719 6. The radius
s0720 The **radius** (*radius*) lies on the lateral side of the forearm (the thumb side); unlike the ulna, its proximal end is thin and its distal end thick. At the **proximal end** is the cylindrical **head of the radius** (*caput radii*): its upper concave surface (*fovea articularis*) articulates with the capitulum of the humerus, and the **articular circumference** (*circumferentia articularis*) on its side articulates with the radial notch of the ulna. Below the head is the narrowed **neck** (*collum radii*), and below that, on the medial side, the **radial tuberosity** (*tuberositas radii*), to which the tendon of the biceps attaches.
s0721 The **body** is triangular and has a sharp **interosseous border** facing the ulna. The widened **distal end** bears on its medial side the **ulnar notch** (*incisura ulnaris*) for the head of the ulna, on its inferior surface the **carpal articular surface** (*facies articularis carpalis*), which articulates with the carpal bones, and on its lateral side the **styloid process** (*processus styloideus radii*), palpable under the skin. The posterior surface of the distal end bears a tubercle (*tuberculum dorsale*) and grooves for muscle tendons.

s0725 7. Bones of the hand
s0726 The skeleton of the hand (*ossa manus*) consists of three parts: the **carpal bones** (*ossa carpi*) — 8, the **metacarpal bones** (*ossa metacarpi*) — 5, and the **phalanges of the fingers** (*ossa digitorum, phalanges*) — 14.
s0727 Carpal bones
s0728 The 8 small spongy bones are arranged in two rows. The **proximal row** (from lateral to medial): the **scaphoid** (*os scaphoideum*), **lunate** (*os lunatum*), **triquetrum** (*os triquetrum*) and **pisiform** (*os pisiforme*). The first three together form an ellipsoid articular surface that articulates with the radius; the pisiform is a sesamoid bone lying within the tendon of the ulnar flexor muscle. The **distal row**: the **trapezium** (*os trapezium*), **trapezoid** (*os trapezoideum*), **capitate** (*os capitatum* — the largest carpal bone) and **hamate** (*os hamatum* — with a hook, *hamulus*, on its palmar surface). On the palmar side the carpal bones form a concave, trough-like **carpal groove** — closed by the flexor retinaculum, it becomes the **carpal tunnel**.
s0729 Metacarpal bones and phalanges
s0730 The **metacarpal bones** (*ossa metacarpi I–V*) are short tubular bones; each has a **base** (*basis*), **body** (*corpus*) and **head** (*caput*). The I metacarpal is the shortest and thickest, and its base bears a **saddle-shaped** articular surface for the trapezium — which allows the thumb to be opposed to the other fingers (opposition). The bases of metacarpals II–V bear lateral surfaces that articulate with their neighbors. The **phalanges** are also short tubular bones: fingers II–V have three each — **proximal**, **middle** and **distal** phalanges — and the thumb has two (proximal and distal). Each phalanx has a base, body and head (*trochlea*); the tip of the distal phalanx bears the **tuberosity of the distal phalanx** (*tuberositas phalangis distalis*). In the region of the I metacarpophalangeal joint there are 2 **sesamoid bones** within the tendons.

s0736 8. Ossification of the upper limb bones and bone age
s0737 In the newborn the carpal bones are formed but are **entirely cartilaginous** — on an X-ray the carpal region looks “empty.” Their ossification centers appear in a strict order, so the child's **bone (biological) age** is determined from an X-ray of the hand and compared with the chronological age (growth delay, endocrine diseases, forensic medical examination).
