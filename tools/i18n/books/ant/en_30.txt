s3762 **Wry neck** (*torticollis*) is tilting of the head to one side with the face turned to the opposite side. In the **congenital** form, the sternocleidomastoid becomes fibrotic and shortened because of birth trauma or a developmental defect — a firm "lump" is palpable on one side of the infant's neck. Early massage and special (stretching) exercises often give results without surgery; untreated, facial asymmetry develops.
s3763 During swallowing and speech the hyoid bone is "suspended" between the muscles above and below it: the upper muscles raise it and the larynx (in swallowing the larynx rises and the epiglottis closes its inlet), and the lower ones lower it. When both groups contract together, the hyoid bone is fixed and serves as a fixed point for the muscles that depress the mandible.
s3764 **Scalenus anterior syndrome** (or when a cervical rib is present) — the brachial plexus and subclavian artery are compressed in the interscalene space: pain, numbness, coldness of the hand and a weakened pulse are observed. The subclavian vein, which passes in front of the anterior scalene, is not compressed — it is convenient for **catheterization** (infraclavicular or supraclavicular puncture).
s3765 Purulent processes (neck **phlegmons**) spread along the fascial spaces of the neck. From the retropharyngeal and pretracheal (visceral) spaces pus can descend into the **mediastinum**, causing a serious complication — **mediastinitis**. Therefore neck phlegmons arising from dental and throat infections are opened as surgical emergencies.
s3766 In the **carotid triangle** the common carotid artery can be pressed against the **carotid tubercle** on the transverse process of the 6th cervical vertebra — the pulse is taken here, and it is pressed here to stop bleeding temporarily. At the bifurcation of the carotid artery lies the carotid sinus — firm pressure on it can cause reflex bradycardia and fainting.
s3767 **Peripheral facial nerve palsy** (Bell's palsy) — all the muscles of facial expression on one side are paralyzed: the forehead wrinkles disappear, the eye does not close (on attempting to close the eye the eyeball turns upward and the sclera is visible — "hare's eye"), the nasolabial fold flattens, the corner of the mouth droops, and food collects between the cheek and teeth (paralysis of the buccinator). In **central** palsy (stroke), by contrast, only the **lower** part of the face is affected — the forehead muscles are spared owing to bilateral cortical innervation.
s3768 **Trismus** is inability to open the mouth owing to tonic spasm of the muscles of mastication; it is an early sign of tetanus and also occurs with inflammation of the lower wisdom tooth and abscesses around the masticatory muscles. When the mouth is opened excessively wide (yawning, dental procedures), the pull of the lateral pterygoid moves the head of the mandible forward past the articular tubercle, causing **dislocation of the mandible** — the mouth is locked open.
s3769 1. Muscles of the neck: general information
s3770 Because the neck muscles differ in origin and function, their topography is complex. By topography they are divided into three groups: **superficial muscles** (platysma, sternocleidomastoid), **muscles attached to the hyoid bone** (suprahyoid and infrahyoid muscles) and **deep muscles** (lateral — scalene muscles; medial — prevertebral muscles).
s3771 2. Superficial muscles of the neck
s3772 The **platysma** (*platysma*) is a thin, broad muscular sheet lying under the skin; it arises from the fascia of the upper chest (at the level of the 2nd rib) and inserts on the border of the mandible, the masseteric fascia and the skin at the corner of the mouth. It tenses the skin of the neck, protecting the subcutaneous veins from compression, and draws the corner of the mouth down. By origin and innervation it is close to the **muscles of facial expression** — it is supplied by the **facial nerve**.

s3775 The **sternocleidomastoid** (*m. sternocleidomastoideus*) arises by two heads — from the anterior surface of the manubrium and from the sternal end of the clavicle — and inserts on the mastoid process of the temporal bone and the lateral part of the superior nuchal line. Contracting **unilaterally**, it tilts the head to its own side and turns the face to the **opposite** side; contracting **bilaterally**, it throws the head back (or flexes the neck), and with the head fixed it raises the sternum and clavicle, acting as an accessory respiratory muscle. It is supplied by the **accessory nerve** (XI) and branches of the cervical plexus. This muscle is an important landmark of the neck — it divides the neck into the medial and lateral triangles.

s3812 4. Deep muscles of the neck
s3813 **Lateral group** — the **anterior, middle and posterior scalene muscles** (*mm. scaleni*): they arise from the transverse processes of the cervical vertebrae; the anterior and middle insert on the 1st rib and the posterior on the 2nd rib. They raise the ribs (accessory muscles of inspiration), and with the ribs fixed bend the neck to the side. **Medial (prevertebral) group** — the **longus colli**, **longus capitis**, and **rectus capitis anterior and lateralis**: lying on the anterior surface of the vertebral column, they flex the neck and head forward and bend them to the side. The deep muscles are supplied by branches of the cervical and brachial plexuses.

s3816 There are two spaces among the scalene muscles: the **interscalene space** (*spatium interscalenum*) — between the anterior and middle scalenes and the 1st rib; the **subclavian artery** and the trunks of the **brachial plexus** pass through it. The **antescalene space** (*spatium antescalenum*) — in front of the anterior scalene (bounded anteriorly by the sternohyoid and sternothyroid); the **subclavian vein** passes through it.
s3817 5. Fasciae of the neck
s3818 According to the international anatomical nomenclature, the cervical fascia consists of three layers:
s3819 The **superficial (investing) layer** (*lamina superficialis*) encircles the neck like a collar, forming sheaths for the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius and a capsule for the submandibular gland; below it attaches to the manubrium and clavicle, and above to the base of the mandible, continuing into the masseteric fascia.
s3820 The **pretracheal layer** (*lamina pretrachealis*) stretches between the hyoid bone, the manubrium and the clavicles, forming sheaths for the infrahyoid muscles; laterally it attaches to the omohyoid; it also encloses the cervical viscera (larynx, trachea, thyroid gland).
s3821 The **prevertebral layer** (*lamina prevertebralis*) covers the prevertebral and scalene muscles behind the pharynx and esophagus; together with the brachial plexus and subclavian artery it continues into the axilla.
s3822 In addition, the **carotid sheath** (*vagina carotica*) is distinguished, enclosing the common carotid artery, internal jugular vein and vagus nerve. Between the layers there are spaces filled with loose tissue: the **suprasternal interfascial space** (between the superficial and pretracheal layers, above the sternum), the **pretracheal (visceral) space** and the **retropharyngeal space** (in front of the prevertebral layer) — the last continues below into the mediastinum.

s3825 6. Topography of the neck
s3826 The neck is bounded above by the border of the mandible, the external acoustic opening and the mastoid process, and below by the jugular notch of the manubrium, the clavicle and the acromion; the posterior (nuchal) region lies behind the lateral border of the trapezius. On each side the sternocleidomastoid divides the neck into the **medial (anterior)** and **lateral** triangles; the omohyoid and digastric divide them into smaller triangles.

s3844 7. Muscles of the head: muscles of facial expression
s3845 The muscles of the head are divided into muscles of **facial expression** and muscles of **mastication**. The **muscles of facial expression** develop from the second branchial arch and are supplied by the **facial nerve** (VII). Their characteristic features: 1) they are not covered by fascia; 2) they arise from bone and insert into the **skin** (some lie entirely within the skin); 3) they are arranged mainly around the natural openings (palpebral fissure, mouth, nostrils, ear) in a circular (constricting) or radial (dilating) direction; 4) on contraction they form skin folds and change the expression of the face — the **facial expression** — and they also take part in speech, eating and vision.
