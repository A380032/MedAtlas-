s0140 The outer surface of a bone (except the articular surfaces) is covered by the **bone membrane** — the **periosteum** (*periosteum*). It consists of two layers: an outer **fibrous** layer and an inner **cambial (osteogenic)** layer adjacent to the bone. Thanks to the osteoblasts of the cambial layer, bone **grows in width** and **is restored after a fracture**. The periosteum is firmly attached to the bone by collagen fibers that penetrate into it (Sharpey's fibers) and is very rich in blood vessels and nerves — which is why bone fractures and periostitis are very painful. The medullary cavity is lined from inside by the thin **endosteum**.

s0143 There are two kinds of **bone marrow**: **red marrow** (*medulla ossium rubra*) — hematopoietic and immune tissue; **yellow marrow** (*medulla ossium flava*) — consisting mainly of fat cells. In the fetus and in early childhood all bones contain red marrow. In adults, red marrow is preserved in the flat bones (sternum, ilium, vertebral bodies), the spongy bones and the spongy substance of the epiphyses of tubular bones, while the medullary cavity of the diaphyses is occupied by yellow marrow.

s0156 **Radiological anatomy.** On an X-ray the bone image is produced by the inorganic substances. Compact substance appears as a uniform, dense, smooth band-like shadow, while spongy substance appears as a reticular (meshed) shadow formed by the intersecting trabeculae. s0157 5. Development of bones and age-related features
s0158 Bone tissue appears from mesenchyme in the middle of the second month of prenatal development. By their mode of development bones are of two kinds: **primary bones** develop directly from connective tissue and pass through two stages (membrane → bone) — most bones of the cranial vault and face and part of the clavicle. **Secondary bones** pass through three stages (membrane → cartilage → bone) — the bones of the cranial base, trunk and limbs.

s0175 The primary ossification center of tubular bones appears in the **diaphysis** in the 2nd month of fetal life. Ossification centers in the epiphyses appear mainly after birth (some — for example, in the distal epiphysis of the femur — just before birth). **Secondary ossification centers** appear in the apophyses from 8–9 years. The cartilage between the diaphysis and the epiphysis ossifies after puberty (**synostosis**), and growth of the bone in length stops — at about 22–25 years in men and 20–22 years in women.
s0176 The skeleton of a **newborn** consists of about 270 separate bones (172 in the trunk and head, 98 in the limbs); later many bones fuse with each other. A newborn's bone consists of coarse-fibered (woven) bone tissue; it contains few mineral salts and much water and many blood vessels, and its periosteum is thick with a well-developed cambial layer. In the first 6 years of life woven bone is replaced by **lamellar** bone and osteons are formed; part of the spongy substance is resorbed, the medullary cavity widens, and red marrow gradually turns into yellow marrow.
s0177 6. The vertebral column and the general structure of a vertebra
s0178 The **vertebral column** (*columna vertebralis*) is the axial skeleton of the body and consists of **33–34** vertebrae: **7 cervical**, **12 thoracic**, **5 lumbar**, **5 sacral** (fused to form the sacrum) and **3–5 coccygeal** vertebrae. Its length averages 73–75 cm in men and 69–71 cm in women. The vertebral column serves as a support, protects the spinal cord in the vertebral canal and takes part in movements of the trunk and head.
s0179 Each vertebra (*vertebra*) consists of two main parts: the anterior **vertebral body** (*corpus vertebrae*) and the posterior **vertebral arch** (*arcus vertebrae*). The body bears the main load, so its size increases downward and is greatest in the lumbar region; its posterior surface has nutrient foramina. The arch is attached to the body by two **pedicles** (*pediculus arcus vertebrae*), and its posterior part is called the **lamina** (*lamina arcus vertebrae*). The body and arch bound the **vertebral foramen** (*foramen vertebrale*); all the foramina together form the **vertebral canal** (*canalis vertebralis*), which contains the spinal cord and its meninges.
s0180 **Seven processes** arise from the arch: posteriorly — the unpaired **spinous process** (*processus spinosus*); laterally — the paired **transverse processes** (*processus transversi*); upward and downward — the paired **superior and inferior articular processes** (*processus articulares superiores et inferiores*), whose articular surfaces form joints with the adjacent vertebrae. The upper and lower borders of the pedicles bear the **vertebral notches** (*incisura vertebralis superior et inferior*); the notches of adjacent vertebrae form the **intervertebral foramen** (*foramen intervertebrale*), through which the spinal nerves and blood vessels pass.

s0183 Development and age-related features of the vertebrae
s0184 Each vertebra develops from three main ossification centers: one in the **body** and two in the **two halves** of the arch. In the newborn the cervical (III–VII), thoracic and lumbar vertebrae resemble those of the adult in shape, but most of their parts are cartilaginous and the ossification centers are separated from each other by layers of cartilage.

s0202 7. Cervical vertebrae
s0203 There are 7 **cervical vertebrae** (*vertebrae cervicales*, C~I~–C~VII~). Because they bear little weight, their bodies are small and elliptical, with saddle-shaped curved upper and lower surfaces; the vertebral foramen is large and triangular. Their main distinctive feature is the **foramen transversarium** (*foramen transversarium*) in the transverse processes: the **vertebral artery** and vein pass through the foramina of C~I~–C~VI~. The tip of the transverse process bears **anterior and posterior tubercles**, between which the **groove for the spinal nerve** (*sulcus nervi spinalis*) runs above. The anterior tubercle of the VI cervical vertebra is large and is called the **carotid tubercle** (*tuberculum caroticum*) — in bleeding, the common carotid artery can be compressed against this tubercle.
s0204 The articular processes are short and their articular surfaces are oblique: the superior one faces backward and upward, the inferior one forward and downward. The spinous processes of vertebrae II–VI are short and **bifid** (split in two). The spinous process of the **VII cervical vertebra** is long, not bifid and clearly palpable under the skin, so it is called the **vertebra prominens** (*vertebra prominens*).

s0207 The atlas — the I cervical vertebra
s0208 The **atlas** (*atlas*) is a vertebra without a body: during development its body fuses with the II vertebra, forming its dens. The atlas consists of two **lateral masses** (*massa lateralis*) and the **anterior and posterior arches** (*arcus anterior et posterior*) connecting them. The anterior arch has an **anterior tubercle** on its front surface and, on its inner surface, the **facet for the dens** (*fovea dentis*), which articulates with the dens of the II vertebra. The posterior arch bears the **posterior tubercle** (a remnant of the spinous process) and, on its upper surface, the **groove for the vertebral artery** (*sulcus arteriae vertebralis*). The oval, concave superior articular surfaces of the lateral masses articulate with the occipital condyles, and their flat inferior surfaces with the II vertebra.

s0211 A pair of ossification centers appears in the lateral masses and posterior arch of the atlas, and two additional centers appear in the anterior arch at 1 year. The posterior half-arches fuse at 2–5 years, and the anterior and posterior arches fuse with each other at 5–9 years.
s0212 The axis — the II cervical vertebra
s0213 The **axis** (*axis*) is distinguished by the cylindrical **dens** (*dens*) projecting upward from the upper surface of its body; the dens has an **apex** (*apex dentis*) and anterior and posterior articular surfaces. The dens articulates with the anterior arch of the atlas and serves as the **axis** around which the atlas, together with the head, rotates to the right and left. 3 main ossification centers appear in the axis in the 4th month of fetal life (2 in the arch, 1 in the body); they fuse at 2 years, while the cartilage at the base of the dens persists until 3–6 years.

s0216 **Radiological anatomy.** In the anteroposterior projection, vertebrae I–II are covered by the shadow of the mandible, so usually only vertebrae III–VII are visible; the upper ones are examined in a special open-mouth projection. In the lateral projection all cervical vertebrae are well seen: the arches of the atlas, the dens of the axis, the vertebral bodies and the intervertebral spaces 4–5 mm high are clearly distinguished.

s0219 8. Thoracic vertebrae
s0220 There are 12 **thoracic vertebrae** (*vertebrae thoracicae*, T~I~–T~XII~); their bodies increase in size from above downward, and the vertebral foramen is relatively small and round. Their main distinctive feature is the **costal facets**: the posterolateral surface of the bodies of vertebrae II–IX bears **superior and inferior costal demifacets** (*fovea costalis superior et inferior*) — the demifacets of two adjacent vertebrae together form one articular surface for the head of a rib. Vertebra I has a **whole** superior facet for the I rib and an inferior demifacet for the II rib, vertebra X has only a superior demifacet, and vertebrae XI and XII each have one **whole** facet.
s0221 The anterior surface of the transverse processes of vertebrae I–X bears a **transverse costal facet** (*fovea costalis processus transversi*), which articulates with the tubercle of the rib; vertebrae XI–XII lack this facet. The spinous processes are long, triangular and directed obliquely downward, overlapping each other like roof tiles (especially in the mid-thoracic region). The articular processes lie in the frontal plane: the superior articular surface faces backward, the inferior one forward.

s0224 **Radiological anatomy.** In the anteroposterior projection the bodies are rectangular and the intervertebral spaces are 4–5 mm; the transverse processes overlap the posterior ends of the ribs (whereas the transverse processes of the I thoracic vertebra are clearly visible). In the lateral projection the bodies are rectangles with smooth borders, and the intervertebral foramina are seen as round lucencies at the posteroinferior margin of the body.
s0225 9. Lumbar vertebrae
s0226 There are 5 **lumbar vertebrae** (*vertebrae lumbales*, L~I~–L~V~). Because they bear the greatest load, their bodies are the largest and **bean-shaped**, and the vertebral foramen is triangular. Since the transverse process is a rudimentary rib, it is called the **costal process** (*processus costalis, s. costiformis*); on the posterior surface of its base lies a small **accessory process** (*processus accessorius*) — a remnant of the true transverse process. Lateral to the superior articular process is the **mammillary process** (*processus mamillaris*).
s0227 The spinous processes are short, flat, broad, thickened at the tip and directed **horizontally** backward — so the spaces between them are wide. The articular processes are well developed, and their articular surfaces lie in the **sagittal** plane: the superior ones face medially and the inferior ones laterally; this allows flexion and extension in the lumbar region but limits rotation.
